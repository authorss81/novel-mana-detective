# The Mana Detective — World Bible

**Status:** Bootstrap canon. Veyra should feel like a functioning modern city first and a magical world second. The extraordinary must arrive through ordinary work: a clinic that cannot close, a landlord who wants a ward removed, a family that cannot remember a funeral, a police form that changes who receives compensation.

## Veyra at a glance

Veyra is a city-state of roughly 2.6 million people at the mouth of the River Orra. It is a dense delta city built on salt marsh, reclaimed land, and layers of old infrastructure. Electric trains cross on concrete viaducts; night buses run under sodium lamps; phones, cameras, card payments, and cloud records are ordinary. The city has a modern legal system and a second, public system for dangerous magical work.

Veyra is not a fantasy kingdom wearing a modern coat. Its institutions, inequalities, and habits are contemporary. Magic is useful, regulated, expensive, and unevenly distributed. A licensed ward can keep a public stairwell safe for years. An unlicensed healing working may cost less than a clinic visit. A person can be prosecuted for an illegal working while the public treats the injury as an accident.

The city is governed by the Veyra Civic Council, courts, the Civic Police, and a separate civic-magic authority called the **Civic Concord**. The Concord's public mandate is broad: register practitioners, inspect dangerous workings, maintain the **Quiet Grid** that stabilizes the city's ambient charge, and certify evidence in magical cases. The grid is publicly owned but paid for through municipal levies, insurer assessments, and private risk models; in practice, an insurer such as Aegis Mutual can make a district effectively uninsurable. The Quiet Hands turn that leverage into political control, while the final reform moves the grid's core funding back into public accounts.

The official slogan is **“No consequence without a witness.”** Most residents hear it as a promise that dangerous magic will be recorded and answered for. The novel gradually shows that a witness can be trained, selected, or made to disagree with another witness.

### Veyra Civic Council

The Council is the elected municipal government: twelve members who set the city budget, approve ward projects, ratify licensing budgets, and can vote on a Safety Charter. It does not itself cast workings or certify evidence. Its members are ordinary political actors, and the Lantern Compact can influence them through contracts, insurance, and public fear. Councilor Dax Merrow is a current member and later uses his position to push the Safety Charter. After the final reform, the Council remains but must publish contracts, budgets, and emergency orders; it no longer appoints a single magical authority.

## Physical regions

### Morrow Hill

The administrative and financial center. Old stone offices sit beside newer glass towers, with Concord Hall, the Proof Tribunal, the Veyra Civic Police headquarters, and the sealed access to the Veyran Vault beneath the hill. Wealth makes it easier to obtain a licensed working and harder to admit that one failed.

### Kettle Rows

Dense mixed-income neighborhoods of rented rooms, repair shops, food markets, clinics, and late-shift workers. It is where Elias grew up and where several unlicensed care networks operate. The neighborhoods are politically fragmented, not uniformly heroic: residents can protect one another, exploit one another, or simply want a working elevator repaired.

### Saint Orra Reach

The hospital and medical campus on the western rise. Saint Orra General treats injuries that conventional medicine cannot stabilize, including magical residue burns. Mara Quill works in a small independent residue laboratory attached to the public hospital. The campus is politically respectable and privately dependent on Concord certification, so it becomes a recurring place where care and evidence compete.

### Saltmarket

The port, wholesale markets, cold storage, ferry terminal, and low-cost housing surrounding them. Magical components travel through Saltmarket under ordinary customs forms. Smugglers, dockworkers, insurers, and small clinics all know which seals can be altered without attracting a full inspection.

### The Cut

Old drainage channels, maintenance tunnels, abandoned rail cuts, and service vaults beneath the city. The Cut is not one dungeon. It is a legal and physical maze owned in fragments by the transport authority, the water office, private contractors, and people who learned to live in the gaps. Unfinished workings accumulate there because no single authority claims the space.

### The outer wards and river corridor

Outside the core city are ring roads, clinics, small towns, flood defenses, and the ward farms that keep the delta stable. The river corridor connects Veyra to Ansel Quay and the smaller river cities. These places matter when the Quiet Grid's failures begin crossing jurisdictional borders. The regional **Ninefold Assembly** represents the corridor through nine city and county seats; it predates the Quiet Hands and is not the same as the nine institutional anchors revealed later. Its members disagree about whether Veyra's evidence standards should become regional law.

## Everyday magical life

Most residents do not cast spells casually. They use licensed services and familiar protections:

- A stairwell has a small ward that prevents falls, not theft.
- A clinic uses a short working to keep a patient's body from rejecting a transplant.
- A transit ticket contains a limited one-way binding that identifies a person, not a destiny.
- A house may have a door ward that refuses entry to a person who has declared violence, but the refusal can be challenged if the declaration was coerced.
- A grocery store may keep a cool-room working that fails in a heat wave and requires a physical backup generator.
- A funeral home may preserve a last message, but preservation is not the same as truth.

The city does not treat magic as a free supernatural convenience. A simple repair may take minutes and a licensed fee. A memory or identity working is restricted because it can alter consent, inheritance, and testimony. A large working can black out a district, redirect a ward, or cause everyone nearby to remember the same version of an event.

### Charge and consequence

The public word **mana** names the ambient capacity for change. It gathers unevenly in water, metal, living tissue, and old structures. A working draws on that local charge and changes something. It is not an unlimited fuel tank and it is not a visible blue glow. When a current is overworked, it becomes **drifty** for a time: ward lights flicker, records misfile, instruments disagree, and people can lose the emotional force of a recent memory. Currents recover slowly from the river, sea, weather, and living ground. A spectacular working therefore has an environmental and social cost even when no one is visibly injured.

A working can affect an unwilling target, but it cannot reliably create a belief that the target never had. It can create fear, paralysis, altered sensation, or a false sense of certainty. The most dangerous memory work joins a living person to a rehearsed sequence and tries to make the rehearsal feel like an event that happened.

## History

### Before the Blackline Event

Magic was taught through families, guilds, clinics, and informal apprenticeships. Techniques were local and poorly documented. A licensed profession did not yet exist. The city had several overlapping ward networks, and every authority claimed the right to keep them running.

### The Blackline Event — thirty-eight years before the opening

A mass stabilization working in the Veyran Vault failed. A line of blackened ward stone ran beneath the city, and a cascade of broken bindings damaged fourteen blocks. People died, buildings changed their locks, and the witnesses' accounts contradicted the physical record. The city called the event the **Blackline Event**; residents also called it the night the record broke.

The first response was to build a stronger grid and a system for certifying dangerous workings. Architects, judges, doctors, police, and ward engineers created the Civic Concord. Their first agreement was a stabilization working built from trained witnesses and public anchors. The agreement did not merely observe events. It aligned the witnesses' inner traces with the residue of the Quiet Grid so the grid could continue to carry load.

The stabilization succeeded, but it also made a new kind of evidence possible. If a witness and a record agreed, the agreement could be certified. If they disagreed, the weave could add a **correction** so the grid would not experience two incompatible versions of the same danger. The first correction was an emergency measure: it supplied a fabricated release after an anchor, address, and turn, but left its central completion unfinished. It was never meant to become a private method of deciding who was right.

### The Quiet Hands

Years later, a group inside and around the Concord argued that unrestricted truth was itself a danger. Led by Iona Saye, a former Concord director, they formed the **Quiet Hands**. They protect the city by removing dangerous contradictions: suppressing a memory, replacing a sequence certificate, steering a dangerous person away from a district, or making a crime appear to have ended differently.

Some Quiet Hands members are sincere protectors. Others profit from the arrangement. The group is not a uniform legion; it is a network of people who can use the proof weave to protect a ward, a patient, a business, a family, or themselves.

### The present

The Quiet Grid is failing in small, uneven ways. Districts lose ward protection for minutes. A public clinic cannot secure a license before a treatment is needed. Insurers demand a certified sequence before paying for magical injury. The Quiet Hands use fresh crimes and copied workings to provide the weave with new anchors while hiding the correction that explains the old case. A scheduled **Ward Reauthorization Review** is due when the central grid must be certified for another decade of operation. This is the first review after two consecutive seasons of ward failures; earlier decade certifications were extended administratively while the Quiet Hands controlled the records. They intend to use this review to complete the unfinished central release.

## Factions and institutions

### Veyra Civic Police

The ordinary modern police force, with a Serious Crimes Unit and a small Sequence Desk. Police officers use phones, cameras, interviews, physical forensics, and legal procedure. Most officers are not magical specialists. The Sequence Desk is trusted because it produces readable certificates, not because every officer understands how magic works.

The police are capable and compromised. Some officers protect vulnerable residents; some obey orders that hide inconvenient evidence. The department cannot be dismissed as a villain, because ordinary policing and care are what make the city survivable.

### Civic Concord

A public authority with four major branches: **Licensing**, **Ward Works**, **Clinical Practice**, and the **Proof Registry**. It trains most witnesses, maintains the Quiet Grid, and certifies evidence for the Proof Tribunal. Its employees range from sincere inspectors to comfortable functionaries who have learned to protect the institution's reputation.

The Concord does not have a single public leader who controls every decision. That is why the proof weave can continue even when individuals defect. The institutional antagonist is the system of centralized certification, with Iona Saye as its most consequential hidden architect.

### Proof Tribunal

A specialist court for unlicensed workings, magical injury, ward failure, and crimes whose sequence cannot be accepted by ordinary courts. The Tribunal can compel testimony, seize dangerous materials, and issue a correction order. Its decisions are binding, but its procedures are deliberately opaque. Magistrate Halden Ro is its cautious public face in the early volumes and a defender of precedent later.

### Lantern Compact

A public-facing coalition of insurers, private clinics, guilds, infrastructure contractors, development firms, and councilors who depend on predictable magical risk. It is not secret, but its influence is easier to see when a working can be insured, licensed, or delayed. Selene Rook represents its most cynical commercial face. Councilor Dax Merrow represents its political face.

### Kettle Rows mutual-aid networks

Neighborhood clinics, tenant groups, night-market organizers, repair workers, and harm-reduction practitioners who share resources and witness duties without official licenses. They are not uniformly anti-Concord. Many residents use them because the legal system has failed them first. Their records are often conflicting, but the conflicts can reveal a sequence that a polished certificate has removed.

### Quiet Hands

A clandestine network, not a single gang. Its members include some Concord staff, police supervisors, clinicians, judges, contractors, and former witnesses. Their shared principle is that a stable public reality is worth a limited amount of deliberate forgetting. The group uses copywork, false rehearsal, correction, and selective disappearance rather than a conventional army of monsters.

## Crime patterns and social consequences

The first crimes are not random spells. A copywork crime usually requires:

1. a living source or a strong personal object as an anchor;
2. a trained or willing working frame;
3. a living target whose memory can be aligned with the copied sequence;
4. a practical reason for the attacker to need a safe official account.

This makes the crimes rare, intimate, and financially possible. A criminal can copy a healing practice, a security ward, a door opening, or a protective charm. The copy is usually technically competent and emotionally incomplete. It tends to fail around ordinary details that were never part of the victim's magic: a habit, a delayed response, a person the victim would not willingly address.

The most common social consequence is not a body alone. It is a dispute over a lease, a custody order, a compensation claim, a work injury, or a family memory. Magical evidence can decide who receives care or believes a child is theirs. The Quiet Hands prefer crises that can be closed with a certificate before the public has to live with uncertainty.

## World-building guardrails

- The city remains recognizably modern: phones, buses, clinics, offices, and paperwork continue to work most of the time.
- Do not invent a new governing body to solve a local problem when an existing institution can plausibly respond.
- New magical techniques must state their anchor, target, consequence, and limitation.
- A district should have ordinary residents and practical routines, not only clue locations.
- The Quiet Grid is infrastructure, not an excuse for arbitrary disasters. Every failure has a location, a maintenance history, and people affected.
- The final conflict uses the established proof weave, Quiet Grid, Quiet Hands, and Iona Saye. Do not add a late cosmic enemy or a previously unseen master wizard.
