# Chapter 0075 — The Heavy Impression

The wire rack behind the dispensary counter had about forty envelopes on it at ten past nine on Friday morning and eleven of them had a paper clip bent double, and the eleventh had been there long enough for the paper to have gone the colour of weak tea.

"You told me on Monday that a certain time is three weeks and a certainer time is eleven weeks," said Elias Venn.

"And I have."

"That one has been bent double for about four days and I do not think it is going to make eleven weeks, and the name on the envelope is a woman of sixty-one and she was in this building on Friday and she has not been in this building since."

Doreen Tillery did not look at the rack, which is a thing dispensary clerks do not do.

"She was in on Friday," she said. "She comes on Fridays. She has come on Fridays for about four years and she has not been in since Friday, and that is not the middle of a month when you expect a holiday, and no, I am not worried, and I am not telephoning anybody at ten past nine on a Friday about a woman whose repeats are late, because I have done that four times in fourteen years and three of them were a woman who had gone to her sister's. And I have been counting down that rack since half past nine this morning like a woman looking for a needle, and I have just worked out that I have been looking at the wrong envelope, and you may tell her that I said so, and she will not care."

"What is her name."

"That is not a general question," said Doreen Tillery, and then she stopped, because she had told him on Monday that the fourth question would be the last thing in that room that day, and it was a different day, and she was the one who had put that in.

"Una Ferrers," she said. "Sixty-one. She is on a repeat of something that I do not say in a dispensary, four years, and she lives with somebody who is not her husband and it is not my business, and she has never once asked me for anything and she has never once been late before Friday."

---

She telephoned the shop at ten to seven on Thursday evening, which was after the second of the two days the arc had, and she had said, *There is a form. There is a form in this building that came from outside this building, and I have had it for three weeks, and I have been looking at it with my glasses on in the back room on my own, and I would like somebody else to look at it who is allowed to say what it is.*

He had said he would come at half past four on the Friday.

He came in at ten past four, because he had been in the shop from half past eight with nothing to do and Otto Vahl had gone up at two, and because the walk from Kettle Rows to Marshall Street is nine minutes and he had done it forty times since November and he could not make himself do it any slower than nine minutes.

Una Ferrers was at the counter with her handbag on her knee and a bad coat and a form in it, and she had it out on the counter before he was through the door, because she had been bringing it for three weeks and it was the reason she was there.

"They have not done it," she said. "That is four Fridays. I have brought it every Friday and they have not done it, and I am not saying anything about the lady, she is very kind, I am saying it about the form."

"What is she saying, Doreen."

"I am saying the form is not ours, Mrs Ferrers, and I have said it every Friday, and I have said it kindly." Doreen Tillery had her glasses in her hand and did not put them on. "And I have said, every Friday, that I will ask somebody, and I have not asked anybody, because there is nobody in this building I can ask, and because I did not want to put it in front of a doctor who will ask me what the form is where the patient can hear me."

"Then I am asking," said Elias Venn.

"You are a member of the public with a sheet on my wall."

"Ma'am, I am asking."

---

They went into the back room because the light at the front counter is bad and the light in the back room is worse and Doreen Tillery has a lamp on a bracket that she brings out for the difficult reading, and because a woman of sixty-one should not stand at a counter for twenty minutes while three people look at a piece of paper.

The back room is nine feet by seven and there is a table, two chairs, a sink, a shelf of boxes and a door that shuts.

Una Ferrers sat down because she was asked to and put her handbag on the table beside her and did not open it. The form came out of it and went on the table and lay flat, and it was A5, folded once, and it had been folded and unfolded a good many times because the folds had gone soft.

There was a head on it. Not the clinic's head.

"There," said Doreen Tillery. "That is the thing. Three weeks. It came out of a bag. She had it in her bag with her shopping and she did not know and she brought it to me because I have done her repeats for four years and she asked me what it was, and I told her it was a form and she said whose and I said I did not know, and she said will you do my repeats, and I said not on that, and she said why not, and I have had no answer to why not for three weeks."

Elias Venn looked at it without touching it for about a minute and a half, and then he said, "May I have it."

"You may have it if you put it back the way you found it, and I will hold it while you do it, because I have watched men in this city take things off counters."

He put two fingers on the corner of it, on the fold, and only on the fold, and the room went up out of the floor the way it always did.

---

There was an address on the paper and it was a room and a bed and a date, and a room and a bed and a date is an address the way a number is a name, and it pointed at nothing anybody in this building had ever seen.

There was a turn, and it was done, and it was done properly, by somebody who knew the method, and it ran on the paper in a hand that leaned right and pressed hard, and it was about nine-tenths of the way through when the hand stopped.

And the order **stopped.**

It did not fade and it did not get thin. There was a mark where it stopped, and the mark was the *beginning* of something and not the middle of it, and the whole of the order stood there with a ninth of itself in front of it and nothing behind it, and in the gap there was a mark of a thing starting.

And then it ran on again, from about a tenth of the way in, on the other side, and it went to the end, and the end of it was a release, and the release was seated, and it was waiting for a hand with a pen in it to put a signature on a line on a table.

Elias Venn came off the form with his back against the shelf of boxes and the left side of his head gone the way it goes, and said it out loud, in order, in the flat voice of a man saying numbers aloud so that somebody else does the arithmetic.

> **MARGIN — The order runs and it does not pause. Where two marks of one figure stand on this paper, one is pressed and one is barely there, and both are in the same hand, and the order does not know the difference between them and neither do I.**

"A room and a bed and a date," he said. "A turn, done properly, by somebody who knows the method. **A release, seated, waiting for a signature.** And in the middle of it a mark of a thing that began and stopped, which is a false start, and I am the only person in this room who can see one, and I have said that out loud, and I am saying it out loud because a woman of sixty-one is sitting in this room with her handbag on the table and she is going to be affected by what is on this paper more than anybody in it."

He did not look at Una Ferrers and he said so, out loud, so that there was a record of it.

"I have not touched her and I have not been in the same material as her for one second, and I am not going to, and the same sentence I said in a corridor on Monday in front of four people, and the reason I am in this room is that I am not a clinician and I am not a police officer and I have been formally reprimanded for touching a thing I was not entitled to touch, and that is the only reason I am allowed to be here."

"You have got your hand on a piece of paper in my dispensary," said Doreen Tillery, "which I gave you."

"Yes."

"Then stop being dramatic about it and tell me what you have seen, because you have said four words in the last ten seconds and Mrs Ferrers is going to think they are about her."

"He has seen an order," said Elias Venn. "He has not seen who. He cannot see who, that is the first thing I say about everything I have ever seen, and I have said it in six rooms in this city since September and nobody has written it down. What he has seen is that this piece of paper is not a piece of paper, and that there is a release sitting on the end of it waiting for somebody to sign a line, and that **a release that is not committed is not a release**, and I told a woman in a room in Kettle Rows that in November and the woman in the room with me in November heard me say it."

"And the false start," said Doreen Tillery.

"And the false start," said Elias Venn, "which is a mark of a thing that began and was stopped and left its first mark in the material, which is a thing neither I nor a woman in a laboratory can do anything about, and which I have said out loud in a room twice in my life and the first time I said it wrong and the second time I am saying it now."

He stopped.

Nobody in the room said anything for about four seconds.

Then Doreen Tillery said, in a completely ordinary voice, "Which figure."

"The fee," said Elias Venn. "The fourth box. The fee is written twice, and the first one is pressed and the second one is barely there, and the two of them are four minutes apart, and the hand is the same hand."

He put the form down, the way it had been, with the fold where it had been, and Doreen Tillery put her hand flat over it without looking at it.

"You are going to tell me what that means," she said, "and I am going to tell you now, because I have been staring at it for three weeks and I could not make it come out right, and because I would rather it came from a man who has been wrong in public before than from me. **There are two figures on that line because I wrote it twice.**"

Doreen Tillery took her glasses off and put them in her pocket.

"No, I did not write it. I have never seen that line before Thursday and I wrote nothing on it and there is nothing of mine on that paper." She looked at the form under her hand. "But I have done that, and every person at that counter has done that, and if I have written a figure down and thought better of it and written it again then the second one is on top of the first one and you can see both, and there is no mystery in that and there is nothing in that. It is Wednesday and a woman comes in and you write four hundred and you look at it and you write two-ninety, and the two-ninety is faint because you have not gone over it twice. That is what a heavy one and a light one is."

"Elias," said a voice from the doorway.

Sana Okafor was in the doorway of the back room of her own dispensary and had been there, by the look of the two cups on the shelf, for about ninety seconds.

He had agreed on Thursday that he would tell her before he said a thing about a document out loud in this building, and she was entitled to stand in the doorway, and she was standing in the doorway, and he had already said the false start, and he had about four minutes left of the minute.

"What have I missed," said Sana Okafor.

---

"The order of it," said Elias Venn. "I have said it in order. There is a release seated on the end of that form and it is waiting for a signature and I have told the woman at the counter and I have told the woman in the room and I am telling you now, and I have not put a hand on a patient or on a person at any point in this room and I said so out loud in front of four people and I am saying it again for you because you did not hear it.

"And I was wrong about the gap," he said. "I said a false start and it is not a false start. **It is a figure written twice. A woman wrote a line and did not like it and wrote it again, and the two impressions are a fact about pens, and I have had that written in my own book since the sixth of December in the words of a woman in a laboratory who told me it happens about four times a day in this city, and I have said it in a room anyway, in front of a patient, four minutes ago.**"

Nobody said anything.

"I have been wrong in five rooms in this city since September and I have never once been wrong in a room with a person in it who is going to be affected by it," said Elias Venn. "And I want to say the other thing before anybody in this room starts deciding that I am the man who sees things in pens, because the other thing is still true and it is worse than the thing I got wrong, and I do not know what to do with it, and I cannot stop it and I have said in three buildings that I cannot stop it."

"There is a release on that form," said Sana Okafor.

"There is a release on that form and nobody has signed it and it is not committed, and it cannot be committed by a person who is not the person doing the work, and that is why we are still standing in a room instead of a corridor." He put his hands flat on the table, well away from the paper. "And I am not going to touch it and I am not going to read it again this afternoon, and I would like that written down as a decision and not as a courtesy."

"Then I am going to ask you the question I have been holding since Thursday night," said Sana Okafor, "and you are going to answer it, and you are not going to like it. Four days ago you came into this building and you told me in a corridor that a set of eleven forms in a cupboard in my dispensary is half of a claim. This afternoon there has been a form on my table with a release on the end of it that has not been signed, and you have just told me that there is a person in a first-floor room over a dry cleaner four streets away who would want it, and you have told me that in front of a patient and a member of my staff.

"So which is it. Are you here about a cupboard, or are you here about a woman with a bag."

"I do not know," said Elias Venn.

"That is the first true thing you have said since the eighth of October," said Sana Okafor, "and I have been waiting about ten weeks for one of them, and it has arrived in my back room on a Friday afternoon and it has cost me something, and I am not going to ask you which."

---

Una Ferrers stood up at four minutes past four and put her handbag over her forearm and did not pick up the form.

"Can I take it home, then," she said.

Doreen Tillery did not answer. She was looking at Sana Okafor, and Sana Okafor was looking at the form, and neither of them looked at Elias Venn, and it had been put to all three of them at once by a woman of sixty-one who had brought the same piece of paper to that counter four Fridays running and had not once been told anything at all.

"Yes," said Elias Venn. "Take it home. Nobody is going to sign it, and there is nothing in it that is yours, and if anybody gives you a pen for it, anywhere, in any building, in any street, you are to come back here and ask for Doreen by name, and you are to put her name in the front of your book with the day on it, and I am not going to tell you why and neither is she."

"Then I shall put it in," said Una Ferrers, "and I shall come on Friday, and I have told you that three times and you have all been very kind about it, and it is a form and it is mine and I have carried it about for three weeks and I would like somebody to tell me what it is for."

And Doreen Tillery, who had not filled it in, and was not going to, and had a woman of sixty-one standing in her dispensary on a Friday afternoon with a piece of paper in her bag that somebody in a first-floor room over a dry cleaner would have wanted very much, picked up the lamp and put it back on its bracket and said nothing at all.
