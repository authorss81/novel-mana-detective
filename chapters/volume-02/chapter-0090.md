# Chapter 0090 — The Day Before

The linen office at Saint Orra Reach has a corridor behind it and a fire door at one end and a window at the other that looks at a wall, and at twenty to ten on Sunday morning, the thirteenth of January, Elias Venn let himself in with a key he had been given and then had taken back and then had been left on a bench by a sink, and Mara Quill was in the room with the blind up and the fire off and a plate of toast on a mug that she had not eaten.

"The gas is not on my account."

"The gas is on nobody's account," he said, and put the kettle on, and then put his hand flat on the bench to lean on, and then took it off the bench and put it on the sink and turned the tap a quarter of a turn, and the tap was the next act after the bench, and it was the same tap and the same quarter of a turn, and he did it without meaning to and he knew he was doing it while he was doing it, which is the part that is new.

"Sit down and tell me what you want to say and then I shall say it and then you can disagree with it," said Mara Quill. "I have got until two and there is a woman coming at two with a plate."

"Two sentences and then I am going to say them wrong and you are going to stop me."

"Go on."

"Nobody lied to him. Not one person, in any building, on any day, told that brother of mine a false thing about his own hour, and the signature at the foot of the six inches by two is his, and the number written across the top of it is a real number in somebody else's hand, and the sheet it came off is a true document, and the room was a real room, and the woman who has run that room for six years charges eleven credits and has never been in this case and is not going to be, and the man four doors from her who has used it nine times is competent and careful and told me the truth twice in four days and is not going to be charged with anything either." He put his hand flat on the bench again and took it off. "And a true signature on an incomplete sentence is worse than a lie, because a lie can be caught and this cannot, and it has been running in this city for six years and it is not going to stop because we found a strip about the size of a ruler."

"No," said Mara Quill. "It is not. And I want to be very careful here, because this is the part of the job I have been doing since I was nineteen and I have never once got it wrong, and I am going to say it as a practitioner and not as anything else, and if I say it as anything else you may take me upstairs and I shall not complain."

"Go on."

"You are going to have to write a finding, and there is no finding in that sentence, and the reason there is no finding is that the sentence is true. A lie is a document that says one thing and is another thing. **This is a document that says exactly what it says and the thing it says has a hole in the shape of a purpose, and you cannot put a hole in a document in a room, and you cannot charge a man for a hole.**" She put the mug down. "There is no name in this. There is no office. There is a man of thirty-nine four doors from the woman who ran that room, and he wrote the word *consent* nine times on a line that is not a box because a patient handed him a strip with the word on it, and nobody has ever asked him a question of any kind, and there is a woman in the room at the other end of that landing who works Fridays and has a green book of about four hundred sessions in it and no names in it at all, and there is a clerk of nineteen years on a tray who has never been asked what an examiner is for, and there is a woman at a forms counter who gave it away for nothing on Tuesday. **Nobody is the villain of this. There is a shape and the shape has four corners and every one of the four corners is somebody doing their job.**"

---

"The five lines," said Elias Venn.

"The five lines are a practice and they are not a document and they were never going to be a document, and I said in November that they were no use for a person, and you did not hear me, and I have not resented it for one hour." She took the notebook out of her coat pocket, where it has been since November, and she did not open it, and she held it against her chest. "We are going to say them. Out loud. Once each. And we are not going to write them, and I want the not-writing to be your decision and not mine, because I set that in November and I am not having the same conversation twice."

"Agreed."

"Line one. **Whose person it is, and who has said so in their own words, with the date.** Not *whose object*. A person. And a person who is in a room is not a thing with an owner, and if anybody in this city ever again says *the patient* to my face in a document they do not own, I want them to be in a room with me."

"Whose person it is," said Elias Venn, "and who has said so, and when. That one has never once been wrong and I have never once been able to do anything with it, and I have wanted to say it out loud in a room since about half past seven this morning."

"It has never been wrong," said Mara Quill, "and it is not going to be wrong, and it is also not going to be enough. Say the next one and do not put a face on it."

"Line two. **What is being done to them, in one sentence they could say back to somebody who is not them.**" She did not look at him. "That one is yours and it has been yours since November and I am not touching it."

"In a sentence they could say back to somebody who is not them," he said. "That is the whole of it and I have had it since the eighteenth of September and I have never once had to use it, because the three times I have nearly used it I have been in a room on my own."

"Three times," said Mara Quill, "and one of them was on Friday afternoon in a corridor in Corrie Street with a door and nobody at the other end of it, and we are not going to do that one again."

"Line three is the corrected one and it stays unpublished, and you are not going to ask me again, and if you ask me again I shall say the thing I said on Thursday and you are not going to enjoy it twice."

"I am not going to ask you," he said. "I have decided that about forty times and I have got it down to a thing I do not do any more, and I would like that noted, because I know what it looks like from your side of the bench."

"Line four. **What has been measured before any hand goes on a person, and by whom, in whose log, under whose number.**" She turned the notebook over in her hands without opening it. "That is the one that has cost him. Four seconds, on a Thursday in October six years ago, on the back of a form, in a hand that leans right and presses hard, and the number written over the top of it is a claim, and the claim was amended after he had gone home and nobody told him, and the log is somebody's drawer two miles from here. **You cannot measure a person into a register and then act as though the register is not the whole of the matter, and the register was never the whole of the matter, and there was never going to be a box for it.**"

"That is the sentence of the month."

"It is on a piece of paper in three places and on no wall, and I have not given it to anybody, and I am not going to. The number is on a fourth piece of paper in a drawer, and it is a claim and not a certificate, and the hand on it is not a name and I have never asked anybody for a specimen and neither are you going to."

"And five."

"**Who consents, in writing, and what they were told,** and then the whole of the sentence, and the whole of the sentence is the one that has been missing since I was nineteen. *And what it is going to be used for.*"

"And what it is going to be used for," said Elias Venn. "I have been four months with a man who puts his hand flat on things, and I have read about nine hundred sheets of paper in three buildings in this city, and there is not one page in any of them with those four words on it, and the four words are the four words on the bottom of a form that is about six inches long and it is the only page in this city that has a man's name on it and it is a receipt."

She said it and then she shut her mouth and Elias Venn did not write it down, and the two of them stood in a room with four instruments and two plates in a padlocked case and a plate in a slot on a shelf and a foil stencil on a plate on a lower shelf that had not moved since October, and about ninety feet of warm water going under a hospital's linen office, and neither of them wrote anything.

"That is the practice," said Mara Quill. "It is five sentences and it lives in two heads and it is about people, and it is not a document, and it is not going to be, and the reason it is not going to be is that the day it becomes a document it will be a form, and a form will get a box, and the box will be unfilled the way the first box on a continuity request in a clinic on Marshall Street has been unfilled for eleven years."

"I know."

"You do not know, you have guessed, and guessing is a thing you do at a counter in Fenwick Street on a Tuesday." She put the notebook back in her pocket. "Now go and see the doctor, because she has been sitting on a date for seven weeks and there is a woman in her waiting room on a Sunday who comes every Sunday and reads the whole of the fan, and that woman is going to ask her a question and the doctor has not got an answer."

She had her hands flat on the bench on either side of a plate of toast she had not eaten, and she was looking at the door rather than at him.

"Elias. I have not said his name in this room and I am not going to start today, and it is not because of the rule we made in November."

"I know."

"He is not found." She said it the way she said the number on a box on Monday. "That is the sentence and it is the only one I am permitted to use and I use it twice a day, and on the days I use it I get up afterwards and I do the four instruments, and it is not a good way to spend an afternoon and there is not a second one available."

---

There were nine people in the waiting room of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic on Marshall Street at a quarter past three on a Sunday afternoon, which was two more than a Sunday, and one of the nine was a woman of about seventy-two who comes every Sunday and reads the whole of the fan.

She was at the board when he came in and she had her hand up about a foot from the polythene, not touching it, the way a person stands in front of a thing in a museum.

"It is going to stay up," said Sana Okafor, from behind the counter, without being asked anything.

"That is not the question I was going to ask." The woman took her hand down. "The question I was going to ask is whether it could come down, and if it could, who takes it down, and you are the one who takes things down, and I have read that fan for four years and I have never once known who holds it, and that has been the whole of the trouble with it."

"It could come down. I hold it. I am not going to take it down."

"Then I would like to be able to tell my neighbour that," said the woman, "because she is seventy-nine and she has been in twice this winter and she asked me on Friday who puts things up on that wall and I had to say *somebody from the clinic*, which is the most useless sentence in the English language, and it has been in her head since Friday and she will ask me again this evening and I shall have to say the same useless sentence unless you tell me something I can say."

"You can say that the person who holds it is the director of this clinic and that her name is on the bottom of the four sheets underneath it, and that when the sheet at the top went up in November she wrote three lines under the heading and her registration number went under his, and that she is the one who can take it down." Sana Okafor did not look up from the counter. "And you can say that she is not going to, on a Sunday in January, and that if she ever does then there will be a date on the wall next to the rectangle and her name on it, and that you can come and read it, because I am telling you now and I am telling you in a room with nine people in it, which is the only way anything gets said in this building."

The woman looked at the board for a while.

"Four years," she said, "and the first three of them I could not tell anybody whose it was either, and I never asked, and now I can tell a stranger, and that is the whole of what I came for on a Sunday." She picked her bag up off the seat. "I have a hip and I stand here for about ten minutes and I read the whole fan, and I would like somebody in this building to know that I read the whole fan, because when it went quiet in December I could have said nothing and I did not, and nobody in this room ever said thank you to me for saying it, and I would like that said."

"It is said," said Sana Okafor, "and I am not going to make a thing of it, and I have been in this room for nineteen years and you are the third person who has said something to me about that wall that I have not been able to answer, and I have written none of them down, which is probably the most unprofessional thing about me."

"You have written this one down," said the woman, who could see the pad on the counter. "You have written something on that pad while I was talking."

"That is a different thing," said Sana Okafor.

Sana Okafor had the board in front of her with three kinds of paper on it and a clean rectangle about nine inches by eleven that is a paler colour than the rest of the board and two pin holes in it, and the four drawing pins at the top were his own and had been in that sheet since the twenty-seventh of November, and there was air round it on all four sides.

"I went to take one of them down," she said.

Elias Venn looked at her.

"Which one."

"The registrar's. It has been on that wall since the twenty-first of September in a clear sleeve with a bent corner and it is nobody's business and it has never once been anybody's business, and in December I took ninety-odd pages down and put them in a box in my office and the woman of seventy-two with the hip stood in this room and would not have them back, and I did not give her an answer then, and I have been thinking about that rectangle for three weeks." She had a pair of drawing pins in her hand and she had not got them near the board. "On Friday I got the pins out of the top corners of the clear sleeve."

"And?"

"And I put them back in." She put the two pins down on the counter. "**I have been in this building nineteen years and I cannot remember whose document that is.** I know it is a registrar's transfer order and I know it came in through the post in a brown envelope in September and I know it was about somebody, and I do not know who, and I do not know which week, and I could not tell anybody whose it was if a man came in here tomorrow and asked me. And a woman who cannot remember whose document is on her wall does not get to take documents off her wall, and that is the whole of the reason, and it took me three weeks and two pins to say it."

"That is not the reason anybody would give."

"That is the reason that is true, and the other reason is that the board is the only thing in this district that anybody can stand in front of, and a board with one kind of paper on it is a wall, and I am not having a wall." She picked the two pins up again. "So it carries three kinds of paper, and it will carry three kinds of paper tomorrow, and I have lost one kind in December and I am not losing another one in the last week of an order, and I will tell you what I am going to do instead, and then I am going to go and see a patient, and then you are going to leave."

"What are you going to do instead."

"I am going to let it run out," said Sana Okafor. "On Monday morning I am going to let a piece of paper in a municipal building say whatever it says about eleven unnamed premises, and I am not going to be in that building, and I am not going to have put anybody's name near it, and I have thought about it for five weeks and I have said that out loud in this building exactly twice and both times to a member of the public, and I would like it noticed that neither of the two times was for you."

---

The Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street shut at three on a Saturday and on a Sunday they are shut, and the forms and publications counter is behind a grille, and there is a clerk in the building on a Sunday because her hours are on a rota and the rota is a rota.

She was about fifty-five and she was not Nell Cadby, and she had a drawer open in the office behind the counter and a form in it and a pen on a string.

"There is no service on a Sunday."

"I am not asking for a service. I am a member of the public and I have never been in this office before, and I have not got a question, and I am going to ask you one general question and then I am going to go."

"That is a good way in and it usually ends badly." She did not close the drawer.

"What I am going to ask is whether anybody has telephoned about a form."

"Which form."

"The one in that drawer."

---

She looked at him for a moment and then she did not close the drawer and she did not take the form out either.

"It is a review," she said. "It goes to the applicant, and the applicant's name is on it, and there is a box in the bottom right-hand corner, and the word that goes in the box is either *renewed* or *lapsed*, and I am not going to tell you which one, because I have not looked, and because I am not the applicant and it is not my word and it is not my box." She put one finger on the edge of the drawer. "It is in here. It has been in here since Friday, because Friday is when the last one of those was filed, and the last one of those is on a different piece of paper in a different drawer, and they do not talk to each other."

"Has anybody telephoned."

"No."

"About this one or in general."

"About this one or in general, no, and there is a telephone on that desk and it has not rung, and it is not going to ring, and the reason it is not going to ring is that the person who could ring it is in her own building on a Sunday and she is not thinking about it, which is what I would be doing if I were her."

"Then I will go."

"You will go," said the clerk, "and I will put the pen back in the drawer, and the form in the drawer, and the drawer in the desk, and I will lock the desk, and I will go at half past five, and that is a Sunday and I am here on a Sunday because the rota says so and nobody has ever once rung me on a Sunday."

---

He put his hand flat on the door of the Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street at ten to six on the Sunday evening, the way a person does it, and then he stood there, and the door was shut and locked and the grille was across it, and the door did not open, and his hand came off it and went flat on it again about a second later, and he let it, and then he took it away and put it in his coat.

In the morning there will be a drawer with a form in it and a pen on a string and a box in the bottom right-hand corner, and the word that goes in that box is one of two and a clerk who is not going to pick the pen up because it is not her word and not her box. And after that there will be nothing in this city that puts a police officer through any of eleven doors, and he knows the address of a first floor where two of those doors are and has stood in the corridor of it, and there is not one thing he is allowed to do about either of them.

He walked down to the bus stop in the dark. The timetable in the case has a column for Sunday and the Sunday column is the only one in it that is not a column of times, and it says *No service before 09:30*, and it has said that since before the timetable was in a case, and he stood and read it twice and it did not tell him anything, and the 09:30 bus came at ten past six with nobody on it but the driver.
