# Chapter 0057 — Somebody Is Losing Money

The bus to the Verge Row side takes twenty-two minutes and he sat on the bottom deck with his hat on his knee and got out at the second stop past the tram sheds, because he had a rule he had made for himself on the Sunday, which was that he was going to walk the whole of a journey before he did anything else in a district he did not live in, and the reason for the rule was that he had once, in September, gone into a building in a hurry.

The district was a business park that had been a goods yard once. There were eight buildings and six of them had somebody's name over the door in letters about a foot high, and the one he wanted had no name over the door at all, which he had not expected and which told him more in one second than an hour of standing in a car park was going to.

He walked past it at ten past ten and went on to the end of the road and back, and then went in, because the rule had a limit and he had reached it.

The woman at the desk was about thirty-five and she was very good. She said the office was a claims administration office, that it was not a clinic and could not discuss anything with a member of the public, and that the number on his card — he had not given her a number and she had not asked for one — was a matter for the post.

"I have not got a number," said Elias Venn. "I have got an address and I have got the fact that a person on this street is the registered premises for something, and I have been told twice this week that a number is not a person, and I have come to see whether a person here will say that to my face."

The woman at the desk said, "The registered premises is not a person either, sir."

"Then I will not need anybody's face," said Elias Venn, "and you have just said in one sentence the thing a man on a third floor of a building in Kettle Rows has been working out for four years, and I am obliged to you, and I am going to go and stand in your car park for two hours and I am not going to bother you again."

"There is a public counter for enquiries inside on the left," said the woman at the desk, "and there are four people in front of you and they have been here longer than you have, and I would be dishonest if I said they get an answer."

---

He joined the queue because a man who has come to a door and been sent away has two choices and one of them is to go and stand in a car park in the rain and the other one is to find out whether the door is real.

There were four people in front of him and all four of them had come to the same counter for the same reason and none of them knew each other, and it took him an hour and ten minutes to find out what that reason was, and it took about a word each.

The first was a man of about sixty with a folder on his knees who had come about a claim that had been reduced by half on the grounds that part of a treatment had not been necessary, and the part that had been reduced was the part where a woman of eighty-two had been given something on a Tuesday to stop her losing the word for her own street, and she had kept the word, and she had a note from a hospital that said she had been given it.

"What do they want," said the woman at the counter. "They want the code. Every time. It is never the treatment, it is that the treatment has to be on a line that says who did it, and if it is not on a line that says who did it then it did not happen, and if it did not happen then it was not necessary, and if it was not necessary then it is not payable. **That is the whole of it. There is no dispute about whether she got it. There is a dispute about whether it happened.**"

The second was a young man with a phone in his hand and a reference on the screen who wanted a letter to say that a course of treatment had been completed, because a landlord wanted the letter, and the woman behind the counter had told him in October that the letter could not be written because the second half of the course had not been delivered, and he said, and it is the sentence Elias Venn wrote in the notebook that night with the time on it, "But the first half happened. I have paid for the first half. **They have got the treatment and they have not got me.**"

The third was seventy and had come about a certificate and was told that a certificate required a practitioner and a practitioner requires a registration, and that if the work had been done by somebody unregistered then the certificate cannot be issued and the person who did it cannot be named on it, and if it cannot be named on it then it has not happened, and if it has not happened then the money is not owed back, and the seventy-year-old man said, "Then what am I meant to do with a thing that happened to me," and the woman behind the counter said, "I am not permitted to advise you, sir," and the man said, "That is what I asked you," and went away.

The fourth was a woman with a plastic folder of photographs who was not there about any of that, and had come about a photograph, and was told that a photograph is not a document, and said that she had been told in July that a photograph was a document if it had a reference on it, and the woman behind the counter said, and this is the fourth time somebody had said a version of this sentence to Elias Venn in four days, "A reference is a number, madam, and a number is not a document and it is not a person and it is not a thing that happened to you."

At ten past ten he left the queue and went and stood in the car park under the awning of a building that had a letting agent's board in it, and got out his notebook, and did not write anything for about twenty minutes, which he had not planned.

Then he wrote it, in six lines, and the six lines took him fifty minutes and they are the only piece of work he did in November that he has never changed a word of since.

> *A rehabilitation claim number in this city is issued by a claims department. It is settled to a registered premises. It is settled to a postal address. It carries a service code. A claim number is a machine for turning a set of documents into a payment and a payment into a set of documents that say the same thing twice.*
> *The three counterfoils in Corrie Street are not claims. They are three settlements in the shape of claims, in a shape that would pass at a counter, made by somebody who has read enough claims to know the shape and not enough to know note 4.*
> *The shape is: an envelope, cash, forty credits, a session, no questions, and a number written across the top of the counterfoil afterwards.*
> *Note 4 has been in the tariff since the 1929 revision and the practice goes back to 1927, and the prohibition is two years old and the practice got older and then got tighter, and the person at the bottom of it was never told either fact, because telling her would have stopped it.*
> *The service code on the third one is procedure 2 at eleven credits, and the tariff for procedure 2 is sixty, and the difference is forty-nine credits, and the difference is not a person.*
> *There is one address in the world that could tell me whose premises is 22 Cutlass Row and I am not going to it and I am not going to ask.*

---

Nadia Kest was in the car park at a quarter to one, on foot, from the tram stop, and she had a paper cup in her hand and had clearly come out on purpose.

"Before you say anything," she said. "I have got a train to the outer wards in eleven minutes and I am not sitting in this car park. I'm going to say three things and you're going to hear all three and then I'm going."

She stood in the rain on the other side of the barrier.

"One. I know where you've been today, because the desk downstairs rang us at eleven and the woman was good enough to say the word member of the public before she rang anybody, and that is the correct procedure and I want it on the record that she did it right. Two. You were right in September to want to be a person in a room, and you were right in October to be a man on a bench, and you are now standing in a car park in the rain on a pavement that is not yours asking a woman with a good desk to break a rule, and you are not going to do it, and I have not come to tell you not to."

"I have not asked her for anything."

"You have not asked her for anything and you have stood there for two hours so that it could be said in a file that you did not ask her for anything," said Nadia Kest, "and I have read two hours of that in a car park on Cutter Street in October and I have read it again now, and it is going in a diary, because the next person who comes at you with a request is going to be better at this than a woman at a desk."

"And the third thing."

"The third thing is a thing I am not going to say." She turned the paper cup round in her hand. "There is a trading name in a police schedule in this city and it has a class and a premises and it is in a file held by a sergeant who reports to me, and there is a collection docket with the same premises and a depot and a time on it, and I have read both, and you have never read either, and I have not come out here to say it to you and I am not going to say it to you in a car park on a Wednesday.

"If I say it, then in about four years there is a member of the public in a room somewhere who has a name that a police officer told him in a car park, and there is no form anywhere in this city that accounts for how he got it, and the first person to say that out loud with any confidence at all will be the last person to have it. **You have spent four months building a book that is the only description of a working in existence. I have read it. I have been three pages into it twice and I have put it down twice.** So I know exactly what it is, and I know what the value of it is, and I am not going to be the woman who is holding it in eleven months when somebody comes and asks me where a member of the public got a name."

Elias Venn stood in the rain and did not say anything for long enough that a car came round the far side of the building.

"You have read my book."

"I have read the first three pages of a notebook that you keep on a shelf in a repair shop, which I have no authority over and have not asked anybody for authority over, and which I have read on two occasions when I was in that shop and you were not, and I am telling you that I have read it because I am about to refuse you something and you are entitled to know what I am refusing you against."

"That is fair."

"It is more than fair and you are not going to enjoy the rest of it." She looked at her watch. "There is a hearing on your appeal in about two and a half weeks. I want to say two things about that and I have never said either of them to you and I have had them since the third of November.

"One. Your written statement of reasons is good. It is the best thing in the file. It is better than my four hundred words on the twenty-second of September and I have told two people that and both of them were annoyed with me. Two. It is good, and it is about a prohibition and a witness and a breach note, and it does not say one word about the thing that actually happened, and when you stand in front of a panel in a room with three officers in it, the thing that is actually happening is a man of seventy-eight on oxygen in a corridor, and if you spend your eleven minutes on your own paperwork they will give you the finding in about nine and you will not have said the only thing you came to say."

"What am I supposed to say."

"I am not your advocate and I am not your counsel and I am an inspector who is about to lose a job over this in about four years, and I am telling you one sentence on a pavement, and here it is: **do not defend the paperwork. The paperwork is not the case. Tell them what you saw in the wood.**"

She put the paper cup in the bin by the barrier, which was not hers to use and which she used anyway.

"Say that again," said Elias Venn.

"No," said Nadia Kest. "I have said it once and I have said it to one person and I am not going to be the sort of officer who hands out a good line to everybody on a Wednesday. Write it down and make it sound like yours and if they ask me whether I said it I will deny it, and that is not a lie, that is how this building works, and you have been in it for twelve years and you know that."

She got on a bus at the tram stop and the rain did not stop, and he walked back to the stop and got on the same bus twenty minutes later and did not sit near her, and he wrote in the notebook on the bottom deck with the book open on his knee and it is the one passage in the whole of November that he has never shown to anybody, and it is four lines long, and the third line is the sentence, and he changed two words in it.

---

He got back to Marshall Street at ten past four, having walked the last mile of it on the wrong side of the ring road because the bus was late and he did not want to be on the pavement at a quarter to four in Marshall Street where four people knew his face, and went into the shop and Otto Vahl was at the bench with a radio that had been finished since Monday and had not been collected, and the till had twenty-two credits in it, and the certificate was on the shelf above the back bench with the protection order's receipt next to it, and he wrote out the six lines in a fair copy for a woman on Marshall Street and a fair copy for a first-floor flat in Corrie Street and he kept the third, and then he sat down on the stool with the blanket over the chairback and the cat in the drawer and did not do anything at all for about twenty minutes, which was the first twenty minutes he had had in five weeks that were not part of a job.

Then he got up and made the six lines into a single sheet, and he wrote at the top of it, in his own bad hand, the way he had written four sheets in a back room in October, a heading.

> *Six lines on three counterfoils and an address, and no person, and the reason there is no person on it is that a person on it would be a door to knock on and a name in an office to ring, and neither of those is anybody's business, and the reason the second half of that sentence is the reason the first half is true.*

Then he did the thing that made the evening cost what it cost, which was to send the fourth copy by post to a woman in Corrie Street who had not asked for it, in an envelope with his own name on the outside, and he put it in the box at the end of Marshall Street at twenty to five on Wednesday afternoon with four stamps on it because he did not have a small envelope, and then he stood at the postbox and worked out that he had just posted a document containing a claim number, a service code, a registered premises and a date of the eleventh of September 1929 to a first-floor flat above a dry cleaner, and that Rhoda Nesbit would open it in a kitchen and read the date, and that she would know what it was the same minute he did, and that he had chosen the street deliberately, and that the stamp on the envelope had a postmark with the time on it, and that was that.
