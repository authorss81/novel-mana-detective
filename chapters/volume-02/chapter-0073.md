# Chapter 0073 — What Came in the Post

The bins went out on a Wednesday, and that was the only reason the door of number twenty-two was open on the morning of the nineteenth of December, and Elias Venn had known it was a Wednesday and had timed himself to it, which was a thing he had decided about himself at four o'clock the previous afternoon and had not been proud of since.

Vilma Craddock came out with a bin bag in each hand and stopped in the yard with the bags hanging and looked at him for about four seconds.

"You are the one from the shop on Marshall Street," she said.

"That is not the shop. That is a clinic. The shop is at the end of it."

"It is the same street." She did not put the bags down. "You are the one who was in this yard on the twenty-seventh of November asking about the third floor."

"Yes."

"Six weeks." She shifted the bags. "I have been thinking about you for six weeks and I have told nobody that I have been thinking about you, and I would like that to stay exactly as it is, and I am telling you that first so that you know the shape of the rest of it."

"Then I will tell you the shape of my part," said Elias Venn, "and then you can shut the door. I am not a police officer and I am not a council man and I am not from the housing. I am a man with a sheet of paper on a wall that you cannot read and I am here because a street in Kettle Rows has got the address of your third floor written on it eleven times in a book, and I have been to that street and I have seen it, and I have decided that you are entitled to know that, and to nothing else."

Vilma Craddock put the two bags on the lid of the bin store and did not go in.

"You are not going to knock."

"I am going to knock once," said Elias Venn, "because I have not knocked at all since the twenty-seventh of November and a man who does not knock is a different animal from a man who knocks twice. After the once I do not knock again unless you open the door, and I am telling you that in the yard so that it is not a promise, it is a thing you can hold me to."

"Then knock," she said, "and I will not answer it, and then you can go and we will both have done what we came to do."

He knocked once on the door of number twenty-two, on the second joint up, which is where everybody knocks, and she did not answer it, and he stood in the yard afterwards and said, out loud, for the benefit of anybody at a window on the landing, that he was not coming back about the third floor.

There was a light on at the third window along on the first floor, which was number nineteen, and it went off about four seconds after he said it, and neither of them mentioned it.

"You have told me what you have and what you are not going to do," said Vilma Craddock, from the step, after a while. "You have not told me what you are going to do."

"I am going to go to a dispensary on Marshall Street on Monday and I am going to say a man's name to a woman who has fourteen years in that building, and I am going to be wrong, and I am going to be wrong in a building with nineteen people in it, and I have already promised her in writing that I would tell her first and I am not going to be able to keep that, and I have known that since Thursday afternoon and I have not been able to do anything about it since Thursday afternoon."

"That is not an answer," she said.

"No," said Elias Venn. "It is the sound a man makes on the way to an answer, and I have been making it since September, and you are the first person this month who has told me so."

"Then go and make it somewhere else, and shut my bin store up, because the lid is heavy and I am sixty-one and I have done this for eleven years and you are not helping."

---

"There is a bag," she said, at about twenty past twelve, from the step.

"I know there is a bag. You told me about the padlock in November. You did not tell me about a bag."

"There is a bag in the hall cupboard and there is post in the bag." She had a cardigan on over her cardigan and her arms were folded and she was talking to the yard rather than to him. "It came for the third floor. It came in the last year the third floor was let, which is to say it came about eleven years ago, and it came in three or four deliveries and a man came up the stairs and put it on the mat at number twenty-two because the bell on the third floor does not work and the name on it is not a person's name."

"What is the name on it."

"A number." She said it the way she had said the word *bag*. "A number and a dash and then a floor. I am not going to say the number."

"I did not ask you to say the number."

"You asked me what the name on it is, and the name on it is a number, and if I say the number then you have it." She unfolded her arms and folded them the other way. "It has been in that cupboard for eleven years. It has not been opened and it has not been thrown out and I have never put it on the fire, and I am aware that a woman who says she has never thrown it out has said something. It is not mine. If it had my name on it I would have opened it in the kitchen with a bread knife."

"You have not opened it."

"No."

"Will you hand it to me."

"No." She said it fast, which was the fastest anything she had said. "And you have no right and I have known you for six minutes and a bit and I can tell you exactly what you are, which is that you are the only person who has come to this door since the summer who has not wanted something out of it. The boy wanted to look. The other three wanted to be let in. You wanted to be let in as well, in November, and I would have said no then and I would have said something kind about it then, because in November I was sorry for you about the thing with the windows." She moved a half step towards the door. "I am not being unkind. I am being the opposite of unkind. If I hand you that bag then in about four years there is a piece of paper in a file with a number and a dash and a floor on it, and the number is not a man's name so nobody can be in trouble, and the paper is in a building where papers go, and the door on the third floor is the only thing in this street that anybody would have to go and look at. And I have been in this building eleven years and I would like to go on being in it."

"Then I will not take it," said Elias Venn, "and I am not going to ask you about it again, and I want to say why in the yard, so that it is out loud. It is because it is not mine, and because you are not a mailbox, and because if I take a bundle of post out of a hall cupboard on the word of a woman I have spoken to twice, then in a year somebody will say that a man with a reprimand takes other people's post, and that will be true, and there will be no way of it being not true."

Vilma Craddock looked at him for a long moment.

"Go on," she said.

"I have one piece of paper and it is my own and it is in my notebook and there is nothing on it that points at anybody, and it has four paragraphs in it and the fourth one is a sentence about a gap of nine seconds." He did not take the notebook out. "I am telling you that it exists because in about two years somebody is going to be in a room telling a person in a good coat that there is no connection between a community hall in Kettle Rows and a shut floor over your head, and I am telling you now, to you, in a yard, so that if that ever happens you will be able to say that a man told you in December that he had been in the hall and had seen the page and had not written it down. That is all it is. It is not evidence. It is me putting a fact in your hands that I am not allowed to put in a file."

"You have not asked me for anything for that."

"No."

"Then I will give you one thing, and I am going to think about it for the rest of the day and possibly not give it to you, and if I do not, you are not to come back."

---

He was on the bus at ten past one and he got the thing before the bus came.

"The plate," said Vilma Craddock. "On that stair. You asked me in November and I told you the number was not a flat number and I have thought about that for six weeks and I have got it wrong. It is not that it is not a flat number. It is that there is a letter in it."

"A letter."

"A letter in it, in the middle, like a dash, and it is not a floor, because a floor is a number and there is no such thing as letter five. I have read that plate about once a year for eleven years and I have never been up those stairs and I am not going to, and every time I read it I get the same thing and I have never once told anybody." She was shutting the door. "Letters in numbers are for accounts. I do not know what an account is. I know that a man came round in the summer with a padlock and put it on the bottom of that stair and I saw him from this window, and the padlock is not mine and it is not the building's either, because Felix from the shop has the only key to the yard and Felix has never had one for that stair."

"And the door on the third floor."

"Pushed," said Vilma Craddock. "Not locked. A boy from number nineteen went up in the summer to see what was there and came down again about four minutes later and said the door was not locked, it was pushed, and he did not go in, and he has not said anything about it since, and neither have I, and that has been the arrangement between the two of us since the summer and I would like it noted that it is the calmest thing on this street."

The door shut. It was not hard and it was not slow.

Elias Venn stood in the yard for a minute and then went out through the gate and did not go up the stair, which was eight feet away and unbarred apart from a new padlock that had been cut and put back about six times.

---

The Aldergate Wing has a public corridor on the ground floor that anybody may stand in, and a nurses' station off it, and a room at the end of it about eight feet by eleven that is used for reviews, and on the afternoon of the nineteenth of December the room had a table in it with three chairs and a folder and a jug of water that nobody drank.

Sister Ivy Warhurst came out of the corridor at twenty-five past three with her coat already on and she had been in the room for fifty-five minutes and she had not cried, and she was not going to.

"Sit down," she said.

"You do not have to—"

"Sit down, I have got about four minutes before Petronella comes and tells me the van has come round twice."

He sat down on the second chair and she sat down on the end of the first one and did not take her coat off.

"It was not about the book," she said.

"What was it about?"

"About whether I understood what I had done." She turned the folder over and did not open it, which told him that whatever was in it had been read out to her and she had not been given it. "Fifty-five minutes of it, and about forty of those minutes were a woman from Personnel explaining to me what a member of the public is, and she had it on a card, and she read the card, and at the end of it she said, *and if you are ever asked to look at anything like that again the answer is to telephone this office first*, and I said I would, and she said that was the correct answer, and then she said a thing at the end that I did not expect."

"What did she say?"

"That I had done it properly." Sister Warhurst looked at the table. "I have been in this building twenty-six years and I have been made to write a minute four times and I have been shouted at by three matrons and I was shouted at by a doctor in 2016 in a corridor in front of nine people over a bed bath, and nobody in this city has ever told me that I did something properly and put it in a folder, and I have been sitting on this chair for two minutes trying to work out what I am supposed to do with that, and I have decided that I am allowed to be pleased about it, and I would like to be pleased about it for about a day and a half before I start being frightened again."

"What is the finding."

"I am back on the book on the seventh of January, a Monday, and it is a Monday because a Monday is when a ward sister goes back on a book." She said it and then said the rest of it, slowly. "**And the minute says that the four photographs in my drawer in the office behind this station are not to be released to any office, and that the page pinned with them is not to be released to any office, and that the officer who took them has them and does not need them.** Written in, in the folder, in the words of the woman from Personnel, over her own name, with a date on it. I have been in this building twenty-six years and in about three of those years somebody read that book and there is nothing in any of the three about me. There is now. And it says the thing I have been frightened of since November, which is that somebody might come and take the four prints off me."

"Nobody is going to take them."

"No, and I know that, and I still put the folder in the drawer and locked it, and I have not been able to work out why." She stood up. "And that is the whole of the review and you did not have to be in the building for it, and I know why you were in the building, and I am not going to ask."

"I was in the building for the corridor."

"You were in the building for the corridor," said Sister Warhurst.

---

Petronella Osei, at the desk at the end of the corridor, with her cardigan on and a card down the side of the machine, watched a man of thirty-six in a good coat not go past her desk for two hours and twenty minutes, and she did not take a name off him once, because you do not take a name off a person who is not going anywhere.

"What did she say to you in there?" she asked at about five to four.

"That I did not have to be in the building."

"That is not what I asked and you know it is not."

"Then I will not answer it," said Elias Venn, "and you have taught me to do that and I am grateful, and if I ever stand at your desk and say her name I will do it with a witness, because there is a page in a drawer in nine years with no surname on it and I am not going to be the man who puts one there."

Petronella Osei put the card straight.

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said on this corridor since November," she said, "and I am going to be here until six and you are not, and that is fine, and go on."

---

The bus queue on Wexford Row at ten minutes to five had a woman in it with a shopping trolley, and about eight other people, and the light had gone early because of the weather, and the woman with the trolley got on the same bus as Elias Venn, down at the far end, without any difficulty at all, and put the trolley across the aisle and said thank you to nobody in particular.

The bus went over two bridges and stopped in Tanners Row, and he got off at a set of lights that were not the ones he wanted, which made two wrong stops in two days, and there was nothing in the notebook about either of them, and a woman at number twenty-two in Cutlass Row had shut a door on a bucket store and had given him a letter inside a number and had not asked him for a single thing, and would open it, or would not, on her own account and on no other.
