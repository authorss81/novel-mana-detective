# Chapter 0083 — First Floor, Back

A hostel at 6 Tanners Row has thirty-one beds and a reception desk on the ground floor with a bell on the counter that a person rings for themselves, which tells you inside about ten seconds that the people who come through that door are not the sort of people who want to be announced.

There is a waiting area with four chairs in it and a table with a leaflet rack on it and a coffee machine that takes coins, and the leaflets are about beds and about a hostel in Halloway Street and about a hostel two streets from the ring road, and there is a notice about the fire door being kept clear that has been on the wall so long the paper has gone the colour of weak tea.

Elias Venn had one question. He had come from Saint Orra Reach on a Saturday afternoon with a piece of paper six inches by two in a drawer behind a hospital's linen office, and on Saturday night he had not slept much, and on Sunday morning he had done four jobs in the shop, one of which was a clock, and at ten past eleven he had got on a bus and gone four stops and walked, and he had a question in his head and he was going to ask it once and then he was going to go away.

"Can I ask you one general question."

"You can ask me one general question and you will not like the answer and I have not got a great deal of time before the lunch lot comes in at two." Hester Kwan was forty-four and had been at that desk for eleven years and she had a booking book open in front of her and a cardigan with a pen in the pocket of it, and she did not say her name and did not offer it. "Go on."

"About six years ago, a man stayed here two nights. He came in on a Wednesday evening and he went out on the Thursday afternoon and he did not come back, and I am not a relative and I am not a police officer and I am not a clinician. I am a member of the public. I am not here about the room he had and I am not going to ask about the room he had. Was he here."

She put her pen down.

"No."

"No."

"No, I am not going to do that with you. Sit down, no, stand there, I do not care. I have had men in this chair in February telling me they were from a hospital and men in that chair in July telling me they were from a council, and the ones who tell me who they are first are the ones I say no to fastest, because a man who says *I am a member of the public* at a hostel reception desk at eleven in the morning on a Sunday is a man who has thought about what he is going to say, and that is fine, and it does not make you a person I talk to about a guest from 2023."

"Then I will go."

"You will sit down for about four minutes and drink the coffee because it is two credits and I am not having it wasted, and then I will tell you the thing you did not ask for, and then you will go."

He put a coin in. The machine took it and made a noise like somebody clearing their throat and produced about a third of a cup.

"That is a machine from 1994," said Hester Kwan, "and it is the only thing in this building I would not replace."

There were three people in the waiting area and none of them was using the chairs well. A woman of about twenty-six with a baby asleep against her shoulder and a bag of nappies on the seat beside her, doing a thing with her thumb and her jaw. A man of about seventy asleep in the third chair with a newspaper over his face and his shoes on the seat in front of him, which Hester Kwan had clearly decided to allow. And a young man of about nineteen with a rucksack between his feet, awake, holding a leaflet about a hostel in Halloway Street, not reading it.

"Fire door," said the young man. "Is that a rule."

"It is a rule that is printed on a piece of paper that has been on that wall so long the paper is weak tea, and it is a rule because in February of 2022 somebody's cousin left a bag in the corridor and everybody came in through this door at once, and we have had the notice up since." She nodded at the young man without looking at him. "You can go through the fire door, by the way. It is a door, it is not a wall. Nobody has ever in eleven years put a chair against it and that is not because of me, that is because they have not thought of it."

"Somebody thought of it," said the young man. "Somebody always thinks of it."

"They think of it in about four years," said Hester Kwan, "and then I will have a problem and it will be your generation's problem and you will not be here."

The man on the third chair took the newspaper off his face and said, without any preamble and to nobody, that it was a lie about the bridge, and put it back.

---

"What I am going to tell you is not in a book and you cannot have it in a book, and if you come back and ask me for it in a book I shall tell you to get out."

"All right."

"Wednesday. Six years and about ten weeks back. October. He came in at about nine at night with a canvas bag and he had a thing in the bag that was about a foot long and wrapped in newspaper, and he paid for two nights in cash and he did not want a receipt and I made him take one anyway because in eleven years I have had exactly one guest refuse a receipt and I did not like it."

"What did he look like."

"I am not going to answer that either, and you will tell me that I have just told you six years of the wrong details. I have not. I have told you a canvas bag and a foot of wrapped newspaper and the fact that he would not have a receipt, and that is what I remember, and I remember it because the second part is odd." She turned the booking book a quarter of a turn. "He was not a man who is in a great deal of trouble. He was a man who is going somewhere for a reason. He asked me three questions before he went out on the Thursday and I have thought about those three questions for six years and they are the only three questions anybody has ever asked me that were not about money."

"What were they."

"I am not going to give you three," she said. "You get one. You came in here and you said one question and you have not asked me a second one, and I have been watching you not ask me a second one for four minutes, which is a thing men do not usually manage. **What did she say to him in the doorway.**"

Elias Venn put the coffee down on the counter without having drunk any of it.

"She told him it was an hour," said Hester Kwan. "That is what I heard from eleven feet away, and I heard it because he repeated it back to her, and he repeated it back the way a man repeats a price. And she said it was for a name and a date. And he said what name and what date, and she said that was the question, and he asked her twice — twice, and I want that in whatever you are keeping — he asked her twice whether he was going to lose anything, and she said no. And he said all right."

"And then?"

"And then he used a word." She stopped and looked at the ceiling for a second, the way a person does when they are checking whether they are about to be honest or about to be funny. "He said *source*. He said, *is it a source.* And she said something I did not hear, and he said *right*, and he went out at about half past four."

"A source of what?"

"That is the question I have been carrying about since 2023 and I have asked nobody, because there is nobody to ask." Hester Kwan put both hands flat on the booking book. "I did not know what it meant then. I have not known what it means now. I have gone through the whole of what I have read in eleven years at a desk and I have never found a use of the word that fits a man standing in a doorway at half past four in the afternoon, and I want you to understand that I am not a person who says a thing because it sounds good. I am a person at a counter. I have had a hundred people lie to me about why they need a bed and I have never once been fooled and I have never once cared as much as I care about that word."

"You were eleven feet away."

"I was behind a desk eleven feet away and there was a radio on in here in 2023 and I have turned it down on my own ever since, and I do not know whether that was because of that afternoon or because I am the sort of person who does that." She picked the pen up and put it down again. "I am not sure about the doorway. I am about as sure as I can be about a thing like that, and *as sure as I can be* is not sure, and if you write it down as sure I will find out, because people come back."

"I will write it down as not sure."

"Then that is the first correct thing anybody has written about that afternoon and it has taken me six years and a cup of coffee from a machine in 1994."

---

She had a book on the desk and it was a hardback house book with a printed cover and about four hundred names in it in nine different hands, and there was a column down the right-hand side headed, in capitals, **WHERE WENT**, and it was empty from top to bottom.

"That is where I write where they went," she said. "I have never written a word in it. I did not write in it in my first year and I decided in my first year and I have been right for eleven years."

"What made you decide in your first year."

"A man of about forty came in one night in the February of my first winter and he went out on the Thursday and he came back on the Sunday with a different name, and I wrote it down, because I thought I was being careful." She put her thumb on the empty column. "And then in the April I had to tell a woman in an office in Calder Street that I had written a name down that was not the name he had given me, and I had written it down in a book that anybody could come in and read, and there was a house on Cartwright Street where somebody's daughter was living that had nothing to do with any of it, and I had put her address in the same column in September, three lines below his, because I was being *careful*."

"So you stopped."

"So I stopped, and I have not written a word in it in eleven years, and it is the only thing in this building I am proud of, and it is going to look like a great big waste of a column to anybody who is not me." She closed the book. "So you are not getting that book and you are not getting the column and you are not getting a photograph of it, and if I let anybody have a copy of that column then a man in a nice coat could come in with a warrant for a house on Cartwright Street in about four years and I would have handed it to him myself."

"I was not going to ask."

"You were going to ask in about eleven minutes, once I had said the word *column*, and that is when people ask, because I have said the word."

She took the machine cup off the counter and tipped what was in it into the sink, because it had gone cold and because it was nearly a cup and a half of water with coffee in it, and she said, "One more thing and then I have to do the two o'clock."

"Yes."

"You wrote *first floor, back* in my head, I can see it, and if you go up a first floor in this district and knock on a door you will find nine first floors and I am not going to help you find the tenth. What I am going to tell you is that when she came in on the Wednesday night with him, and I have told you she came in because he was not going to be able to carry that bag and get a bed, which I know because he tried twice and put it down twice, and there is a bench in there and a woman with a bag that size cannot get through that door past a man with a suitcase — she told me the floor."

"And the street."

"She told me *first floor, back*, and she said it to me the way you say it to a person who is writing something down, and I wrote it, and there is no street on that line because she did not give me a street." Hester Kwan put the cup in the rack. "**I have thought about that for six years. I have been at this desk for eleven years and I have taken four thousand addresses off people and she gave me a floor and a position in a building and no street, and I did not ask her for one, because I was writing, and because a person who has not offered you a street is a person who has a reason about the street, and in 2023 I was the kind of clerk who thought the reason would be told to me later.**"

"So she was careful."

"Everybody is careful," said Hester Kwan. "That is the whole of what I have learned at a counter. The ones who are not careful are the ones you want to be careful about."

---

He left the money for the next cup in the tray and did not tell her he had done it, and he was on the pavement of Tanners Row at twenty to two on a Sunday afternoon with a question asked and answered and a witness who had been right not to be sure.

The bus stop at the end of Tanners Row has a timetable in a case with a Sunday column and the Sunday column is the only one that is not a column of times. It is a column of words. *No service before 09:30.* That is all it says, and it has said it since before the timetable was in a case.

There were eleven people at the stop and none of them was going anywhere, which is a thing you can tell at a stop on a Sunday at twenty to two. A man of about nineteen with a bag of football things. Two women with a pushchair. A man in a hi-vis with a flask, asleep standing up, and Elias Venn thought about the one on the bus on Friday and thought: *there is a man in a hi-vis on every single one of these routes and he is always asleep and I have never once seen him get off*.

He looked at the timetable for a while, and the thing he had learned on Saturday morning was sitting in him and would not go down, and it was not a fact about anybody. It was a shape. There was a sentence at the foot of a form, printed in a smaller type, on every sheet in a tray four hundred deep behind a counter on Fenwick Street, and the sentence said that a man had been told what the work was and had had the chance to ask questions, and it did not say what the work was for, and there was a man in a doorway six years ago who had asked whether it was a *source*, and been told something, and had said *right*.

The 09:30 was the only thing in the case with any words in it, and he read it three times, and then the bus came, and it was the 09:30 bus, at twenty to two, on a Sunday, and it was empty apart from the driver, and the driver opened the doors and looked at eleven people on a pavement in a district where nothing was happening and said, "That's the first one. You want the first one."

"Does this one go to the ring road?"

"It goes everywhere the first one goes," said the driver, "and I have been driving it eleven years and I can tell you now that I have never once had a conversation on it, and that is because the only people who get on it are people who have been awake since four in the morning."

Elias Venn got on. There were eleven people on the pavement and none of them got on, and the doors shut, and the bus went four hundred yards and stopped at a light, and he sat at the back with his hand on the rail and his book in his pocket with a claim number in it and a false memory in it that he had never written down, and neither of them was the reason he was on the bus.
