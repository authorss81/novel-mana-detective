# Chapter 0080 — Three Kinds of Paper

The light in 14 Kettle Rows had not been on since the summer and he put it on at ten past nine on the morning of Boxing Day and left it on, and Otto Vahl came up the half flight at about ten and looked at the front window of the shop from the back room and did not say anything about it, and that was the whole of the conversation.

There were three credits' worth of jobs on the bench and he did them. He did not open the front until half past ten, because there is no point in opening a repair shop on Boxing Day for about forty minutes before a kettle, and no customer came.

He wrote four paragraphs on a sheet of his own writing paper at the front bench with the good light, and it took him a long time, and none of it was a list.

> *On Friday the twenty-first of December, at about ten past four in the afternoon, in the back room of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic, in front of a member of staff and a patient, I said out loud that a mark in the middle of a document was a false start. It was not a false start. It was a figure written twice by a person who did not like the first one, and a woman in a laboratory told me in December that that is a fact about pens, and I had written it down in my own book in her words, and I said it in a room anyway.*

> *I said the rest of it too. There is a release on the end of that document and it is not committed and nobody signed it. I do not know who made it and I did not say and I was not asked. I have not been in the same material as a patient for one second and I said so in the room before I said anything else.*

> *A woman of sixty-one who has had a repeat at that dispensary for four years has not been able to complete a form since about the middle of November because there is nobody at her address to receive one. She went without for four nights, from the Friday evening to Tuesday morning, because a repeat comes off that rack against a completed form and a note on a file is not a form. I asked a member of staff on the Saturday whether she could have it on the Monday, and I did not apologise, and I did not explain, and I have not spoken to her at all, and I do not know what I would have said.*

> *I was wrong in front of her and I said the wrong thing first. I am a member of the public with a formal reprimand and I have no standing in that building and I have been wrong in five rooms in this city since September, and this is the first time being wrong in a room has cost anybody anything. I would like it recorded that I have not put this on the board and that I am not going to, and that the woman who owns that board has asked me twice for a wall and been refused twice, and that this is for her drawer and not for the board and not for a file and not for a man in an insurance building who wrote a four-line note in November and has never been told that any of it exists.*

He read it through twice, and there was no number in it of any kind, and it went into a plain envelope with nothing on it.

---

The waiting room had nineteen people in it at eleven o'clock on Boxing Day, which was more than a clinic should have, and there were four of them who had stood in the queue on the Monday the building reopened in November and had not been back since, and two children, and the sheet was at the top of the fan with air round it on all four sides and the four drawing pins in it were his.

Sana Okafor came out of the back office at eleven and stopped when she saw him standing in front of the board, and then she came over and looked at the board, which was a thing they had not done together, and said, "It looks wrong with the fan gone. I have looked at it four times a week for nineteen years and I have got used to the wrong and now I can see the right of it and the right of it is a lot smaller."

"You asked me for it," he said, and gave her the envelope.

She did not take it into the office. She opened it standing up in the middle of a waiting room with nineteen people in it and read the four paragraphs standing up, and it took her between eight and nine seconds, and she read them once and not twice.

Then she folded them once and put them back in the envelope and gave it to him.

"That is the second document you have written in my building in four months and the first one went on a wall," she said. "This one goes in the drawer in my office on the shelf under the maintenance book, and it is mine, and I will read it again in about four years when I am bored, and I will not be impressed, and I am telling you that now so that you do not spend four years hoping that somebody will be."

"That is fair."

"Here is what I am actually going to say, and then I have got a list." She kept her voice down and did not look at him. "You had one thing to do. You had four days. And I am not going to be standing in a doorway on a Friday for ever because of it, and I am not going to be disappointed in you on a Tuesday either, because that is how a condition like that dies — not by being broken, by being waited on. **I said I would stand in the doorway. That is all it ever was. I was saying I would be there.**"

"Yes," said Elias Venn.

"Say it back."

"You were saying you would be there, and I did not use it, and there was a woman in a doorway, and it was eleven seconds, and there is no way of it having been anything else."

"That is the first time anybody has said a thing back to me in that building and got it right," said Sana Okafor, "and I have got a list, and you are going to be here for it, and I am not going to have this conversation in a corridor."

---

---

There was a woman of about seventy-two at the board at five to eleven with a shopping bag on her feet, and she had been reading the bottom of the fan every Friday for about four years, and she was not happy.

"They've taken your pages down."

"Somebody has taken my pages down."

"It's not you then." She moved her bag about two inches. "I read them while I wait. I have a hip and I am in here every Friday and it is the only thing I read. I have read about the water going off the top floor in March, and about the lamp in the corridor, and about a man in a van that came for a part on a Tuesday and did not come back until the Thursday, and about the little girl who came in with an earache in the summer and waited an hour and a half with a plastic spoon in her hand." She looked at the rectangle. "You cannot have that with a hip. You cannot have that and say it was all right, because it was not all right, and somebody sat in this room and read it, and now there is nothing there but a clean bit of board."

"No," said Elias Venn. "There is not."

"Well." She put her bag back where it was. "Then somebody should have asked me, and I am not saying they did wrong. I am saying that in about four years when that dispensary lamp goes again there will be nobody in this room who can say it happened twice before, and it will have happened twice before, and nobody will be able to prove it, and that is what happens to a fact when a wall goes up in front of it. My mother was a district nurse. That is the only thing I have ever said about her in here and I have said it to about four people and one of them was a man with a sheet of paper."

She looked at the sheet at the top of the fan.

"That is yours, that."

"That is mine."

"Then you have had a bad month," said the woman, "and so have I, and I am going to read the top of the fan instead, and I shall tell you what I think of it when you have finished whatever it is you are doing."

---

"What I am going to ask you," he said, at five past eleven, in the middle of the waiting room, in a voice pitched so that it would carry about nine feet and no further, "is a question, and I am going to ask it in a room with nineteen people in it, and I am asking it because a doctor told me in a car in November that a certified record that is false in a register has to be challenged in public or it is a register, and I did not take her advice and I have thought about it every day since, and I have arrived somewhere different, and I would like the somewhere to be said out loud in a room or it is not anywhere.

"**Are the arrangements that keep these people alive the same arrangements that keep them out of every book in this city?**"

It is a long question and it is not a clever one and it is in the plainest words he could find for it, and about eleven people in that waiting room heard the whole of it.

Nobody answered.

A man in a coat near the door said, "Are we talking about the pensions at the hall?" and a woman said, "No," and then nothing happened for about twenty seconds and a child dropped a toy and somebody picked it up, and Dr Okafor stood against the wall with her arms folded and let it lie, which was the correct thing to do and cost her something.

Then she said, to the room, in the same voice, not to him: "Eleven of them. There is a drawer in this building with eleven names on it and not one of their own addresses, and there is a rack behind that counter with eleven envelopes on it, and nobody in this building had put the two pieces of paper side by side until Saturday, and the man who did it is standing over there with nothing in his hands."

And a man of about sixty at the back said, "Is that the same as the list on the board?"

"That is the same as the list on the board," said Sana Okafor, "and I did not know, and I have run this building for nineteen years."

Nobody else said anything, and about four people looked at the board, and the nineteen went back to being nineteen people in a waiting room on a wet afternoon in a city where it was the twenty-sixth of December and nothing whatsoever happened.

"That is not an answer," said Sana Okafor, when the room had gone back to itself, and she said it to him and not to the room. "I have given you four facts and a number and they are correct and they are not an answer, and I want that said out loud in this building as well, because in about four years somebody is going to come in here and tell a patient that the question was asked and answered on the twenty-sixth of December in a waiting room, and it was not."

---

The dispensary counter at twenty past twelve on Boxing Day.

Doreen Tillery was on the late and the rack had been cleared down to six envelopes because it was Boxing Day and the building was quiet.

"She rang at nine this morning," she said.

"Who rang at nine this morning."

"Mrs Ferrers. She rang the dispensary at nine this morning and asked whether her repeat could be sent to the dispensary on Halloway Street instead, and she was very polite about it and she said it was because of the walk, and it is not because of the walk, and I did not ask, because I do not ask." Doreen Tillery did not look up from the rack. "And I said yes, and it went out at ten on Christmas morning, and there is no rule that says she cannot do that, and there is no form for a woman to have her repeat moved from one dispensary to another, and she could have done it in September, and I have known that since November and I have said it to myself about forty times and it has not once helped."

"And you did it in about nine seconds."

"I did it in about nine seconds and I have been thinking about those nine seconds since about half past nine and I have not been able to stop, and I have got a bad thing to tell you and I am going to tell you it because I would rather you had it from me than worked it out in about a fortnight and thought I had hidden it." She put the last envelope on the rack. "I cannot fill a form in for her. I have never been able to fill a form in for her. There is a box on that form that says where to send it and I have never been able to complete it, and when I asked her, in about November, she said she was staying nowhere and I put down the address of the friend whose floor she is on, and I have been doing that for eleven years for about eleven women, and it has worked, and the friend does not know, and the friend has never known, and the friend is a woman of about sixty-five who has had a stranger's prescription in her letterbox every three weeks for a year and has never once asked about it."

"That is the only arrangement there is."

"That is the only arrangement there is," said Doreen Tillery, "and I have been quietly building it in a drawer for eleven years out of nothing but kindness and about nine seconds at a time, and I want you to understand something, because I do not think you have seen it. **Nobody has taken anything away from her. It was never hers.** There is no address in her own name, so there was never a form she could have completed, so there was never a repeat she could have claimed, so there has never been a thing in her life that a person in an office could have refused her. She has got her tablets every Friday for four years and she has not once been refused, and she is losing it this week because a man said a thing in a back room."

Elias Venn stood at a counter on Marshall Street at ten to one on the twenty-sixth of December with nothing in his hands and a woman of fifty-two in front of him, and the arithmetics of it were the arithmetics of the whole of the case and of the last four months and of every building he had been turned away from at the door, and they were perfectly simple, and he had known them since the seventeenth of December, and he had spent nine days getting to them.

"Will you tell me what you did on Tuesday," he said.

Doreen Tillery did not answer for a moment, and then she took a form out of the drawer under the counter and put it on the counter, flat, and turned it round so that it faced him, and it was a completed form, and the first box on it was full.

"I wrote the address in myself," she said. "The house she is at. The friend's. And I put my initials in the fifth box, which is *who told the patient*, and I put your doctor's name in it, which is correct, and then I put a second line under it with my own name on it, because I decided that the second line was the true one and I am the one who has to be on this piece of paper.

"And then I wrote her repeat on it and I sent it to the Halloway Street dispensary and I did not tell the friend, and I have not told the friend in about eleven years of doing this, and I am not going to start on Christmas Day." She put her hand flat on the form, the way you do on a counter. "So there is a piece of paper in a file in this building with a house that is not hers on it and my name on it, and it is the first one in eleven years that has got both, and it is on my own initiative, and nobody made me, and I would like it noted that nobody made me, because if it was made of me then it would be a rule and it is not a rule, it is me, and in about four years I shall not be in this room and somebody else will be, and they will not know why there is an address in that box."

"You have just made yourself the person the arrangement is run by."

"I have," said Doreen Tillery, "and I have been that person since about November and I did not know the word for it, and I would like you to go and have your tea now, because you have been standing at my counter since half past twelve and you are not a customer and I have not got anything for you." 

Nineteen days on the order. Somebody in a municipal building was going to write a word on a form on the fourteenth of January, and he had known since November what the two possible words were and had deliberately written neither of them down, and there was exactly one address in that district that anybody knew for the thing the order is about, and it was a first-floor room above a dry cleaner, and a woman in that room had written back one line by post on the fourteenth of December.

---

He did not put it on the board. He did not put the certificate anywhere, and he never would, and he has still not got it, and the record of issue in a tray in a district office carries no patient and no procedure and will go on carrying none.

He did not go to the Verge Row side, where a claims director had given him two documents that were hers about her own money, and he did not go to Corrie Street, where a woman was working and had told him not to come. He did not telephone Vine Street. There is a Sunday in the third week of December that is written in the back of his notebook in his own hand in November, with the day of the week on it, and it came and went without a car park in it or a woman in a coat, and he has not told anybody, and he is not going to before somebody asks him a question about it that is not a question about it.

The wall in the waiting room had three kinds of paper on it and a rectangle on the board that was a paler colour than the rest of it, and two pin holes, and a sheet at the top of the fan with air round it on all four sides.

At four o'clock Doreen Tillery put her coat on over her cardigan and stood in front of the closed rack with her hand flat on it, and said the last thing of the year anybody said to him about any of it.

"There is a fifth box on our form," she said. "*Who told the patient.* It is the only box on either piece of paper that anybody in either building ever fills in, and I fill it in every single time, and I fill it in with the doctor's name, and I would fill it in if the doctor's name were the wrong one."

She took her hand off the rack.

"It is the only one of the five that anybody has ever bothered to answer, and I have not got the faintest idea why, and I have been doing it for fourteen years, and it is the last thing I am going to say to anybody in 2029."
