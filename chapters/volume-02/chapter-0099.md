# Chapter 0099 — The Tuesday Room

Kieran Slack let them in at about ten past five on Tuesday the twenty-second of January and stood on the far side of his own counter with his arms folded while Fenella Carr read the warrant out loud, twice, and the address was in full and the words *first floor* were on it twice, and Kieran Slack listened to both readings the way a man listens to his own name.

"It does not say premises once," she said. "I had the magistrate print it three times, and I have read it to you, and I have read it at a quarter to seven this afternoon to a district nurse on the ground floor of that building, because she is going to be the second person in that building tonight."

"You are not going up."

"I am going to sit on the bottom step of that stair for about five hours and I am going to be let in when the room is opened, and if anybody wants to know why then it is because an inspector told me on Wednesday in a corridor in writing and I signed it."

Kieran Slack unlocked the back room at five twenty-five, let a sergeant of forty-one and two constables through it, and went back behind his counter and put his hand flat on it, and then took it away and put it in his pocket, which was not a thing he had ever had to do before five o'clock in the afternoon in four years.

The stair door at the top was locked. It was unlocked from the inside by a man of fifty-two with a set of keys on a ring on his belt, and Nadia Kest had him arrested for the offence of unregistered practice in respect of memory and identity work before his feet were on the landing, and he said nothing at all while it happened, and then he said his piece.

"I am the practitioner," said Ivor Skellern. "Since 2026, and there is nobody above me and nobody below me, and I have said that once and you can put it in a piece of paper with a number on it. And there is a woman in the fifth chair who came in in the spring of 2026 and has not been able to pay anything since, and I have done two hours for nothing about four times and never told her, and I would like somebody to tell her, and I would like it to be somebody who is not me."

---

The room was empty and eleven chairs were in it. There was a couch against the wall with a blanket folded on the end of it, a table with a glass of water on it that had not been touched, a tin about the size of a cake tin with no lid on it, and a cloth folded and squared over one end of the table, and nothing under it.

"Under the cloth," said Nadia Kest.

"There is nothing under the cloth."

"Then I am going to write down that the member of the public told me there was a sheet of A4 with a drawing of a room on it, and it is not here, and I am going to write down that the man who was in this room has been arrested and has declined to say where it is." She looked at him. "Is it in the building."

"It is in my coat," said Ivor Skellern, "and I would like it photographed where it is, because it is folded in four and it has a date on it and a code, and if you unfold it on that table then a man in a wig will say I have had the chance to make it into something else."

"In your coat, then," said Nadia Kest, "and Fenella, on the stair, and a photograph of the inside of a coat pocket with the camera moved and nothing moved, and it goes into an envelope on the landing and it stays folded."

They waited in a first-floor room above a shop on a parade in Kettle Rows for two hours and ten minutes, and it is the part of that night that nobody has ever asked Elias Venn about, and it is the part he would go back to first.

There were five of them in the room or the corridor by about six o'clock and there was nowhere for five of them to be. Two constables stood at the bottom of the stair because a landing is a choke point. The sergeant sat on the couch with her hands on her knees. Nadia Kest stood by the table and did not sit down for about two hours, and at about half past six a doctor of forty-seven came up the back stairs with a clipboard, and at about seven a woman came up the back stairs with a notebook under her arm and stood in the corridor and did not go into the room, because she had said on Monday night that she was not going to be in a room where she did not know who was in it, and there was nobody in it.

"You are early," said Nadia Kest.

"There is a fire door on the other side of that building and I have been sitting by it since a quarter past six." Mara Quill had her coat on. "I have brought one of the four and not the plate, and the plate is on a shelf in Saint Orra Reach where it has been since the third of November, and it is not coming."

"I was not going to ask you about it."

"You were going to ask me about it in about ten minutes, which is a corridor," said Mara Quill, "and I have beaten you to it."

At about twenty to eight somebody walked into the shop below and a bell went, and Kieran Slack said, in the voice of a man who has heard a bell four hundred times, "That is them."

They came up a wooden stair with a handrail on one side only, past a first landing with a meter cupboard on it, and there was a police officer at the top of it, and the officer did not ask anybody a question, and the eleven of them came up anyway: a woman of thirty-eight in a coat with a handbag on her knee, a woman of fifty-one with a bag of leeks, a man of thirty-six with his head down, a man of about forty-five holding a bicycle helmet, a woman of sixty-three with a zip purse in both hands, and a man of seventy-four who knew which chair was his. None of them went past that officer quickly. None of them went past him at all, and they all went past him.

"There are eleven of you," said Fenella Carr, from the landing.

"There are eleven of us," said a voice from the bottom of the stair. "We have got to be out by nine because the buses stop."

Nadia Kest looked at Elias Venn, who was sitting on the bottom step, and Elias Venn said, "I am not going to do anything. Nobody is going to do anything. You have a schedule."

The room was opened by the officer. The eleven went in and sat in the eleven chairs, most of them in the place they had sat a hundred and forty times, and the man of seventy-four went to the second chair on the left, and the woman of fifty-one sat in the fifth, and Bernadette Lyle sat in the fourth with her handbag on her lap.

The working was in the room by about eight minutes past eight, and everybody in it felt it at the same time, which was the thing Elias Venn had not been ready for and never would be.

He had his hand on the door frame and the room came up out of the floor of him, and there was a wall of nothing to hold and the left side of his head went, and the room was already in the middle of something, and all eleven people in it were looking at a chair.

"Everybody's looking at a chair," said the man of thirty-six, in a voice that was not frightened. "Why is everybody looking at a chair."

Nobody answered him. Elias Venn understood, standing on a landing that was not a landing, that nobody in that room was going to answer for about four minutes, and that if one of them did, it would be the last thing any of them did for a long time.

A constable of about twenty-six took four steps in and looked at the fourth chair.

"Which one," he said. "Which one is it."

He put his hand out towards the back of the chair Bernadette Lyle was sitting in, and his hand was about six inches from her shoulder.

"**Stop.**"

The constable stopped with his hand where it was.

"Say what."

"Say it again, in a voice the whole room can hear, and take your hand off that chair." Elias Venn did not move off the step. "**I am a member of the public. I have a finding against me in December and I am not a constable and I am not a doctor. I have a faculty and I do not use it on people. I have never read a person in my life, I am not reading anybody in this room, and there is a woman in the fourth chair who is the one it is happening in and I cannot look at her.** I am saying it in a room with eleven people in it so that if a policeman's hand goes towards a frightened woman in the next hour somebody here knows why it stops."

The constable took his hand off the chair.

"That will be in the report."

"It will be in the report in your words and not in his," said Nadia Kest, "and I have written it, and he read it back this afternoon and he signed it, and it is a piece of paper and it is the only piece of paper in this city that says a man in a good coat cannot look at a person, and I did not write it for him."

"You are in a room with eleven people in it."

"I am in a corridor, and I have been in a corridor since six o'clock, and that is where I am going to stay, and the reason is that the only thing this city has that stops one of these is a person standing still in a place."

Sana Okafor went along the row of eleven chairs with her hand a foot off the nearest shoulder and asked each person their name, what day it was, and the year, and wrote eleven lines, and asked nothing else.

"They are all oriented," she said. "Three of them have answered me in somebody else's voice and I am not going to try again for the next hour, because trying again is what gets people hurt. They are not asleep. They are sitting up and they are watching a chair."

"Ma'am, with respect," said Fenella Carr, "we do not have anybody in this room who can tell me what is happening in it."

"Neither do I," said Sana Okafor. "There is nothing in twenty-three years for a room where eleven people watch a chair, and I am telling you that as a professional, because a woman who pretends to know is worse than a woman who says so." She turned round to the room. "**I am going to speak to all of you and keep speaking for as long as I can, and I am not going to ask you to do anything. If you can hear my voice, put your hand on the arm of your chair. If you cannot, do not do anything at all, and that is also a correct answer.**"

Ten hands went onto ten armrests.

The eleventh was a woman of about thirty-eight in the fourth chair who put both her hands on the arms of her chair and said, "I can hear you, and I want to say something, and I have said it four times and nobody has ever answered me."

"Say it now," said Sana Okafor.

"I am going to say it now," said Bernadette Lyle, "and I have said it four times and I am not going to say it again after tonight, because I have worked out that the only way anybody is going to answer it is if nobody is allowed to ask me anything else."

One tin, the size of a cake tin, with no lid, empty. Sana Okafor looked at the tin from about four feet away. "Do not open that in front of me. If there is anything in it with a name on it, then somebody in a hospital in this city is going to be told something on Wednesday that they have not been told, and I am not going to be the one who does it at nine o'clock at night with a police inspector standing over it."

"Open it," said Nadia Kest, "and not in this room."

"It is empty," said a man of fifty-two from a corridor, and the tin went into an envelope, and the schedule was signed by three people and read back twice, and the objects were lifted, and tagged, and weighed, and logged with a line each, and sealed, and the return was written out, and nobody in that room was photographed in it.

He did not touch it. The front was a room in ink with a door, a meter cupboard, a couch and eleven chairs, a scale in the bottom corner, a date in the top right in the form 04/06/26, and a code under it in a hand that leaned right and pressed hard. "Seven of the eleven places on the front are drawn once," he said. He put his hand out in front of the fourth chair and put his body between her and the room, because he did not think about it.

The Return travels the working looking for something that can hold what is left of it. There was nothing in that room that could hold it except a person, and there were eleven people in it, and the room began, not deliberately, the way water finds a crack, to go towards the one who had asked.

He took it.

It was not like pain. He had been ready for pain and had said so to a man in a kitchen and been wrong. It was not like a nosebleed, or the order of a day, or a place, or a duration. It was a room. The room went, quietly, for a gap, to the place where the turn had happened and the release had come to look for the thing it was going to close over, and found a refusal in it, and found the refusal was not finished.
