# Chapter 0064 — The Record of Issue

The district office on Harrow Lane had a fan in the ceiling that came on at ten past nine and went off at half past three, and a smell of warm paper that did not go away, and two long tables with a tray on each end of them, and the trays had what was in them in an order that the people who worked there understood and that no visitor ever would.

Elias Venn was let in at four minutes to nine by a woman of about forty-five who looked at his coat, and then at the door, and then at his coat again.

"Public enquiries is through there, and it is a queue, and I am not going to pretend you have not seen the queue from the step."

"I have not got a case number," said Elias Venn, "and I have not got a form, and I am not here to make an application for anything. I want to ask whether a document exists. That is all I am here to ask, and if the answer is no, I will go."

The woman considered this for longer than it deserved.

"The office does not answer whether a document exists," she said. "The office answers whether this office has a record of it. Those are different answers and the second one is a fact about a filing system and the first one is a fact about a person, and we only keep the first kind."

"Then I want the second one."

"Second desk on the right. Ruth will tell you what you can have." She sat down. "And I am going to say one thing and then I am going to stop saying it, because you have got the look. Nothing on any table in this room leaves this room. Not a copy, not a photograph, not a note of it in a book you take home. **If you write it down, you have copied it, and a copy you made is not a copy you may use.**"

---

Ruth Callow had been on the trays for nineteen years and had a way of turning a page that did not involve her hands, which is a thing you learn or you do not, and she had learned it, and it took about a second and a half.

"Date of service and premises," she said. "Those are the two things I can index on. I cannot index on a name, because there is no name, and I cannot index on a claim number, because that is a claimant's and you have not got one, and I certainly cannot index on a procedure, because there are fourteen of them and I would be here until Friday."

"Eleven September 2029," said Elias Venn. "Second to eighteenth Wexford Row, the rehabilitation wing, the Harlowmere premises. Any account, any ward, any procedure."

She went along the tray. It took her about a minute and a half and she did not hum and she did not hurry and she did not say anything encouraging, and when she had it she put a single sheet of thin paper on a clipboard and turned it round and pushed it two inches towards him, which was as far as it went.

> **RECORD OF ISSUE — SEQUENCE CERTIFICATION, DISTRICT OFFICE**
> *Certificate:* CR-22-4471
> *Issued:* Tuesday 2 October 2029, 09:40
> *Issuing office:* Sequence Certification, district office, 14 Harrow Lane
> *Registrar-counsel:* B. Haile
> *Claimant of record:* Harlowmere Rest and Rehabilitation Ltd, 2 Wexford Row
> *Premises of service:* 2–18 Wexford Row (Harlowmere premises)
> *Service date of record:* 11 September 2029
> *Schedules relied on:* **No Schedule 1 material. No Schedule 2. No Schedule 3. Schedule 4 not compiled.**
> *This office holds a record of issue. It does not hold the certificate.*

"There is no patient on that," said Elias Venn.

"No."

"And no procedure."

"No."

"So the office that issued it does not know who was treated or what was done."

"The office that issued it knows that a certificate went to a payer on the second of October in respect of a claim on a claimant with a premises and a service date," said Ruth Callow. "That is a fact about this office and this office can stand on it. Who was in a bed and what was done to them is on somebody else's piece of paper, and the certificate is that piece of paper, and the certificate is not in this building." She put her hand flat on the clipboard. "I have said that sentence four hundred times and about four of the four hundred have asked me why, and it is always the same why, and the answer is always that the certificate went to the payer and the payer is not us, and nobody in this building may ring the payer and say *we have issued a certificate, would you like to confirm the patient.*"

"Why not?"

"Because the certificate says that it is not our job to know, and the office that has to be able to say *that is not our job to know* is the office that people are frightened of, and if we could not say it then nobody could sign the sheet you have just read." She turned the clipboard back. "You can copy that down. You cannot have the sheet. That is the door's rule and the door is not mine; my rule is the other one, and I have broken it four times in nineteen years and I have never once been sorry."

---

He copied it down in his own hand, in a notebook, in about four minutes, and he did it at the table she pointed at and not in the corridor, because a man who copies a thing down in a corridor is a man copying a thing down in a hurry. He copied all of it except the line at the top with the number on it, and he left a gap in his own book the width of the line it had come from, and wrote nothing in the gap, and Ruth Callow watched him leave it and did not say a word about it then or afterwards.

Then a door at the end opened, and a woman came out with a folder under her arm, and Elias Venn knew the walk before he knew the face, because he had watched it come across a room in Morrow Hill on the first of November with a certificate in her hand.

Bettina Haile stopped about six feet away and said, "No."

"I have not asked."

"You were going to, and I am saving us both about nine minutes." She put the folder down on the table, and did not sit, and did not open it. "Mr Venn. You have been to this office five times since the beginning of September and I have refused you five times, and the second time you wrote me a letter and the third time you wrote my director, and I want you to know that the letter went into a file and that the file exists and that I am not going to pretend to you that I have not read it twice."

"Then say the six words."

"I am not going to say six words, because six words would be a second hearing, and if there were such a thing then everybody refused here twice would come back a third time, and some of them are in jail." She looked at the notebook, which was face down on the table because he had turned it. "You have written something down and turned it over. That is correct and I would like everybody in this room to notice it, because about one in ten of them does it and the other nine are shocked when I tell them they cannot take it."

"I am not taking it."

"You are going to take a copy of a record of issue, and I am going to let you, and I am going to tell you exactly what a record of issue is worth, because you are the ninth person this year to ask me for one and the ninth is the first one who has asked for it correctly." She put both hands on the folder. "It is worth nothing. It has no patient in it and it has no procedure in it and it does not say whether anything happened. **What it does say is that on the second of October this office put a number into the world about a bed on a date, and the number is in a payer's drawer, and the payer's drawer is the only copy there is.**"

"And you have left a hole at the top of your page about the width of the line at the top of that sheet," said Bettina Haile, "which is the single cleverest thing anybody has done at that table in about four years, and you did it before I said one word about it, which means you are cleverer than you would like me to know you are."

"Then may I have the number."

"You already have it. You have had it since you read it, about six minutes ago, and you read it yourself, because I was not in the room." She said it without heat. "And you are not going to write it in that book, and if you do, then in about four years somebody is going to be able to say that a member of the public had a certificate number in a personal notebook with no form anywhere accounting for how he got it, and the number will be the only thing anybody can prove and I will have handed it to you myself."

"Since the corridor," said Elias Venn. "And I am not going to write it, and I want you to know that I understand exactly what you have just told me and that I would have arrived at it in about a fortnight." He did not say thank you, because he was not going to thank a woman for something he had worked out himself, and she looked at him for a second and then nodded, and it was the smallest nod anybody had given him in four months.

"Now sit down, because there is one more thing and then I have a file at two o'clock."

---

"What is on the certificate," said Bettina Haile, standing up, "is this, and I am going to read it to you because you cannot have it, and because a man who has heard it and a man who has read it are not in the same position, and because if I do not read it to you then in four years you will tell somebody I refused to, and you would be right.

"Certificate number CR-22-4471. Issued the second of October at twenty to ten in the morning by this office. Claimant of record Harlowmere Rest and Rehabilitation Ltd, 2 Wexford Row. **Patient schedule: Thirlmere 4.** **Procedure schedule: D-2, grounding and anchor, two hours, the eleventh of September.** Fee settled: sixty credits. And then the four schedules, which are the same four you will find on any dangerous-working certificate in this city, and they read *no Schedule 1 material, no Schedule 2, no Schedule 3, Schedule 4 not compiled.*"

"And underneath?"

"And underneath, in the words of the form, which are printed and which I did not write and which I sign every working day of my life: **the issuing office has certified the completeness of the record. The issuing office has not certified that the treatment was administered.** That is on the certificate. That is on every one. That sentence is the reason this office exists and the reason nothing has ever gone wrong in it and the reason two of the things in your case happened anyway."

"Thirlmere 4," said Elias Venn. "That ward is shut. It has been shut since the seventh of October, and there is a notice on the door with the date on it, and the door is locked, and the notice says the residents and their effects are on site."

"Yes," said Bettina Haile. "And what I am not going to tell you is why it shut, because there are about nine reasons a ward shuts and a date on a notice is not one of them, and the reason is on the manager's minute, and the manager's minute is not in this building and is not mine. Ask the manager. **Or do not, because a man who has been a member of the public for five weeks and asks a manager about a shut ward ends up being a man who was in that ward.**"

"Then a shut ward is a place where nothing happened."

"No," said Bettina Haile. "**A shut ward is a place where nothing happened *in that ward.***" She put her hand flat on the folder. "In a day room, on a bed number that belongs to the ward it came from, which is how it has been done for fifty years, because when you decant a ward you do not renumber the patients, you renumber the sheets, and a sheet is a piece of paper in a tray and it has been a piece of paper in a tray since before either of us was born."

Elias Venn did not say anything for about four seconds.

"That is a better answer than I had, and it is the only one there is, and you have just told me a thing that would let a certificate like this one through a tribunal in about nine minutes," he said. "Have you written it down anywhere?"

"No," said Bettina Haile. "**And I am not going to start, and you are not going to ask me to, because the moment it is written down somebody can rely on it and the day somebody can rely on it a ward in this city that was shut for a fortnight for want of water becomes a ward that was shut for a fortnight for want of water on a certificate.** And that is the last useful thing about this office and I am the registrar-counsel and I have decided to keep it."

She picked the folder up. "And the rest of it is this. **Four thousand one hundred and six of those in the last twelve months.** Every one signed by a registrar-counsel who has not examined anybody, about a treatment the registrar-counsel did not give, for a patient the registrar-counsel did not see. That is not a confession and it is not a defence, it is a job description, and I am good at my job, and if you want the job changed you want a different office and there is a form for that and the form is worse than this one."

---

There was one more thing and he had to ask for it, and asking was the whole cost of the morning.

"The material," said Ruth Callow. "The compiled material for a certificate is the claim form, and a claim form is made out in two, and the top copy is the claimant's and the claimant's is at the premises on Wexford Row, and the one in this building is the office copy, and the office copy is the sheet behind the carbon with the number on the front of it. **Two copies of a form and one number, and the number is on the front of the top copy and the office copy has not had a number on it in its life, and neither of the two is the certificate.**"

"May I see it?"

"You may see it here and you may not have it and you may not photograph it, and I will not ask you why you asked, because everybody who asks says the same three reasons and none of them are true." She opened a box on a shelf and took out a folder and did not give it to him. "It comes out on the table and it stays on the table and I turn the pages and you read across the table."

He read across the table.

It was a form with a header and four lines and a service code and a fee and a patient schedule box, and the patient schedule box had **Thirlmere 4** in it, and the code had **D-2** in it, and the fee had **60.00** in it, and the date had the eleventh of September in it.

And in the same box, on the office copy, in the same hand, there was a second impression of the same three words, much lighter, that stopped after the second of them and did not finish.

Elias Venn did not touch it. He looked at it for a while and then said, "There are two of them."

"There are two of them," said Ruth Callow, "and there are about four hundred thousand of those in this building, and about four a day come in light, because a clerk writes a line and does not like it and writes it again, and the carbon takes both."

"That is what I thought."

"You did not think anything, sir, you have been in the building twenty minutes." But she said it without unkindness, and she did not close the box, and she did not ask him to write it down, and that was the kindness.

"May I write it out," said Elias Venn. "Not the form. The four lines of it, and the two boxes, and the two impressions, and no number anywhere on the page."

"You may write out a thing that is not a copy." Ruth Callow put her thumb on the edge of the folder. "A copy is a second piece of paper with the same words on it in the same order, and a copy can be put in front of somebody who was not in the room. That is not what I am offering you, and I want the difference in your own book in your own words, because you are the sort of man who is going to be asked about it in about four years. **And I will not turn the pages while you are writing, because a woman turning pages is a woman choosing what a man sees, and if I choose then the page in your book is mine and not yours.** You have got until half past ten."

He wrote it out in his own hand on a page of his own in about eleven minutes: the heading, the four lines, the patient schedule box, the procedure box, the fee box, the date of service, and under the patient schedule box two sentences, **a second impression of three words, lighter, stopping after the second of them.** He left a gap for the claim number the width of the line and wrote nothing in it, and a gap for the certificate number and wrote nothing in that one either, and he did not copy the countersignature at the foot, because he had worked out in a car park in November that a name and a number in one notebook is a file, and a file is a thing he is not.

The page in the book is a fair copy and it is in his own hand and it has no number in it, and it is in a repair shop on a shelf with three other documents he has no right to hold.

---

He telephoned Saint Orra Reach at twenty past eleven from the call box on the corner of Harrow Lane because the district office does not have a public telephone and the man on the corner has one and does not mind.

It took two minutes and Mara Quill answered, and it was the fourth time they had spoken in six weeks, and every one of the four had been about four minutes long.

"There is a lighter impression of the same three words on a carbon, and it stops after two," said Elias Venn, "and I know what I want to ask and I am going to ask it badly and you are going to tell me I am wrong, and then I am going to do what you tell me."

"Go on."

"Tell me it is not a false start."

"No," said Mara Quill. "It is not. **It is a woman who wrote a line and did not like it.** It happens about four times a day in this city and in about nine offices in this city it happens eleven times a day, and it is a fact about pens, and it is not a hole with a lip on it."

"You have said that to me before."

"I said the other half of it to you in a doorway on the twenty-sixth of November, and the reason I said it in a doorway is that I have said the first half four times this year and I am not going to be the woman who finds things on paper that she can only find on paper, because in about two years somebody is going to take my instruments to a hearing and say *the witness at the doorway read a form.*" There was a pause on the line. "Put it in your book as a light impression and a heavy one. Not in any of the other words. If you put it in in any of the other words, then in four years you will have a book with a thing in it that I have told you does not exist, and that is the only way anybody ever gets into a book of mine."

"Right."

"Are you sleeping?"

"I am in a call box at the end of a corridor in a building with a ward in it, and there are eleven beds down that corridor and I have not been inside one of them. Four hours' sleep."

"That is not sleeping." She did not say anything else for about three seconds, and neither did he, and the call box on the corner of Harrow Lane had a light in it that had been out for a week. "Then I am going to say the thing I am going to say, and then we are not going to talk about it, and I am not writing it down either, so do not tell a woman on a ward that I said it."

"I would not."

"You are on a ward." A pause. "Of course you are. Of course you are. Then say it to a nurse and not to a patient, and do not say my name in a building with a bed in it, and Mr Venn — you have got four hours and you have got a bench, and the bench is the only thing in this case that has never cost anybody anything."

He put the receiver on the hook and the light went out in the box about a second after the money ran out, and he stood on the corner of Harrow Lane for about a minute in the rain.

Then he got the bus and went to Marshall Street and did not telephone anybody and did not go to Wexford Row, and at four o'clock in the afternoon he put a sheet of A4 on the front bench of a repair shop and wrote four lines on it in his own bad hand. It was not a copy of anything: it was the four things he had actually been told, which were the office that issued it, the day it issued, who issued it for, and what it said. Underneath he wrote one line more, and the line said that the certificate number was in his head and was going to stay there.

Then he put the sheet in the back of the notebook, on top of a print of three counterfoils and a page with four things on it that he had no right to hold and that had been in the book since September and had nothing to do with any of this, and he put the notebook back on the shelf above the bench and stood with his hand flat on it for a moment longer than he had meant to.

Because in about four years the only proof that a registrar-counsel had refused him five times will be a nineteen-year-old clerk on a tray in a building on Harrow Lane who will have retired by then, and a record of issue with a hole punched out of it in his own hand. **That is not a document. It is a man's account of a document.** It is the only kind he has ever been allowed to make, and he will have to make it again on Monday, and on Wednesday, and in January, and there is nobody in this city he can give it to.
