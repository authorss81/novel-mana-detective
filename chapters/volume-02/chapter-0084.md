# Chapter 0084 — Back on the Book

The Aldergate Wing is a municipal fever hospital of 1911 on Wexford Row with twenty-eight beds and four wards, and at seven o'clock on a Monday morning in January there are twenty-eight beds and twenty-seven people to look after them, and the corridor at that hour is the only corridor in the city where nobody is hurrying, because everybody in it has been awake since half past five and has somewhere to be at eight.

He came in by the main doors at ten past seven. The second lift was locked until eleven for the linen run, and there is a sign on it that says LIFT 2 — LINEN 10:00–11:00 that was put there in about 1998 and has been repainted four times.

"Saturday's easier," said Winnie Arkwright, coming past him with a cart. "I can tell you that as somebody who has been doing this run for twenty-two years. There is nobody in this corridor at seven in the morning and I can stack the whole thing and be out by nine."

"You do the run on a Monday as well."

"I do the run on a Monday and I have done it since 2008 and I will do it until they put me in a chair." She had the cart at the wall and was not unloading it. "Are you the one that was in the corridor last month with a sheet of paper?"

"I am."

"Mrs Okafor's sheet. Yes." She looked at him properly for the first time, and there was nothing in her face at all, which is not a thing people expect. "You are not going to ask me what is in the trolley."

"I was not going to."

"Good, because I have been asked twice this year and both times it was somebody with a reason." She turned the cart about six inches. "It is sheets and it is gowns and it is about four people's laundry and it is a label off a man's left boot that I have been carrying since November because there is a bin store in this building that nobody has opened since the summer, and I have thought about the boot a great deal, and I have decided that a boot is a bin store and a bin store is a boot and I am not going to worry about it any more."

---

Sister Ivy Warhurst came into the corridor at ten to eight with a book under her arm and she saw him from about thirty feet and stopped, and came the rest of the way, and did not pretend.

"You are not here about the book."

"I am not here about the book."

"Then you are here about the corridor, and there is a bench in it, and I have been on a book since Monday and I am about eleven minutes late for my own handover because I stood outside the dispensary looking at the sky." She was sixty-one and had twenty-six years in and she had a folded cardigan over her arm. "Sit down. You are not to touch anything in this corridor and I am saying that as a courtesy, not as a rule, because if it was a rule you would have asked me what the rule was and I would have told you and we would both have been embarrassed."

He sat down.

"I am not going to be a route into anything," she said. "You have not asked me and I am telling you in advance so that you do not spend the morning working out how to ask. A ward book is a ward, a bed and a date. That is what it has been since before I came and that is what it is going to be when I am gone. If a person is in a bed, they are in my book. If a person is in a room with a door that shuts and a practitioner in it, they are not in my book, they are in that practitioner's book, and that book is not a ward book and it has never been a ward book and it is not a copy of one." She sat down on the bench, which took about four seconds longer than it should have. "A man who goes into a first-floor room is not in this building. He never was. There is no line on this book for what a room was for, and there has not been one since about 1974, and in 1974 somebody had a fight about it in an office and the ward won and nobody has gone back to it."

"Who is on the book this morning."

"Twenty-seven people," said Sister Warhurst, "and if I tell you that I will be telling you something that I have no authority to tell a member of the public, and you are one, and I have known that since December when you stood in that corridor for forty minutes not asking me anything."

---

He had been in a dispensary back room on Marshall Street in December with four people in it, in a corridor at the end of a session list with a nurse and two patients and a porter in it, and in a room on Sallow Lane in November with six people in it and a child of nine on the floor with a book about a pony in it, and the third of those had ended with a man in a doorway and a working that did not finish.

This was a hospital corridor at ten past eight on a Monday morning, and there was a woman with a cart forty feet away, and a nurse coming towards them with a clipboard, and two porters at the end of it by the linen room door.

He stood up and did it in his own words, in the middle of the corridor, pitched to carry about nine feet and no further, which is the volume a man uses when he wants a record and does not want an audience.

"I want to say a thing in this corridor and I want it said where the four of you can hear it and I want it said again in a book tonight, because I have said it in three rooms in this city since September and I have been told in this building that it is being repeated." He put his hands where they could be seen. "I do not read people. I have never read a person in my life and I am not going to start this year. There is a thing I do that works on objects and on paper and it has cost me a trade, a case and four samples since September, and if anybody in this building has been worried that I put a hand on them, or on their card, or on their name, then I have not, and I am not going to, and there is a woman in this city who has known that since September and who told a sergeant the same thing in a corridor in November before I had thought of saying it myself."

The nurse said, "I know. She said it in the clinic meeting in October and she was quite emphatic about it."

"Then you have heard it three times and I would rather you had heard it four from me than three." He did not look at Winnie Arkwright, and that was deliberate, and she noticed, and he knew she noticed, and he let it stand. "The reason I am allowed to stand in this corridor is that I am not a clinician and I am not a police officer and I was formally reprimanded in November for touching a thing I was not entitled to touch in a room with a man in it, and that is the whole of my qualification and there is no other one."

Winnie Arkwright had the cart at the wall and her hand on it.

"Nobody has ever asked me that," she said. "I have been doing that run for twenty-two years and in twenty-two years I have been asked whether I mind the lift, whether I mind the stairs, whether I would rather do the trolley or the sacks, and I have been asked once whether I was a friend of the patient, which I was not, and I have never once been asked whether I could see something." She pushed the cart two feet towards the lift. "So you have got that from me, and I have no idea what to do with it, and I am going to be thinking about it at the other end of this corridor."

---

He got the lift and went down four floors and found a corridor outside a room with a light on under the door, which is a thing that has happened to him before, in a different building, on a different morning, and he stood outside it for about a minute and then went and stood somewhere else.

Inspector Nadia Kest had come out of a stairwell two floors down and was carrying two coffees and gave him one of them.

"You have been in the building since ten past seven."

"I have."

"Warhurst?"

"I have."

"And you have not asked her for the book and she has not offered it, and she came back to the book on Monday, and she is standing in that corridor right now being extremely pleased about it in a way she will deny by Wednesday." Nadia Kest drank about a third of hers in one go, which she did when she was already behind. "How is the first of January to you."

"Nineteen days on the twenty-sixth of December. I did the arithmetic on the train and I have not written it down because I do not want to be the sort of man who has a figure in a notebook." He looked at the coffee. "I have four minutes and I have a question and it is a public question and it is not about a person."

"Go."

"A claim can be amended before it is settled. Not after."

"Yes."

"And when it is amended, to a service code, who gets told."

Nadia Kest put the cup on the sill of a window and did not pick it up again.

"The account and the issuing office," she said. "The patient is not a party to the claim. A patient is a participant in a claim and not a party to it, which is a thing the claimant structure has been since the nineteen-sixties and which nobody has ever had to defend because nobody has ever cared. So the correction goes to the account and it goes to the office that issued whatever the claim was turned into, and it does not go to the patient, because the patient cannot receive it, there is nowhere to send it, and in about half the cases in this city the patient has not got an address." She said all of it in about ninety words, in a corridor, at half past eight on a Monday morning, to a member of the public. "And a patient who has been given a copy of a claim holds a copy of the claim as it stood when it was sent to her, which is a true document, and it is a copy of what was true that day, and it stays true for the rest of her life. **And there is no mechanism in this city by which anybody is told that the thing they are holding has been changed.** There is no form. I have looked. I looked for about nine minutes, because a man of thirty-six asked me a general question in a corridor and then went away, and I did not find one, and I have been in this job for twenty-two years and I have never had to look for that before."

"That is the whole of what I came for."

"That is the whole of what I am permitted to give you, and I want to be exact about the shape of it." She picked the coffee up again. "I am telling you a rule because rules are public and I am not permitted to tell you one single thing about your brother, or hers, or mine, and I have not looked at a file about this, and if you tell anybody in this building that I said that, I will say you made it up, and both of us will know whether I said it."

"I am not going to tell anybody."

"You put four sheets on a wall in a waiting room on Marshall Street at the end of November and about sixty people have read them since, and one of them rang this desk about it inside a week, and that was a professional courtesy and I am not going to discuss it." She looked at him for a second. "Elias. It is Monday. I am not going to ask you about anything."

"I know."

"I am telling you that I am not going to ask you about anything, so that you do not have to spend the next four minutes deciding whether I am going to."

---

They went out into the yard at half past nine because the yard is the only place in that building where two people can have a conversation that a nurse cannot hear, and it was cold, and there were two cars in it and a skip and a bench nobody sits on.

"You have got a face," said Nadia Kest, "and I have known that face since October, and the thing about it this time is that it is not the face of a man who has been told no."

"No," said Elias Venn. "It is the face of a man who has been told the rule and has worked out that the rule is the crime."

"Your word, not mine."

"I have had six years and I have had a rule and I have not had a document, and I am not going to say that to a police officer in a yard at half past nine on a Monday morning, and I have not said it, and you did not hear it." He put his hands in his coat. "What I am going to ask you is a date and it is the only thing I am going to ask you all morning."

"Ask me."

"Twenty-two November, a Thursday, you served a document on eleven addresses in this district and one in the outer wards, and it runs to the fourteenth of January, and that is seven days, and on Monday morning a clerk in a municipal building is going to put a word in a box on a form, and on Tuesday morning it is not there, and there is nothing in this city after Tuesday morning that puts a police officer in any of those eleven rooms."

"That is correct."

"And in November a working was run in a first-floor room in Sallow Lane and it did not finish, and what finished it was that document and two officers and a shoulder on a door, and there was a girl of nine on the floor of that room with a book, and nobody in that room had asked for a police officer and there was no police officer in that room, and after Tuesday morning all of that is a thing that happened in a room with no instrument attached to it."

Nadia Kest put her coffee down on the bench nobody sits on.

"Say the last part again."

"I am not saying it again. I said it once and you heard it and I have not written it down." He looked at the skip. "I am telling you that I know what is about to happen and I have known since November and I have deliberately not written one word about it, and the two possible words are on a form in a drawer in a municipal building and I have never told anybody what they are, and if you ask me what they are I will tell you that I do not know, and that will be the truth, because a man who has guessed twice in a row and been right once is a man who has a habit."

Nadia Kest looked at him for a long moment in a yard in the cold.

"Do not guess," she said. "Not this time. You have a very good instinct and it has been wrong in front of a room once already and there is a woman of sixty-one who went four nights without a drug because of it, and I have read that minute, because I read all of them, and it is in a file and it is in your own handwriting, and there is not one word of it that I would change." She picked her coffee up. "Seven days. And when it runs out, it does not renew by itself, and nobody rings anybody, because the only person who can ring anybody is the woman whose registration number is on it, and she will be asked, and she will be asked by a clerk, and a clerk will be told to write a word in a box, and I have been in this service long enough to know that the clerk will be frightened and will put the pen down."

At eight o'clock Sister Warhurst had gone into the nurses' station and had taken a patient's name off a list and had written it into the ward book in her own hand for the first time since before Christmas, and at a quarter to ten Petronella Osei at reception told a man in a good coat that there was no need to wait at the desk, and at eleven the second lift was unlocked and Winnie Arkwright took forty-one beds' worth of linen up it in nine trips without once being asked whether she minded.

She did not mind the lift. She had said so twice.

He was in the corridor at half past eleven with his coat on when Sister Warhurst came out of the nurses' station with a book under her arm, and it was a book he had not seen her put down since the third of December, and he did not ask her about it, and she looked at him for about a second and said, without being asked anything, "No. I am not going to tell you whether it is the same book. Ask me in a year, and ask me in a room with nobody in it, and do not write this down."
