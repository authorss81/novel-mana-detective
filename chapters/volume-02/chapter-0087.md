# Chapter 0087 — A Line With No Box In It

The first floor of 4 Corrie Street is eight rooms and a landing with a window at the end, and under the window is the dry cleaner's, and the bell on the corridor wall is the dry cleaner's bell on a string, and it goes off in the shop when anybody upstairs pulls it.

Four doors away from the back room on the left, on the other side of the landing, there is a door with a plate on it that is not a name, and no letterbox, and the door is shut, and there is no light behind the frosted glass in it, because the woman who works in there does her work on a Friday and this is a Thursday. Elias Venn did not knock on it and did not go near the string, because a woman in a laboratory on Saturday morning had given him a fact about that bell in the middle of an argument about something else, and he had not asked her for it, and she had given it to him anyway, and he had thought about it on two buses and a walk.

At the far end of the landing there is a door with a bell on it, a small brass one above a letterbox with a wooden flap, and the flap is up, and there is no plate. It is the fourth door from hers counting both sides. He knocked twice, because a woman at a desk in Tanners Row had said you knock twice, and it had been the only piece of guidance anybody had given him in nine days.

The door opened about two inches and a man's eye came to it.

"Yes."

"I am not calling about anything on the board. I am not from the council, I am not from the police and I am not a doctor. I am a member of the public, I would like to ask you one general question, and I would like the door shut behind me and opened again afterwards, and I am telling you both of those now so that you do not have to decide them while I am standing on a landing."

The eye went away for about a second and came back.

"Which one are you."

"Which one of what."

"Which door. There are eight on this floor and one of them has been shut since about Tuesday, and there is a man at the other end of this landing who will open a door to anybody who says the word *boiler*, and I have had him out here in his dressing gown at eleven at night in December." The eye went down an inch. "You did not say boiler."

"That man asked me on Wednesday whether I was the agent and I was not, and he has been waiting for the agent since November because the meters are on the supply of a shop that shut in October, and he has written four letters and pays for the stamps."

"Right," said the man, and opened the door.

---

Eamon Brack was thirty-nine and had been in the room six years, and there was a bench along one wall, a table with two chairs, a gas fire that was off, and a couch against the far wall. On the table was a green hardback book with a rubber band round it. On the shelf above it was a cardboard box with about forty small reels in it, and every reel had a paper band round the middle with a date on one side and a fee on the other.

"Shut it," he said. "Not because of anything. Because I have been asked about that corridor twice in six years and both of those times it was because the door was open, and I am not interested in being a room with an open door."

He shut it, put forward the chair that was not the chair with its back to the door, offered nothing else, asked no question at all about what the question was going to be, and after about a minute said, "You have not asked it."

"You have not said that I could."

"No. That was on purpose and I am not apologising for it. Go on, then."

Elias Venn asked a general question.

"There is a practice in this city where a procedure a person has had done is used afterwards, in other rooms, on other people, and I am not asking you whether you do that. I am asking what a practitioner is given to work from, generally, and you may refuse and tell me so and I shall go downstairs."

Eamon Brack looked at the shelf for a while.

"A person," he said, "and that is not the one anybody expects. Not a plate, not a book, not a record. A person who has had it done properly, in a room, by somebody who knew the method, and who was told the truth about it, and who believes it. A record has no person in it. A person believes. And a person who has been through it will say the same thing about the same room for the rest of his life, and it takes nine minutes for eleven people in a waiting room to agree with him." He took the band off the green book and did not open it. "That is a source. And there is no word for it on any form in this room, and I did not know that until you asked me, and I have known what I was doing for six years."

---

He put his hand flat on the table, not on the book. "There are about forty of them in that box. What is written on them is a date and a fee and nothing else. No name, because I do not write names. No code, because the code is on the green book under the date, and the green book has about two hundred and forty sessions in it in six years with a date and a code and a fee and the number of hours."

"And the forty."

"About half are mine. The rest came from four doors down that hall, off the woman who works there, in a carrier bag on a Friday in about 2027, and she said they were the ones she had not thrown out. I have never asked her one question about any of them and she has never asked me what I do with them, and that is the exact shape of the arrangement between us and I have never once liked it."

"Did you look at them."

"I have listened to four and I have used nine." He put the band back on the book. "The number I have is nine. The number I think is eleven. Both are honest and I am not giving you the second as though it were the first."

"Nine times in six years, and about two hundred and forty sessions, and never to anybody who did not ask you for it."

"Never once. And I would like that said in that order, because the order is the whole of what I am."

---

Elias Venn did not write it down. The notebook was in his coat and he did not take it out, and the man across the table watched him not take it out, and neither of them said anything about it.

"How does it work," he said. "Not why. I am not asking why and I will not ask why. How."

"You take a sitting that went well and you play it. You do not put it into somebody who has never been in a room, because a person who has never been in a room knows it, and it comes out like a story they were told." He turned the reel nearest the front of the box a quarter of a turn and put it back. "You put it into somebody who has been in a room. Somebody who has had it done and thinks it did not take. And I did not know that I had looked until this week, and I have looked, and there is no form in this city that says a word about any of it."

"And the paper."

"The paper is the part that is going to make you angry and it is not going to make me angry, because I have had six years with it." He took the band off the book, turned to a page about a third of the way in, laid it on the table with the spine towards Elias Venn so that it could be read upside down, and did not let go of it. "There is a sheet, from a printer in this district, four hundred at a time, and I have never known what I pay for them. Five boxes on the top half and a tear-off line, and I fill in all five. And then under the fifth box, on the same sheet, there is a line of type, and there is nothing printed at the end of the line, and it is not a box, and it has no heading, and it does not stop at the edge of the paper, and it is in the same place on every sheet I have ever had."

"What is on the line."

"Nothing. It is a ruled line with a printed line of type above it and it is about a third of an inch high." Eamon Brack turned the book about ten degrees. "And I have written in it nine times, and every one of the nine times I have written *with consent of the patient*."

He let go of the book.

"That is the whole of what I did and I have never had a moment about it, and I will tell you where the words came from. They came off a strip. A patient hands you the bottom of the sheet and you read it, and the patient has signed a printed sentence saying they have been told what the work is and have had the chance to ask questions. **Consent. Consent of the patient. It is the same word.** So I wrote it in the only place there was, and I did not ask, and I have thought about it every day for six years and I have not stopped, and those are both true."

Elias Venn looked at the page upside down for about four seconds.

"What is the other half of it. There is always another half."

"There is not," said Eamon Brack, and shut the book. "Nine entries, a box with forty things in it, two hundred and forty sessions, and no second half of that sentence anywhere on the page. If you want to know why there is no second half then it is in a form, or in a law, or in a person's head, and it is not in here, and I have been the one writing in here for six years."

---

"What did you not ask."

"I did not fail to ask them a question," said Eamon Brack, without any drama at all. "I asked them out loud in the room and they said yes, every time, in words, and then there was nowhere on the paper to write it, so I did not write it, and so the next time there was nothing to read."

"Who said yes."

"Four of them are mine and I know the four." He looked at the shelf. "The other five came out of a carrier bag off a woman four doors down that hall. I have met about nine of the people they are, because they come to the same building and I have made them tea. I do not know the names of the five, because I was not asking them about the reel."

"Then who is on the one that is dated the twelfth of October."

"I have no idea," said Eamon Brack. "There is no name on it. I have never known a name for any of the five and I have never wanted one, and I would like that noticed, because I know exactly how that sounds."

He looked at Elias Venn's coat, where nothing was showing in any pocket.

"You have not written one word down."

"No."

"Then I am going to say the last thing." He put the box back on the shelf with the open end facing out, because that is how he kept it. "There is a woman in the room at the end now. She has been four times, she pays in cash, she is not on my book for today, and I have never had a name off her. I am telling you because on Monday that document stops having anything behind it, and I would like one person in this city to have said a sentence out loud before it happens."

"What sentence."

"That I have been told about it, and I have written it in my own book, and I have taken it up with a man who came up four flights on a Thursday and did not telephone me." He stood up. "I am not going to be charged for anything. I have known that for about four years. I have not been sorry, and I am not going to pretend to you this afternoon that I have been, because you would be able to tell, and because it would make the two of us feel better and it would be false."

---

Elias Venn stood in a first-floor room in Corrie Street and did not say anything for about a minute. "One more question and it is not a general one, and then I am going, and I am not coming back to this floor this week."

"Ask."

"If it is somebody's mother on a Tuesday, or somebody's husband, or a woman of thirty-eight who has been four times, would you do it again."

Eamon Brack took his hand off the back of the chair.

"No," he said. "And I have not stopped. Those are both true, and in six years I have never been able to make the two of them fit together and I have got a book that they do not fit in."

Elias Venn knocked once on the door frame on the way out, which was the only knock he made on that floor, and he had not planned it.

---

"Elias," said a woman, "I have been here since Friday and you have been extremely stupid about a corridor, and come and let me in."

She was on the landing with her coat on, and she had come up four flights rather than telephone, and the fire door at the top of the stair was propped with a wedge that somebody in that hospital has been carrying around illegally since about 1988.

"You told me not to come."

"I did not tell you not to come. I told you I would find out, and I have thought about it every day since, and I came up four flights rather than telephone you, and I would like you to understand which of us that makes worse and I am not going to help you." She shut the fire door behind her and the wedge made a small noise. "I went to her."

"You went to her."

"Wednesday morning, about eleven, in her own room, on a Thursday morning when she is not there, because it is not a Friday. He told me in about four minutes what you have spent nine days and two buses and a pawnshop finding out, and I did not tell you one word of it, and I have not slept properly since Wednesday, and I am telling you now because I have decided to and not because you worked it out."

"You set a condition about that on a staircase in November."

"I set four conditions on a staircase in November and the second was that neither of us does this alone, and I have broken it on purpose, and I have come up four flights to tell you that I have broken it and that I did not have to. Because if I had waited for you we would have gone together, and then it would have been your finding, and I could not stand four more days of it being yours."

---

"You are a practitioner," he said. "I am asking you as one and not as anything else, and if you answer me as anything else I shall go downstairs."

She thought about it for longer than he expected.

"It is possible," said Mara Quill. "A person who has had a grounding and a name and a date put in them properly, in a room, by somebody who knew the method, and who was told the truth and believed it, is the best source there is. Better than a record, because a record has no person in it. That is not a discovery. It is in a module that costs three credits and is sold on a rack in the same building as the tariff."

"And the patient."

"The patient is the source. The patient is not the victim. And that is the part you have had in the wrong place for six years and so have I, and the right place is this: the person in the room is the only person in the whole arrangement who gets nothing, and the only thing anybody ever took from them is a piece of paper that says they agreed to it, which is the one thing they did do, and it is in a drawer behind a linen office and it is about six inches by two."

She was not crying. He had noticed in November that she did not cry and had written it down and had never told her he had noticed.

"Then we agree," he said, "and I have wasted a week."

"No," said Mara Quill. "That is where we are going to be for about a year, so I am going to say it properly while we are both standing on this landing and neither of us can go anywhere."

---

"A person consents to the work, and not to the use," she said. "That has been my sentence since I was nineteen and it is correct, and I have taught two people who do not now speak to each other. The work was done to him. He walked out of that room able to say a name and a date he could not say on the Wednesday. He has got a true piece of paper. He was not injured and he was not worked on without his knowledge. He was a customer, and he got what he paid eleven credits for, and if you ask me what happened to him afterwards I will tell you that I do not know."

"That is one half."

"That is the half I am the practitioner for. And the other half is yours, and you have had it since Friday morning and have not said it out loud, so I am going to say it for you, and then I am going to go down and put the kettle on, and we are both going to be extremely tired."

"Go on."

"A person who consents to a small true thing has not consented to a large false one. And there is no sentence anybody can sign that means *I agree to this and not to what it is for*, because the second half is not a box, and it is not a line, and it is not on any form in this city." She said it quietly. "And that is not a gap in the law. **That is what a sentence is.** A sentence is the smallest thing you can agree to, and a sentence that cannot say what it is for is a sentence somebody else can finish later, in a different room, on a different day. That is the whole of the trick, and it is the trick the certificate does, and it is the trick he signed, and he signed it because he was told the truth about an hour and a name and a date, and all three were true, and I would have signed it."

"That is the sentence that lets every room in this city do what it likes."

"That," said Mara Quill, "is the sentence that keeps a room from being a prison. And you have been holding it since Friday and I have been holding the other one since I was nineteen, and neither of us is going to put it down, and I am not going to pretend that the person who has spent six years waiting for a brother has the better of it, because it is not about having. I have the laboratory and the four instruments. You have the shop. Neither of those is a thing you get to weigh."

---

She took the notebook out of her coat. It had been signed at the front by both of them in a laboratory in November, torn on a fold, and lodged nowhere.

"Line three," she said. "The corrected one. *Who is doing it, who is watching, and if it cannot be known in advance then who finds out, and in what order.*"

"That is the line that would have caught this."

"That is the line that would have caught it in four seconds on a Thursday in October three years ago, because a claim amended after a patient has gone home is precisely a thing that cannot be known in advance. The line asks who finds out, and the answer is nobody, because the patient is not a party to the claim. It asks in what order, and the answer is that there is no order." She put the notebook back. "And I will not publish it, and I am telling you why now so that you do not have to be polite about asking."

"Tell me."

"It is eleven words. It is the best sentence either of us has ever written and it is eleven words, and anybody who reads it is a person who can carry it, and it does not stop anybody. It makes a good paper." She did not raise her voice. "I have been wrong about paper twice and I am not doing it a third time with the only thing I have got. I will publish it on the day there is something on the other end of it, and you are going to hate that, and you are not going to win it, and I would rather you went down and made the tea than stood up here and tried."

He stood on the landing next to a woman who had come up four flights to tell him she had broken a rule she had set herself, with a light on behind the frosted glass of a door he had not knocked on, and he did not try, and it was the hardest thing he did that week.

She took the biro out of the cuff of her coat and turned the back of her own hand up and wrote on it in numerals how many weeks it had been.

"Three hundred and twenty-five," she said, and showed him. "Because I could not hold it, and because if it is on my hand and somebody sees it in about a year then it is a fact with a date on it instead of a feeling with a day on it. And I learnt that from a man who writes everything down and I have never once told him so."

She put the biro away.

"Now I am going to put the kettle on, and then you are going to tell me exactly what that man said in that room in the words he used, and I am going to write them down, because you will get them wrong inside a week, and because you have been wrong out loud in front of a patient once this year already and I am not going to hear about the second one from somebody else."
