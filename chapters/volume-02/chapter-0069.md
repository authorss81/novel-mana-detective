# Chapter 0069 — What He Is Not Going to Publish

Sana Okafor's car was in the yard behind the clinic on Marshall Street and the gates were shut, and she was standing by it with her coat on and her bag over her shoulder at twenty past twelve on Tuesday the eleventh of December, which was twenty minutes before her afternoon list, which was not a thing she did.

"You have got until twenty to one and then I have got a list."

"I have got the certificate."

"You have got a record of issue with no patient in it, and a fair copy of a claim form in your own hand with the number left off it, and two documents an insurance company gave you out of its own file, and you have not got the certificate, and the certificate is in a drawer in a building on the Verge Row side and the woman who has the drawer has told you she cannot see round the edge of it. So what you have got is a shape, and shapes are what got you into this in November, and I would like it on the record that I said *shape* before you did."

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"Get in the car," said Sana Okafor. "Not because I am going to drive you anywhere. Because it is raining and I have got the heater on and because the last time we had this conversation it was standing up in treatment room two and I have been on my feet since seven and I am forty-seven and there is a bench in this car."

---

She drove out of the yard and stopped in a side street, because the clinic car park is not a place to have a conversation, and that was a decision she made without discussing and without asking.

"Say it."

"The certificate is a public certified record," said Elias Venn. "It says a treatment was administered in bed four of a ward on the eleventh of September, and the ward was shut for eight days, and the treatment was given in a first-floor room in Corrie Street by a woman who is fifty-eight years old and has been doing it for six years for eleven credits, and the claim was settled and the money moved, and the counterfoil that says eleven is the only piece of paper in the world that says what actually happened, and it is in a dry cleaner's front room in a biscuit tin.

"And I want to put it on your board."

"No," said Sana Okafor.

"I know."

"You do not know, because you have not said the rest of it, so say the rest of it, because I have given you about four seconds of a decision and I would like to know whether you are the man who made a decision or the man who made a speech."

Elias Venn put his hands flat on his knees.

"A certified record that is false in a register has to be challenged in public," he said, "or it is a register. That is the whole of my half and I have had it in my head for two days and it is a good half and I am not giving it up because it is inconvenient. If that certificate stays in a drawer then in about four years somebody will take it out and refuse a woman a claim on the strength of a bed in a shut ward, and it will take them four minutes, and nobody will ever find out, and the person it is done to will be told that the treatment did not happen and she will be right and she will have no document in her hand."

"That is a good half. Before you say the other one I am going to tell you that I know something you have not told me, and I am going to tell you how I know it, and then I am going to ask you for it, and you may say no."

"You know something."

"I know that you were in a hospital on Saturday morning, because Ahmed at the clinic saw a man of your build go past the dispensary window at about half past seven and he said *the marshal*, and I said *leave it*, and he left it. And I know you have not slept properly since the twenty-seventh of November because you came to the dispensary on the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth and on the second of December to buy a comb, and nobody buys a comb three times in a fortnight. And I know you have had something in your head since Monday because you telephoned me at eleven o'clock on Sunday night and asked me whether a patient can be told something about her own care by a stranger who is not a doctor. I said no. You said thank you and hung up."

"Yes," said Elias Venn.

"Then give me the thing you telephoned about, and not the version with the certificate in it. The patient. What did they say."

"You have not been to a registry in your life."

"I have been to a registry in my life and I did not enjoy it, and I have been to eleven offices in this city about a woman's hip and I can tell you the number of the ones that made it worse." She turned the heater down. "Go on."

---

"Your half is a patient," said Elias Venn.

"My half is a patient," said Sana Okafor. "**The certificate does not name a patient. That is the entire reason it is dangerous and it is the entire reason you cannot use it. It names a bed, and the bed is a shut ward, and there is no person in it, and that means there is nobody in this city who can be harmed by it today — and there is a woman of eighty-one in a day room on Wexford Row who went out for an hour on the eleventh of September and paid sixty credits and does not know to this day what was done to her, and she is not in the certificate, and if the certificate goes public it will be public with her bed in it inside two weeks, because somebody in an office will have to go and find out who bed four was, and they will find her, and she is eighty-one, and she has a hip, and somebody will ask her.**"

"They would ask her about a treatment."

"They would ask her about the eleventh of September," said Sana Okafor. "Which is the week the water was off, and which she spent in a day room with a fire bucket and a television, and which she has probably told about four people, and one of those four people will be an assessor and three of them will be in a corridor. **You cannot un-ask a question. That is the whole of my half and I have had it since you sat in that car and I have been sick about it since you sat in that car.**"

"And my half."

"Your half is a floor under a door in four years' time and it is a good floor and I am not saying it is a bad one." She shifted in the seat. "And here is the part you have not thought about, and I have thought about it, because I am a doctor, and because I have been in this building since I was twenty-eight, and because in 2013 a woman who did eleven-hour days in a kitchen in Halloway Street went to a practitioner, and her employer found out inside a fortnight, and I did not find out about it for a year, and I have carried it since.

"**The protection of a person like Rhoda Nesbit is that nobody has written anything down about her.** Not the paper. The *paper*. The moment the certificate is public, then a certificate is a thing that can be wrong, and the first thing anybody does — not this week, not this month, in about two years, in a corridor, in a room like the one you sat in on Harrow Lane — is to go back to the small work. To the eleven-credit Fridays. To the woman in the first-floor room. And they will not go after the certificate. **There is no certificate office. There is a first-floor room over a dry cleaner with a bell on the door, and a certificate cannot be served on a bell.**"

Elias Venn looked out of the windscreen at a wall about four feet away.

"And the fourteen of January," he said.

"And the fourteenth of January," said Sana Okafor, "which is in a drawer with a doctor's registration number under it and which somebody in a municipal building is going to write a word on, and that word is going to be *extended* or the word is going to be *discharged*, and if it is *discharged* then in that week a woman in Corrie Street has no order in front of her and nothing at all in this city, and on the Friday of that week somebody is going to look for somebody to blame, and there is exactly one address that anybody in this district knows for it, and it is hers, and it is not yours, and it is not mine."

"It is not on the certificate."

"It is not on the certificate," said Sana Okafor, "and it will be on the certificate in about four years, because I have read the small work for nineteen years and the certificate always comes second. **The paper comes first and the certificate comes afterwards, and the certificate is always right, and that is why the paper has to be protected for as long as there is a person underneath it.**"

---

He sat in a car in a side street off Marshall Street and found that he had lost.

He had the best half. That was not a matter of opinion; it was the half that four hundred people had a certificate about and a woman was going to be turned away on, and it was right, and it was the reason he had built a book with a private documented copy of a working in it, and it was in his hands on a Tuesday lunchtime in December, and it was not going to cost anything and it was the only time in four months that anything had not.

"Who is it," said Sana Okafor. "The patient. You have not said a name once since Sunday and I have watched you not say one, and I want to know whether you have one and whether you are not saying it or whether you do not have one."

"I have one and I am not saying it."

"That is the correct answer and I want you to notice that it is the correct answer, and that four weeks ago you would have said the name, and that a man who has not said a name for two days is a man who has worked out something about names in two days that he had not worked out in four months, and I am not going to ask you what it is, because if I ask, then I know, and then I am a doctor with a patient's bed in her head and I have put it nowhere and it will be in about nine years inside a doctor who is frightened."

"Then what do you want me to do."

"I want three copies of a fair document," said Sana Okafor, "in your own hand, with nothing on it that points at anybody, and I want the first one to mine before it is yours, and I want to know that you are going to write it and not me, because the day it is my document it is a document of a clinic and it goes on a board, and the day it is yours it is a thing in a shop, and I have watched two of these turn into matters in my life and I am not having a third."

"And the board."

"The board is mine and it has got four things on it and three of them were there before you and one of them is yours, and the fifth is not yours and it never will be, and I am not going to give you that in a car and I am not going to give you that in a corridor either, and if you ask me in a corridor I shall say no in front of six people." She started the engine. "Make your three. No certificate number. No claim number. No ward, no bed, no date of treatment, and nothing that a stranger could walk in and use. And post one to Corrie Street on Friday, and tell her that you have not published it and why, in your own words, so that she knows before she hears about it from anybody, because the day she hears about it from anybody is the day she decides to stop."

"She is not going to stop."

"She has not said that to me and neither have you, and I have believed both of you for a week," said Sana Okafor, "and I would like to be able to keep on believing it, and the way I keep on believing it is by there being one fewer document with her street on it every month and not one more."

She pulled out of the side street and stopped at the lights and put the heater back up.

"And one thing more, and then I have got a list."

"Yes."

"You sat in this car and decided, in nine minutes, that you were not going to publish a document about an eighty-one-year-old woman's hip, and you have a panel at ten to ten tomorrow morning, and I cannot stop you thinking about it, and I am not going to ask you about the panel." She looked at the lights. "**That is the case, and it is the smaller half of what you have decided today. The larger half is that you told me a patient's business in a parked car in nine minutes because it was the fastest way to get your own way, and in about four years somebody is going to ask me how I came to know a stranger's patient, and I will have to say that a man in a car told me because it was quicker than explaining why he could not.**"

Then the lights went and she turned into Marshall Street and parked in the yard and got out and shut the door, and went in, and did not look back at the car.

---

The back room at 14 Kettle Rows at four o'clock on Tuesday the eleventh of December had the paraffin heater on and the cat in the drawer and nine jobs on the bench, six of which were done.

He made the copies at the front bench with the good light, and he wrote them in his own hand, and they were four paragraphs and not a list, and he wrote them out nine times, because the first eight had a ward in them and a bed in them and a date in them, and the ninth did not.

> *In this city a rehabilitation claim is made by an account and not by a patient, and the claimant is a company with a registration and a premises, and a certificate for a treatment is issued to the payer and not to the patient, and a patient is notified and given a copy of the claim and not of the certificate. That is not an abuse. It is the arrangement, it is printed, and the people who keep it are doing a job. A patient holding nothing is not a person who has been refused something, because nobody refused her, and I have not been able to explain that to one person in four months and I am not going to try in a document.*

> *There is a certificate. It was issued in October by a district office of a public registry, in the ordinary way, by a registrar-counsel doing her job. It records a treatment, a service code and a fee, and the fee was the published fee for the code, and the money moved. It records a bed and a code and a date, and it does not record a person, and every line of it is a fact about an office and not about a human being.*

> *The treatment did not take place in the place the certificate records. It took place in a first-floor room over a shop, in an hour, on one Tuesday afternoon, and it was real work, done properly, by a competent practitioner, and it cost what it cost, and the person who received it is eighty-one years old and cannot remember it and has no document about it and has never asked for one.*

> *Four offices hold a part of this and cannot reach one another, and not one of the four has broken a rule, and that is neither an accident nor a piece of luck: it is the arrangement, and the arrangement is printed.* There is no order in this city that stops any of them, and the distance between a bed and a paid claim is four hours and a rubber stamp. The only reason that has not happened is that a woman has spent six years not asking questions and not handing paper over, and she has done that without any help from anybody, and I am not going to put her name next to this.*

He read the ninth one through four times, and it had no number in it of any kind, and it named no company and no ward and no date of treatment, and the only person in it was eighty-one and unnamed and described by her age, and he read that sentence four times too and decided it was the one line in the document he was not going to take out.

He wrote the same four paragraphs out twice more, clean, in his own hand.

He put the first one in Dr Okafor's hand at ten past five on the Tuesday, in an envelope with nothing on it. He put the second in the back of the notebook, between the print of three counterfoils and a page with four things on it that he had no right to hold. And the third went in an envelope with his own name on the outside and four stamps on it, because there is only one kind of envelope in the post office on the corner of Corrie Street, and he put it in the box at the end of Marshall Street at twenty to five on the same Tuesday, and the stamp on the envelope had a postmark with the time on it, and Rhoda Nesbit would open it in a kitchen on the Friday and read it before work and read it and work out in about four seconds that a man had been in an insurance office and a registry and a hospital and had come out of all three with nothing that could be used, and that he had posted it to her instead of putting it on a wall, and that the reason he had posted it instead of putting it on a wall was that in October a man had put four sheets on a wall and got a certificate and a licence bracket and a woman's kitchen out of it.

He got in at Marshall Street at ten past six and Otto Vahl had the radio on the bench that had been finished on Friday and had not been collected, and the till had thirty-one credits in it, and the certificate was on the shelf above the back bench on its own with a receipt from the municipal building six inches from it.

He had written the word *door* on a page on Monday and had put a line through it, and he was not going to write it again.
