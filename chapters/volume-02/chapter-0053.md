# Chapter 0053 — A Woman Who Is Not a Villain

The dry cleaner on the ground floor of number four in Corrie Street had a woman in it at twenty past three on Monday afternoon who knew exactly where she was going and had a bag with her.

"Give us a minute," said Rhoda Nesbit from the top of the stairs, and the woman on the counter said, "I have got till half four, I have been coming up here for four years, and I know what you are going to do, which is nothing to me." She put the bag down on the counter. "It is the Kaiser's, it is the one you told me not to shorten, and the girl on Thursday said the ticket was in the drawer and it is not in the drawer, and if it is in that drawer I will find it in about a year when I am sixty."

"Two minutes," said Rhoda Nesbit, and the woman said, "You said that in October," and went out into the street.

Elias Venn had been standing on the second-floor landing for four minutes with a notebook in his hand and had not written anything in it.

"You have come up on a Monday," said Rhoda Nesbit, coming back through her own door. "I do Fridays. I do the other days if somebody cannot do a Friday, and a lady from over the parade did a Tuesday in March because her husband was away, and I wrote it in the book as a Tuesday, because that is what it was, and I have never pretended otherwise about a day in my life."

"I heard that from a woman on the ground floor."

"You heard that from a woman on the ground floor," said Rhoda Nesbit, "which means this street already knows, and this street has known for four years, and my difficulty is not that people know. My difficulty is that the people who know are all paying me eleven credits, and the people who do not know are all being charged sixty, and neither of those two groups has ever once asked me a question about the other one."

She was fifty-eight and she was wearing a cardigan over a blouse with a pen in the pocket, and there was a room behind her with a table, two chairs, a sideboard, a kettle and a wall calendar with this month's Fridays ringed in ink, and a pot of somebody's knitting on the sideboard, and a plate with three clips on it where a sheet of A4 was held flat under a sheet of glass like a picture in a gallery.

"Sit down," she said. "Do not sit on that chair, that is where the clients sit and I have never once had a client and a man in a shop in the same room and I am not starting."

---

"I know who you are," she said, before he had got his notebook out. "I have never met you and I know two things about you, and the first is that there is a practice note on the shelf in the back of Concord Hall that uses your four items as a worked example, under a heading that is not your name, and the second is that in September your face went round a residents' group on a picture of paint bins and there are four hundred people in it and my landlord is in it."

"That is not a good first meeting."

"It is a very good first meeting if you are used to people not knowing things about you, and it is a very bad one if you are used to people knowing." She put the kettle on. "I read the practice note because I taught myself out of the manual. I bought the manual in 2025 for three credits off the rack in the back of the building where I did not work and where I have not been admitted since, and I read Module 4 eleven times and Module 7 twice and I have never read a word of any of the other nine because I could not find a use for them, and I would like you to understand that the last time a person in a position of authority said anything to me about a sequence was in 2023 and what she said was that the forms would be changed in the spring."

"The practice note does not say what I perceive is true."

"No. It says *such an account requires support, not clarification*, and it says a supplied order is not an account, and that is the sentence I have made my whole working life out of, and I have made it out of a book." She turned round. "You put a description of an order into a box on a telephone to a woman in that building in September, and it went into a curriculum, and it did not have to be true to do that, and nobody in that building stopped it happening, and the reason is on page five of Module 7 in a box in italics, and I have read that box more times than I have read anything else in my life."

Elias Venn put the notebook on his knee and did not open it.

"You have read the manual more carefully than I have."

"I have read the manual more carefully than the man who wrote it," said Rhoda Nesbit, "because I read it because I could not pay anybody, and he wrote it because there was going to be a rack of it in the hall."

She put a cup in front of him and did not sit down.

"Now. You have not said why you are here, and you have not said it in the first two minutes, which means you have a reason you have decided to arrive with. I am going to help you with it, because I have a Friday at four and a lady who wants her mother's kitchen, and you are going to want to be here on Friday. But you do not get the sheet. If you want to photograph it, no."

"I want to photograph it."

"No."

"Then I will read it," said Elias Venn, "and I will not write anything down, and you will watch me do it, and if I write one word you will take it off me."

Rhoda Nesbit considered this for about four seconds and then took the plate with the three clips off the sideboard and laid it face up on the table between them.

It was a single side of A4, printed on one side only, cream, and slightly foxed at the edges, and it had been folded twice and unfolded so many times that the folds had gone through to a shadow. There was no letterhead and no printer's mark and no page number. At the top, in a bold face that was two sizes larger than everything else, it said:

> **GROUNDING AND ANCHOR — SMALL PROCEDURE**
> **NOTES FOR THE PERSON DOING IT**
> **Module 4, page 11**

Underneath that there was a short paragraph, and then three boxes.

Elias Venn did not touch it. He read it the way a man reads a name on a list.

The paragraph said that a grounding session addresses an anchor in one of four ways and that the practitioner is not to choose the address on the person's behalf, and that the person is to be asked which of the four it is and the answer is to be used as given, and that where the person cannot answer, the commonest stated reason is that the person cannot travel.

The first box was headed **THE SUPPLIED ORDER** and its text was, word for word, the first box on page eleven of the printed manual: *A supplied order is not an account and may not be recorded as one.*

The second box was headed **THE ROOM** and its text was the room rule from the same page, word for word, including the eight words at the end about the commonest stated reason.

And the third box was headed **THE CLOSE**, and its text was this.

> *The working is not finished when the person stops talking. It is finished when it is released, and the release is the act that lets it settle and stop holding. Where the session is conducted in premises that are not a registered practice, the release is made in the room and is not left in the person. Write the date, the time, the procedure number and the fee, and give the slip to the person, and keep the counterfoil, and the counterfoil is the only record that the work was done.*

Elias Venn read the third box twice and then sat back in a chair in a first-floor flat above a dry cleaner in Corrie Street and put both hands flat on the table on either side of the plate, where she could see them.

"That is a release," he said.

"That is a release," said Rhoda Nesbit. "It has been in that box for four years, and I have never once used it, and I am going to tell you why, and then you are going to tell me why you have gone the colour of the wall."

"Because there is no such box in the manual."

"There is no such box in the manual," said Rhoda Nesbit, "and I have checked, and I have the manual, and I have a bookshop on Cutter Street that has the 2025, 2022 and 2019 printings on a shelf in the back and I have read all three, and the third box is not in any of them. It is not a later edition. It is not an erratum. It is not in a book by anybody who has ever printed a book, and the type is not the type of the manual and the paper is not the paper of the manual."

"How did you get it?"

"I took it off the rack." She said it without any drama. "I was in the building in 2025, in March, because I had gone to see about a form. I was on the ground floor for about four minutes and I was early, and there was a basket by the radiator and I had bought a manual out of it and I was standing there doing what everybody does, which is looking at the rest of it, and this was on top of the basket, and it was folded twice, and I put it in my bag. I did not take it out for four months. I read it at my kitchen table in 2025 and I have been working out of it since, and I have done about four hundred sessions out of that sheet, and I have charged between eleven and twenty credits, and I have never charged a woman more than she had, and twice I have not charged a woman anything and one of those was a fortnight ago and she does not know that either."

"What order does it tell you to do them in."

"It does not tell me an order anywhere," said Rhoda Nesbit. "That is the thing I want you to understand, because you have come up those stairs for four minutes with a notebook and you have been waiting to ask me who taught me, and nobody taught me. **There is no order in it. The sheet does the order.** I read the two boxes and I do what they say and the third box is the close, and if you have never been taught a sequence in your life and you have this page in front of you, you will do a competent job, and I have watched four hundred competent jobs and I have never had a failure that was anybody's fault except mine."

She sat down on the client chair, opposite him, which was against a rule she had made in the first two minutes and had not expected to break.

"Who printed it."

"I do not know, and I have stopped thinking about it, and I want to tell you exactly what I think about it, because you are the first person in four years to ask me a question instead of a fee." She put her hands on her knees. "I think it came off that rack, because it was on that rack in the same basket as the manual and it was the same fold as a thing somebody has had in a bag, and I think somebody put it there on purpose, on the third of March in 2025, and I think they have never in four years come to collect it, and I think that if I am frightened about it then I will stop working, and if I stop working then the woman on the top floor of number fourteen goes to a proper place in a fortnight and pays sixty credits, and I have watched a woman come back from a proper place having been told that the name was never going to be reliable and to stop coming. So I do not think about it. I have not thought about it in a proper way since about 2026."

"You are frightened of it now."

"I am frightened of it now," said Rhoda Nesbit, "because it has been in a tin in a flat above a dry cleaner for four years and you have come up those stairs and put your hands flat on the table either side of it."

---

He did not write anything down. He read the sheet twice more, all of it, including the parts about the four addresses, and he gave it back, and she put the plate flat and clipped it and set it on the sideboard.

"One question, and then I am going to let you go downstairs," he said. "I am not asking who printed it. I am asking whether anybody has ever told you the order of it. Not given you the sheet. **Said it to you.** A practitioner, a course tutor, a man in a corridor. Anybody at all."

Rhoda Nesbit looked at the sideboard for a while.

"Twelve years I was a claims clerk," she said, "for a rehabilitation account on the Verge Row side, and I will tell you what that was, because you have come up here for four minutes and I owe you the honest version of what you are sitting in a room above. I did not read people's memories. I read what a clinic had put on a form, and I checked it against what a counterfoil said, and if the two matched I passed it, and if they did not match I put a query on it and it went to a person called an assessor, and the assessor decided, and in twelve years I never once went and looked at a person. I did not know what a person looked like. I knew what a form looked like and I was extremely good at forms."

"And then a reorganization."

"And then a reorganization, and I was forty-six, and there was no money in it and no fault in it and nobody in that building was unpleasant to me, and I have never said in public that I was let go and I am not going to start." She straightened up. "So: nobody has ever told me the order of anything in my life. A supervisor told me how to alphabetise. A woman in 2019 told me that a release is a thing that commits a change and closes a working and that I should think of a form as a working, with a header and four lines, and that if I could not fill a line in honestly then I was to put *not stated* in it and let the assessor argue with *not stated*." She almost smiled. "I put *not stated* in about nine hundred of them. That is the entire training I have had, and I am telling you it because you asked whether anybody ever said anything to me, and the answer is one sentence about a form, in 2019, by a woman whose name I could not tell you."

"Twelve years in claims," said Elias Venn. "And a release is a thing that commits a change and closes a working. You know exactly what the third box is."

"I know what it is," said Rhoda Nesbit. "I have known for four years. That is why it is in the tin and not in the drawer, and why I have never used it, and why I have never charged anybody for it, and why I have never told one of them it exists." She stood up. "You are going to ask me whether I have used it, and I am going to tell you before you ask, because I have found that out about people and I do not like it. No. Not once in four hundred. And I will tell you why, and it is not virtue. It is that the third box says *write the date, the time, the procedure number and the fee, and give the slip to the person, and keep the counterfoil, and the counterfoil is the only record that the work was done.* And I have four hundred women and men in this city who would be sitting in a chair with a slip in a tin, and I have never once had a slip in anybody's hand, and the reason I have never handed one out is that the day I hand one out, this stops being a favour and becomes a practice, and a practice is in a different category of law from a favour, and I have stayed in the first category for four years on purpose."

Elias Venn sat in the client chair in a first-floor room above a dry cleaner and understood, about nine seconds late, that he had come up those stairs to take a sheet off a frightened woman and he was in the room of somebody who had understood the whole of it four years ago and had built a rule out of it.

"That is the third box in your own manual," he said. "Module 4. Page eleven. *A supplied order is not an account and may not be recorded as one.*"

"It is," said Rhoda Nesbit. "It is the reason I bought the book and it is the reason I have never used the third box, and you have just quoted it at me, and I would like you to notice that."

He said he was sorry and she said that he was not, and that he had said it in the first minute, which was why she had let him read it.

---

Saint Orra Reach was four miles away and he got there at twenty to seven and phoned the laboratory from the porter's lodge because the linen office does not take visitors after six, and Mara Quill came out to the door in a coat over her working clothes with her hair wet and said, "You have the look of a man who has been told something true by a person who is not a doctor and it has not helped you."

"It has not helped me at all."

"Then come and I will give you a bad cup of tea and you can tell me about it in a room with a door, and then I am going to tell you the sentence I have been trying to say to you since the fourth of October."

The laboratory was two rooms behind the linen office and it was warm and it smelled of dust and warm glass and there were four instruments on a bench and two of them were older than the department beside them and one of them was under a hood with a plate in it that he was not allowed to look at and did not try to.

"Sit down. That chair. Not that one, that one has a plate in it." She put the kettle on. "The five lines are no use."

"They were on a wall—"

"The five lines are no use for a person, and I have known that since the Saturday I lent you a plate and I have not said it out loud because I have been waiting to see whether you would." Mara Quill put a cup in front of him and did not sit down. "Line one: *whose object, and who has said it is theirs, in their own words, with the date.* A person is not an object. A person is an object that is going to be in the room when you make a mistake and is going to have an opinion about it, and when you ask her whether the object is hers and she says yes, you have not got a name on a thing, you have got a person who has agreed to be treated as a thing, and she will remember that she agreed to it, and in four years' time somebody will ask her what she was told."

"That is a harder sentence than yours."

"It is a harder sentence than mine and it is the correct one, and I have had four documents on a table in that room which were not people, and I have had no documents at all about people, and I did not know that was a different job until a woman on a first floor in Corrie Street described to you what she does for eleven credits." She sat down at last. "Now. The sentence I have been trying to say since October, and I am going to say it badly, and I want you to write it down afterwards in your own handwriting and not in mine, because it is a sentence about a hole and I do not own a hole."

"Go on."

"A **false start** is a working that began and was stopped and left its first mark on the material." She said it slowly. "That is the whole of it. That is the definition and it is not mine, and I have been looking for one for two months and I have never seen one, and in September I had a plate with a set order on it and in October I had a plate with two orders on it and neither of those was one, because a false start is not a second order. A false start is the *beginning* of an order, and then nothing, and then the order again from somewhere else. It is a hole with a lip on it."

"And you cannot see one."

"**Neither of us can see one.**" Mara Quill put her hand flat on the bench. "That is the first honest sentence I have said to you about what my four instruments are for. I can hold one order on a surface and I can tell you a made order from a made one and I can measure a seat to four thousandths of a millimetre, and if you put a false start in front of me on a plate tonight I will tell you there is something in the material and I will not be able to tell you what it is, and neither will you. I have spent eleven years being the best instrument in this city at something, and the thing I cannot do is the thing your faculty cannot do, and I have never said it to you before because there has never been a reason, and there is now."

Elias Venn put both hands on the table.

"Does anyone know how to tell?"

"Nobody I have ever met. There is nothing in the manual. I have looked, and I have looked hard, and what I have found is four people in this city who would insist they can and would be guessing." She picked her cup up and did not drink from it. "And that is not a sad fact, Elias. That is the most useful thing anybody has said to me in a fortnight, because it means that if a working in this city ever turns out to have a false start in it, then **there will be no instrument and there will be no certificate, and the only thing in the world that will have seen it is the person who reads orders.** And I have spent two months trying to work out how to build a room in which a man who reads orders can be believed, and I have got as far as a sheet of paper with five lines on it that we both signed and that is lodged nowhere and is worth nothing in a room full of lawyers. So I would like to stop, and I would like us to go and look at a thing that is not a person for a living, because at the moment there is nobody in this city I can trust to be in a room with a woman who cannot afford sixty credits, and that is not a sentence I expected to say in November."

Outside, on the third floor of Saint Orra Reach, a ward light came on and held, and neither of them turned round.
