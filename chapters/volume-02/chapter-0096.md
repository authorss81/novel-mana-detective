# Chapter 0096 — Nine Tables at the End

The canteen at the Aldergate Wing had a hatch with a rail on it and a servery and nine tables of four and a matron of about seventy-eight who had been there since 1979 and who called everybody love and had called everybody love since before any of them were born, and on Saturday the nineteenth of January at about twenty to eight in the evening it had nine people in it and Elias Venn, and at ten past eight Sana Okafor came round the tables with a clipboard and told him that he was not going to sit at any of them.

"I have not asked to sit at any of them."

"You are going to sit at the end of number seven on your own for about two hours and I am going to tell everybody that you are there and I am not going to tell anybody why, and then I am going to come and sit with you for about ten minutes at the end, and if anybody comes over to your table in the meantime I would be grateful if you did not answer them in a voice." She put the clipboard under her arm. "This is not unkind. There are seven people at these tables who have been asked something by a stranger in the last four months and every one of them has been asked it by you or by somebody you had spoken to that morning, and I have spent nineteen years finding out that the fastest way to lose a waiting room is to have a man in a good coat in it."

So Elias Venn sat at the end of table seven with a cup of tea and a plate of something and did not answer anybody, and the matron brought him a second cup without being asked and told him the sugar was on the left, and he said thank you, and the matron said love, and went away.

He had the list out of his notebook at about half past eight and he read it out at the end of table seven to nobody, and then he read it out again to a canteen.

"A ward sister of sixty-one with twenty-six years came back onto a book on the seventh of January after six weeks off it, and she has four photographs in a drawer that are not going to any office."

"That is me," said Sister Ivy Warhurst.

"I know that is you."

"Then you have wasted about nine seconds, and I have told a man in a shop that I was not going to be a route into anything and I have not moved an inch since the seventh, and I am not going to be a route into anything tonight either, and I would like my tea."

"I am not asking you to be."

"You have a list and it has my name on it and a number next to it and you have read that list to about nine people in about four months, and one of them asked me about a photograph in a corridor in December, and I said no, and here we are." She turned her cup round on the table. "I am not cross with you. I am going to eat my dinner."

"A woman of fifty-two in a dispensary filled in a box on a form herself on Christmas morning for the first time in eleven years, with her own name under the doctor's in the fifth, and it is on a file in her own building."

"That is me," said Doreen Tillery. "And I would like to say one thing about that before anybody else in this room says something kind about it. I have had three weeks of that piece of paper and I have not told anybody in this building that it is there, including the doctor, because I do not know who put it there, and I have decided that a person who finds out that a stranger wrote their name under a doctor's on a form is allowed to take a while."

"You are allowed to take a while."

"Thank you." She turned her cup round as well, and it was not a thing they had agreed. "And I have been to a counter on Wednesday evening and a man in a coat bought a tin of tea and asked me whether he could have a chair in this building on a Thursday, and I said yes, and if anybody in this room is wondering whether that was a good idea, it was, and I would do it again, and I have been thinking about it for two days."

---

He got as far as the woman of sixty-three with the ledger before he was stopped, and being stopped was the useful part, because a woman of sixty-three with nineteen years of a rack in a building across the river put her cup down and said, "No."

"You have not heard the list."

"I have heard about four items of it, which is more than I came here for, and no." Enid Marlow was not a large woman and there was nothing at all aggressive in her voice. "I did eleven names off a rack in about nine seconds in the last week of December and I have not had a week since, and that was not a favour anybody did me and it was not a cost I paid, it was a job, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that it was a job."

"You are saying that because you are the only person in this room who has not been hurt."

"I am saying that because I have noticed that every single one of us at these tables has been *about* something for four months," said Enid Marlow, "and I have not been about anything since the nineteenth of December, and I have found that the cheapest way to stay a person in this city is to be the one who does a job and is not in anybody's notebook, and I have got eleven names in a book that I wrote there in a job because it was a job and nobody asked me to, and I do not want to be a thing that happened to somebody."

Nobody said anything, and a man of sixty-three three tables away, who had a crate store on Fenwick Street and had not said a word all evening, said, "That is the best thing anybody has said in this canteen in about four years and it is going to be in a report."

"It is not going to be in a report."

"It will be in my head," said Wendell Combe, "and that is worse and I am aware of it."

---

The porter came round last, at about half past nine, with a tray of four plates that he had not eaten off, and Sana Okafor stopped him at the servery and made him put it down and sit at the end of table two, and he sat down and did not touch it for about a minute.

"Ahmed," said Sana Okafor. "I have worked in this building for nineteen years and I have got you down here because I want to ask you a question and I have thought about how to ask it for about four days."

"Then ask."

"Would you be willing to be asked to sign things."

The canteen got about a quarter as quiet as a canteen gets.

"I do not know what you mean," said Ahmed.

"I mean: from Monday there is going to be a piece of paper with a list of names on it and I am going to be the one holding it, because a member of the public cannot hold a list of patients, and I have been sitting at this table on and off for nine years watching you carry eleven trays at a time and never once sign anything, and I have a theory and it is a stupid theory, and I think the reason is that nobody has ever handed you a pen and said you are needed, and every single one of us at these tables has been handed something." She put her hands flat on the table. "That is the question. You can say no. I want to say that before you answer, because if you cannot say no to me then the paper is worth nothing."

"Ahmed, no surname and none is to be invented," said Wendell Combe from table three, with great satisfaction.

"Ahmed, no surname, yes," said Sana Okafor.

The porter looked at the plate in front of him for a while.

"I will sign it," he said. "I will sign anything on a Monday. I have never once been asked for anything and I have been in this building nineteen years and my whole life I have been the person you give a tray to and then you say thank you, and everybody here has been *about* something and everybody here is wrong about me, and I would like it noticed that I have been the only one of us who has not had a single thing done to him and it has been the best thing about my life."

"That is not going to be in a report," said Sana Okafor.

"It is going to be all over a report," said Ahmed, "and I do not care, and you are going to give me the pen on Monday and you are not going to ask me whether I understand it."

---

A woman of fifty at the end of table one had not said anything all evening and had a hand flat on the table beside her plate, and she had not eaten any of it, and she had come in at about a quarter to nine and had said one sentence to the matron and had not sat with anybody.

"That is Petronella Osei," said Sana Okafor, across the room, "and she works at my desk and she has been at that desk for six years, and she does not take names, and I have never once asked her why, and I am not going to ask her tonight either."

"You are going to ask her something else," said Petronella Osei.

"I am going to ask you something else, and I am going to ask it in front of nine people, and I am not going to be able to unsay it, and I have been putting it off since about eleven o'clock this morning."

"Go on, then."

"Does the Aldergate Wing have anybody in it who goes to a room above a shop on a Tuesday."

The matron, who had been at the servery with her back to the room, said, "Oh, Petronella, love."

"You have a Tuesday," said Sana Okafor. "You have had a Tuesday for about two years. You do not go to a hospital and you do not go to a doctor and you do not go to a church, and you go four streets and a parade and you come back at half past twelve and on Fridays you are here at twenty to eight and you have been here every Friday for about two years, and last November you were here on the Tuesday, and I noticed, and I did not say anything, because I have a rule."

"You have a rule."

"I have a rule that says I do not ask my own front desk staff what they do with their own time," said Sana Okafor, "and I have had that rule since about 2021 and I have never once thought about it until about four days ago, and it is the best rule in this building and it has just cost me a Tuesday."

---

Elias Venn had been at the end of table seven for two hours and had been spoken to twice, both times by a woman who had been told not to speak to him, and at about a quarter to eleven he got up and carried his cup over to table four, where Winnie Arkwright was sitting on her own with a cup in both hands, and sat down without asking, and she moved her plate about four inches to make room and did not comment on it.

"Twenty-two years on the linen run," she said.

"Yes."

"I have got one question and it has been sitting in me since about the seventh of January and I have not asked it, because I did not want to be the woman who asks a man a question twice."

"You asked me a question once and I answered it and you were glad to be asked it. Ask it."

Winnie Arkwright looked at the table.

"In a corridor on the seventh of January you told me you do not read people and you have never read a person and you are not going to, and about four people I work with heard you say it and I have thought about it every day since, and it has not upset me and I have not been frightened, and here is the thing I have actually been wondering about." She turned the cup. "**Can you tell when it is happening.** In a room, with somebody sat in it. Not what they are thinking. Just whether something is going on in there that has not finished."

"No," said Elias Venn.

"Are you certain."

"I am certain, and I have been certain since September, and it is the single most useful thing anybody in this case knows about me and nobody has ever put it in a document, and the reason it is useful is that it means the man who stands in a corridor and tells you a working is happening in a room is either in that room or has been told by somebody who was, and that is the whole of what a working in a room amounts to as far as anybody in this city is concerned." He turned his cup round. "**There is one thing I can do and I have only done it once. On the eleventh of January I put my hand flat on a door and whatever was on the other side of it came through into my arm instead of stopping at my hand, and I have not found out since whether that is the same faculty or the other side of it, and I am not going to be able to find out on my own, and it is not a thing you can send a man into.** And in four months in this city I have not had one person tell me they can do that, and I have had a laboratory technician tell me she can do about a quarter of what I can and not the half of it that matters, and I have told her so in a laboratory and she did not argue."

"That is a very strange way to live."

"It is the way I live," he said, "and it is why I have got a notebook and fifty credits and a formal reprimand, and if you meet a man in a good coat in this city who tells you he can see it happening, you do not have to take my word for it, you have only to ask me in front of him, and he will not be able to do it either, and one of us will be wrong about a thing that is not in dispute, and it will not be me."

She laughed, which she had not expected to do.

"Twenty-two years," she said, "and the question I have been carrying since the seventh of January has been *can you tell*, and it has taken you two and a half minutes to tell me no, and I am going to think about how long I spent not asking it and I am going to have a very bad half hour about that."

---

Sana Okafor found him in the corridor outside the canteen at about ten past eleven at night with the corridor light off and a fire bucket and a bin and nothing else in it.

"I have been in that building nineteen years," she said. "I have never once been asked to put my own name on a piece of paper. I have had a registration number on about four hundred documents and my name at the bottom of about eleven of them, and I have never once had to go and stand in a room and swear that something happened, and I have not wanted to, and I have been told about a great many things by a great many people and I have said yes to none of them."

"You are going to have to."

"I am going to have to, and I have been thinking about it since about eight o'clock this evening while eight people said no to me in a canteen, and I have arrived at a place and I am going to tell you what it is and then I am going to go to bed." She had her coat on over her clothes. "In December I took ninety-odd pages off my own waiting-room wall and put them in a box in my own office because a woman of seventy-two with a hip would not take them back and I could not give her an answer. I did that because a wall with one kind of paper on it is a wall. And on Friday I got the pins out of the registrar's transfer order, which has been on that board since the twenty-first of September, and I put them back in, because I cannot remember whose document it is, and a woman who cannot remember whose document is on her wall does not get to take documents off her wall."

"That was Friday and I know it."

"I know you know it, which is the trouble." She turned round and faced the wall. "**So the whole of that wall has been sitting there for four months waiting for me to put my own name under it, and I have refused twice, and I am not refusing a third time, and I am going to do it in a way that is mine and not yours and not an inspector's, and the first thing about it is that there will be a name on it and there will be a date on it, and the second thing is that the date will be a range and the range will have an end in it.**"

"That is not how orders work."

"I know exactly how orders work, I have spent nineteen years watching other people ask this building for them and I have never once had a reason to." She looked at him. "A piece of paper with a date range on it that has an end in it is not a promise. It is a thing that has to come back to me and be looked at, and I have had four months of a wall that nothing comes back to, and that is the whole of what is wrong with it, and it is not a legal instrument and it is not a document and it is the only thing I have ever wanted to put up in nineteen years."

She took her coat off her arm and folded it.

"Do not tell me anything before Monday," she said. "Not one thing. Not how many, not when, not what is in the room, and do not come and stand on my landing on a Sunday, and if you telephone me I shall not answer it, and on Monday I am going to be in a building on Vine Street, and on Wednesday morning I am going to put my registration number at the bottom of a piece of paper, and I have never done it, and I am not going to tell you what hour, because I do not know what hour I am going to need, and I am going to be frightened, and I have not told anybody that I am going to be frightened except a man in a corridor at ten past eleven at night who is not allowed to be in the room."

She went up the stairs with her coat over her arm, and on the half-landing she stopped and turned round and looked down at a man in a corridor in the dark.

"Whatever it is you are going to do on Tuesday," she said, "you are not going to do it on your own, and I do not know a thing about you and I have not asked, and if you want a doctor in that room then there is a doctor in that room, and if you do not then there is not, and either way I am going to know which, because I am going to be the one who tells the eleven of them to put their hands on the arms of their chairs."

He did not answer, and she went up, and he stood in the corridor in the dark and put his hand flat on the wall beside him, twice, and then went down and sat on the wall outside the shop for forty minutes.
