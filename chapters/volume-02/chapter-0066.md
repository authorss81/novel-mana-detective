# Chapter 0066 — Four Hours and Six Coins

The coin laundry on Verge Row was four machines wide, a dryer on the end that worked and three that did not, a bench along the wall with a folding board at one end, and a woman on the second machine who had been on it since half past six and who was folding a sheet in four and had been folding a sheet in four since before he came in.

He had worked nights there for four years when he was twenty-two. He had been the one who swept it out at two in the morning and the one who was still there at five with the machines going. The owner had a name on the glass and the name on the glass was a name and not a company, which was the reason he had worked there, and it was still the same name, and the woman on the second machine looked at him for about four seconds on Saturday morning and then said, "You are the one who used to do the five o'clock."

"That is me."

"Then you can do the four." She put the folded sheet on the pile. "I have to go at eleven and Ruth cannot do the four, she does it in three, and I am not doing the three at half eleven on a Saturday because I have a bus."

"Give me the sheet," said Elias Venn. "I will do the four."

"You will not do the four, you will do it badly and I shall have to do it again, and I have not got time to do it again." She said it without any edge at all, which was worse. "You are a man who is going to be here all morning and you can carry that basket, and when the man comes for the dryer on four you will tell him it is fixed and it is not fixed and I will fix it on Monday, and he is seventy-eight and he will not mind and he will say thank you and go away happy, and if you tell him it is not fixed he will stand there for ten minutes and ask you about the bearings and I do not have ten minutes."

---

He carried the basket. The man who came for the dryer on four was not seventy-eight; he was about fifty and he was a plasterer and he had a folding table under his arm and he was going to a house in the outer wards and he wanted it by twelve.

"It is not fixed," said Elias Venn. "She says Monday."

"Monday." The plasterer put the table down. "Is that the woman on the second machine? Because I have been coming here since her father had it and I have never once known what I am paying for. How much is a wash?"

"Eleven credits for up to six pounds. It used to be eight."

"Eight," said the plasterer, "in the year I came, and it was eight then and I have never had a bill and I have never asked, and I would like to be told that it is eleven and not that it is something else." He got the table under his arm again. "Tell her it is Monday. And tell her the drain is the thing, not the drum, and I am not the first one to say that and I will not be the last, and tell her if she puts a new drum in it and not a new drain she will be doing it again in a year and I will be the one standing here."

He went, and Elias Venn carried four more baskets, and the woman on the second machine said, without stopping, "You did that well."

"I told him it was not fixed."

"Nobody tells anybody that." She put another sheet down. "**You are going to be here all morning because there is nowhere else you want to be, and that is not the same as being lonely, and I have watched men get that wrong for twenty-nine years and I have never once seen a man get it right by wanting to be somewhere else.**"

---

He asked her, at about ten, what the number was for, and she told him, and he wrote it in his own book in his own hand, and then he did not write anything else in it all day, which is the first time since the seventeenth of September that has happened.

The number was on a card in a plastic frame by the door, in the same type as the matron's card at Wexford Row had been in a different typeface, and the card said what everything cost and when the last change had been, and the last change had been in March, and eleven had been eight before March.

"You did that."

"I did that," said the woman on the second machine. "Two years ago it was eight and there is a woman on Aldermere Road who has been coming here since her husband died and she brings us about four pounds a week and she pays in coins out of a jar, and eight was fine for her at eight. And I told the owner she was going to have to find somewhere else because I am not going to work at eight for a machine that costs him forty credits an hour to keep warm, and he said that to me, and he was right, and she was still welcome, because there is no rule anywhere that says a laundry has to charge less, and she is welcome and she does about three pounds a week now and she has never once said a word about it, and that is the whole of what happened."

"What is your name?"

"Ruthie," said the woman on the second machine. "I have been Ruthie since 1978 and before that I was Ruth, and there is one Ruth in this laundry and that is me, and I am fifty-nine and I have two machines and no register and no supervisor and a card on the door that says what everything costs, and there is a rule in this city about a person who does what I do to a person's memory and there is not one rule in this city about a person who does what I do to a person's washing, and I have thought about that a very great deal in the last four years and I have got nowhere with it."

"Have you ever thought about getting a register."

"I have looked at it up on the machine in the corner." She put a folded sheet on the pile. "There is no such thing as a registered laundry. There is a water board and a licensing man who comes and looks at the drains about twice a year, and he is a nice fella and he signs the card, and that is my whole relationship with the government of this city. The number is not what makes you all right. The number is what makes somebody else all right. And the somebody else is the woman on Aldermere Road with a jar, and she does not have a jar in March, and I have made that decision on my own account and not with anybody and I would make it again."

And at about eleven, with the folding done and the machines going and the door shut, he stood by the bench in a coin laundry on Verge Row with twenty-two credits in a till four streets away and did the arithmetic, and then did not do it, and did not ask anybody for anything.

Eleven credits was a session. He could have afforded it. He had decided in the bus at ten past nine that he would not, and the decision was not about money, and he worked out what it was about at about half past nine, sitting on a bench with a folding board at the end of it.

He could not say the sentence. To go to 4 Corrie Street at one o'clock on a Saturday and say *a woman I met on Wednesday did this for you once, for sixty credits, and got eleven written in a book, and you will not remember it, and there is a certificate* — he could not say it, and the reason he could not say it was that Rhoda Nesbit had spent six years never asking anybody a single question, and he was not going to be the man who finally asked her for one, and it was not a small thing to refuse to do, and refusing it cost him the only door he had.

At half past eleven the woman on the second machine got her coat and said, "If you turn the bench up, that is my hour, and I have never had to ask anybody to do it in twenty-nine years, and I am asking you, and there is nothing to be gained by telling anybody I asked."

He turned the bench up.

He walked back and got in at Marshall Street at ten past twelve with his hands smelling of hot cotton, and Jules Ardent was not there yet, and Otto Vahl was at the front bench with a radio that had been finished on Friday and had not been collected, and the till had twenty-two credits in it, and the certificate was on the shelf above the back bench on its own.

Otto Vahl looked at him for a moment.

"You have worked a Saturday."

"I have worked a Saturday."

"Right." He turned the radio over and looked at the back of it. "I will open up at half past eight. Go and lie down, and I will tell the people who come, and if anybody wants a kettle mended they can come back Monday, and I have said that to a man already and he was not pleased about it and he can be not pleased on Monday."

---

Jules Ardent came at half past two with a radio in a carrier bag and an envelope in his coat pocket, and he put the radio on the bench and the envelope on the bench next to it, and did not sit down.

"Seventeen credits of yours," he said. "Three jobs, October, November, and one you did for nothing in September that I am not paying you for. I have had it in the drawer since the twenty-eighth of October and I have not known what to do with it."

"Put it back."

"I am going to." He did not. "It is in an envelope and it is not a form and it is not a claim and there is no line for it in a book, and I have had it in a drawer for six weeks, and I would like to hand it to a man I owe and be told no, because I have not been able to work out how to be a person about it." He put his hand flat on the counter. "No."

"No," said Elias Venn. "You have got a maker's enquiry open on your own die block and a charge on a file with a sergeant's name at the bottom of it and a bench room you have not opened since October. If I take seventeen credits off you today then in about two years a lawyer stands up in a room and says a mechanic paid a man with a reprimand seventeen credits in a repair shop on the eighth of December, and I cannot be there saying it was for radios, because I will have written it down, and then it is not a gift, it is a payment, and I have spent four months trying not to be the sort of man somebody hires."

"That is the second time you have said something like that to me and both times it were in the shop."

"Both times it were true and both times I did not want to." He turned the radio round. "Sit down. Tell me about the shoulder."

"My shoulder is a thing I have had for four years and a doctor looked at it in October and told me to stop carrying things and I did not stop carrying things." Jules Ardent took the chair at the end of the bench, the fourth chair, the one that had been in the room since September and had never had anybody in it. "You did not ask me that in a corridor in October and I noticed that you did not, and I have thought about it about four times since."

"I did not ask you because you had come and told me something you had not told anybody, and it seemed to me that a man in that position is allowed one thing that is about himself and one thing about somebody else, and not two things about himself."

"Right," said Jules Ardent. "Then I will tell you the shoulder, since nobody else is going to, and you are not to write it down, and I want that on the record as a request and not as a rule, and the difference between the two is that a rule I can break."

Then he told him about the shoulder for about nine minutes.

It was the right one, the one he had been carrying other people's work in, and it went in 2026 in a workshop in the low cut with a canvas bag, and it had been all right for four years because the weight had been other people's and the shape of it had not been his, and then in November it had been his own envelope, and the difference between carrying a bag for a man you have never met and carrying an envelope of cash to a shop where the man you are paying is sitting on a stool is about a hundred and forty pounds, and the hundred and forty pounds had gone in about four seconds.

"I have had a doctor look at it in October and he wrote a word down and I have not been able to read the word, so I have decided the word is *stop*," said Jules Ardent. "And I have not stopped, and I have not been able to make myself be the sort of man who stops, and the reason is that the die block is in a registry drawer and the bench room is shut and the enquiry is open, and if I stop carrying then the enquiry finishes, and if the enquiry finishes then in about two years there is a maker's report with one set of figures in it and one set of figures is not a report."

"Jules—"

"That is the thing I have not told anybody," said Jules Ardent, "and I have just told you, and I said I was not to write it down, and you wrote it down, and I am not going to stop you, and I would like you to understand that if I had wanted it kept I would not have said it in a shop at half past two on a Saturday to a man who has not been able to do anything for me since the seventeenth of September." He got up off the fourth chair, which he had been sitting in for nine minutes and which had a dip in the seat from somebody else's weight over twenty years. "I am going to go and be somewhere with a bench in it, and I am not going to carry anything, and the shoulder can have it."

---

Otto Vahl put four credits into his hand at about ten to four and said, "That is for the cat."

"Otto, I have been on the laundry since seven."

"That is for the cat," said Otto Vahl, "and if you say one word about it I will put it back in the till and it will be a worse thing for the cat, and I have had that cat since the second of November and it has cost me a hundred and fifty credits and it has not made anybody happy including itself."

Elias Venn took the four credits, because there was no version of the afternoon in which refusing them was the better thing.

He put the blanket over the chairback and lay down on the bench at ten to four in the afternoon on Saturday the eighth of December, in a repair shop on Marshall Street, with the paraffin heater going and the light off, and he slept for six hours and forty minutes, which was the longest sleep he had had since the seventeenth of September, and he did not dream about the seventeenth of September, and he did not wake up at any point, and Otto Vahl came in and out of the shop eight times between half past eight and ten to six and put a card against the door at half past eight that said BACK AT SIX and turned the bell off at eight because the bell went all over Marshall Street and it is a Saturday and the shop is shut.

He came up off the bench at ten to six in the dark with a mark on his cheek from the edge of a drawing board, and the till had twenty-six credits in it, and there were nine jobs on the bench that Otto Vahl had written out for him on the back of an invoice in his own enormous hand, and outside on Marshall Street the lamps were on and the rain had come back, and there were thirty-seven days on the order in a drawer in a municipal building four miles away, and his arm was over his face and his right hand was open on the blanket with nothing in it, and for about four seconds on Saturday evening he did not know what day it was, and then he did.
