# Chapter 0100 — Where Anybody May Read It

The corridor at Vine Street at ten past seven on Wednesday the twenty-third of January had six chairs in it and a radiator and a fire bucket, and Nadia Kest had a sheet of paper in her hand and did not sit down, and there were two other people in it and one of them was a man of thirty-nine in a shirt and a jumper with no sleeves who had come on a bus and had a bus ticket in his hand.

"Three names," she said. "And I am going to read this out properly, in order, with the reason attached to each one, and I am going to do it standing up, and Mr Brack is going to have to stand up as well because there is nowhere for three of us to sit."

"Stand up," said Eamon Brack, and stood up.

"**Ivor Skellern, fifty-two, of nine Calder Place, arrested at about twenty past five last night on the first floor of that building, for unregistered practice in respect of memory and identity work, and for being named on two claims as the practitioner who performed services he did not perform. The second one is a false statement made in a claim and it is a separate charge and I am not going to pretend it is the same charge and I am not going to pretend it is small.** He has had a solicitor from about nine o'clock this morning and I have not spoken to him and I am not going to."

"Is he in custody?"

"He is, and he asked at about eight o'clock this morning whether the eleven people in that room were all right, and I have told him that nine of the eleven can say their own name and two of them cannot, and he said *right* and nothing else, and that is the whole of what has passed between us."

She turned the sheet over.

"**Rhoda Nesbit, fifty-eight, of four Corrie Street, first floor, arrested at about twenty to two this morning at Vine Street, for unregistered practice in respect of memory and identity work. She is not charged with anything else. I want that said out loud in a corridor and I want it written down in the charge sheet as a negative, because a charge sheet that says *not charged with any other offence* is a document, and there is not one of those in this city, and somebody should start.**"

"Three of them," said Eamon Brack.

"You are the third and you have not been arrested and you are not in custody, and I am going to read your reason and then I am going to tell you why you are different, and then you are going to hate it, and then I am going to have to live with it for about two years." She looked at the sheet. "**Eamon Brack, thirty-nine, of four Corrie Street, first floor, charged by summons on the twenty-third of January 2030 with unregistered practice in respect of memory and identity work, on the basis of a book seized under an order of the eighteenth of January and a fee charged in it.** That is the whole of it."

"That is not the whole of it."

"It is not the whole of it and I have tried three ways to make it the whole of it this week and I cannot, and here is why, and you are going to hear it now because you are standing in a corridor and not in a room." She put the sheet down on her arm. "**I cannot charge you with the nine.** Not because nobody saw it and not because you will not admit it and not because I have no book. Because a use is not an offence. There is no offence in this city of taking a sitting that was done well and putting it in somebody who was in the room, and if there were, then the sheet that says a patient has been told what the work is would be a charge sheet, and I have looked for four days and there is not one, and I have asked two people who are paid to know and one of them would not put it in writing. So a man can take nine of them and go free, and the only thing in the world that stops him is that a nurse of fifty-one can swear to four minutes in a room."

Eamon Brack stood in the corridor with the bus ticket in his hand.

"A man of twenty-five behind a counter told me on Friday that he would not sleep that night," he said, "because I asked him, and I did not tell him what I asked him for and he did not ask me. That is not a charge. That is not going to be in a document. I would like somebody to know that it is the only thing in this case that I have done in six years which I would put in a document if anybody ever let me, and nobody is going to let me, and I am going to have to be a man who did that and said nothing, and that is what I am, and I have been it since Friday and I have slept about four hours."

"You are charged with one offence."

"I know exactly what I am charged with," said Eamon Brack. "I have had a green book with two hundred and forty sessions in it for six years and I have never once been asked about a single line of it by anybody in this city until a man in a good coat asked me about a column, and that man is standing in the corridor and he is not charged with anything either, and neither is she, and neither is a woman in a laboratory, and in about four years the whole of this will be a list of people who were not charged."

---

She read out the fourth line, which was not a charge.

"There is a licensed day room in a private ward on the Wexford Row premises. On the night of the twenty-second an officer of this force entered that building at half past ten at night with no instrument of any kind and no warrant and no consent from anybody with a right to give it, and stayed in that room for an hour and ten minutes, and took nothing and arrested nobody and spoke to nobody. The name at the top of the report is mine. **I did not apply for a warrant for that building. I went in front of a magistrate at Vine Street on the twenty-first of January and asked her to minute that I had not applied for one, and she let me go, and the reason is in her words and I have read it twice and I am not going to paraphrase it.**" She looked up. "I did that because a warrant names a place and a place is not a person, and a person is the only thing that has ever stopped one of these, and because there were three people in that room at half past eleven and I was the only person in this city who would go and stand there."

Nobody said anything.

"The twenty-second of January is on the file," said Nadia Kest, "and so is the eleventh of January, in a first-floor room in Corrie Street, where a member of the public put a hand on a door and it went into a working and it failed, and where I came up a stair with a folded sheet that was not a warrant. Both of those are in there. I have not asked either of them to be taken out and I have not asked for the second one to be left out because it was mine. **A man was not found in either building, and a man has not been found in either building this morning, and I have said the sentence in writing that this force uses when a person cannot be accounted for, and nobody in this corridor is going to make me say anything else about it, and the sister of that man is entitled to hear the sentence and not a paragraph.**"

Sana Okafor got to Vine Street at about half past seven on Wednesday morning and was frightened, and told him so afterwards and not at the time.

The application was one sheet and it was made by a person and not by a solicitor and it named four premises, and the magistrate read it and asked her one question, which was what she was afraid of, and which was whether she was asking for protection for a building or for the people who came into it, and Sana Okafor said that she was asking for a building and that the people who came into it had not asked her for anything, and that she had said so out loud on a Saturday night in a canteen in front of nine people, and the magistrate wrote that down.

It was granted at about twenty past ten on the morning of the twenty-third of January 2030 and it ran **from the twenty-third of January 2030 to the twentieth of February 2030**, and her registration number was at the bottom of it, and the word *temporary* was in the heading in a heavier face than the rest.

"Twenty-eight days," she said. "And somebody has to look at it on the twenty-eighth day, and if nobody looks at it then it is a piece of paper in a drawer in twenty-eight days, and I have had four months of a piece of paper in a drawer and I am not going to have another one without a date on it. I have written a date in it. I have never done that in nineteen years and I did it this morning and I did not tell a soul and I am telling you because you will be the person who remembers it."

She posted the original on the door of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic at about half past twelve with two drawing pins and a length of tape at the top corners, and the range was on it where anybody coming up the path could read it.

---

The four months of Sundays were told about at nine o'clock in a room at the back of that building on Wednesday morning, and Elias Venn was not in it, and Kerry Roche, thirty-one, was told them one at a time by a doctor with the door shut.

She was told the Sundays in September. She was told the Sundays in October. She was told the Sundays in November, and the first one in December, and she said *December* twice and the second time she said it as a question, and Sana Okafor said, "Yes. The first one in December. And that is all of it, and it is not coming back, and I have had a fortnight to find a way of saying that which was kinder and there is not one, and you are thirty-one and you are going to have to plan your weeks around the holes for the rest of your life, and everybody who has ever lost one has said to me *I will manage*, and everybody who has ever said that has meant it, and I have never once said it back."

"I am not going to manage," said Kerry Roche.

"No," said Sana Okafor. "You are not, and that is the better answer, and there are nine people in this city who sat in a room on Tuesday night and came out of it able to say their own name, and they will be exactly where you are in a year's time about something different, and I would like you to meet one of them, and I have not got time this morning and I will do it in a fortnight."

---

The rehabilitation enquiries counter at 6 Verge Row had a bell on it and a leaflet rack and a queue of one, and Maureen Skelland, forty-four, eleven years at the counter, said general questions were free before he had got to the counter, and then took a sheet off him and read four claim numbers off it in about two seconds.

"Two of these are not mine," she said. "They are settlement references and they belong to the fourth floor. Have you got the other two."

He had the other two. She wrote them down and looked at them and then looked at him.

"Where did you get these two."

"Off three counterfoils in a tin in a police corridor at twenty to two this morning, and off a notebook of my own, and I am not going to tell you whose notebook and I am not going to tell you whose tin."

"Fair." She wrote one digit on a slip and pushed it across. "That is the floor, and it is claims assessment, and it is not me and I have never been them. I will tell you the thing that I am allowed to tell anybody, which is that two of the four you have got are not claims, they are settlements, and a settlement is a number that has already been paid, and the fourth floor will not thank you for a settlement number, they will thank you for a claim number, and I do not know the difference and I have asked twice and been told it was not my counter."

---

The fourth floor of 60 Verge Row had a window, a tray system and a fan, and a woman of forty-seven behind the second desk called Imelda Szabo, and she had a jacket over the back of her chair and a cup of tea and the particular manner of a person who has answered the same nine hundred questions a year for six years and is not unkind about any of them.

She did not ask who he was. She asked what the number was, twice, and typed it, and then said, "Settled, and not long ago. It goes out of a rehabilitation claims department and not a general one, and the department is Aegis Mutual's, because the whole of this floor is, and I will tell you that for nothing because everybody asks and nobody knows, and it is not a secret, it is just that nobody has ever asked the right person."

"And who signs the payments out of that department."

"A director." She turned a folder round. "And I am not her, and I am not going to say her name to you, because you would go and look at her, and she has been decent to me, and that is the whole of my reason and I am aware of how small it is."

Then she took out a sheet of paper from a tray, which was not in an envelope, and put it on the desk, and squared it, and did not let go of it.

"This is a settlement sheet. It is not a certificate and I am not going to pretend to you that I could give you a certificate, because I have never seen one in this building and I have been on this floor for six years." She put her finger on a line near the bottom of it. "That is the line. Read that one out."

Elias Venn read it out.

> *The department records that treatment was received. Receipt is recorded by the department on the account and is not a question of memory.*

He read it twice.

"Who is the patient."

"There is no patient on that sheet. There is a patient schedule, which is a ward and a bed and a date, and there is a service code and a fee and an account, and the account has a registration and a premises and a set of books and it is a legal person." She took her hand off the sheet. "That is the line, and it is printed, and it is on every settlement sheet this department has ever issued, and I have never once in six years had anybody read it out to me at my own desk."

"Then let me ask you the question I have come four miles to ask," said Elias Venn. "**Who is allowed to sit in a room where a claim is turned into a certificate?** And the other half of it, which is worse. The claim was signed by somebody, and the certificate was issued by an office, and the treatment was received by nobody who remembers it — so which of those three people is the one who is wrong?"

Imelda Szabo did not answer, and she did not look away, and she picked up her tea and found it cold and put it down again.

"I am not permitted to answer that," she said, "and I am going to tell you why, because you have read a line out to me and you did it properly, and because nobody has ever got as far as the second half of it in six years." She put her hands flat on the desk. "**The department is not a person.** If the department is wrong then there is nobody to be wrong, and if there is nobody to be wrong then there is nothing to put in a file about it, and this building will go on issuing settlement sheets for as long as there is a rehabilitation claims department in it, and the line will go on being printed, and the only person in this city who could ever be shown to have got it wrong is a person, and there is not one on the sheet."

"So it cannot be answered."

"It cannot be answered by anybody who works here, and it can be answered by a person who does not work here, and I have thought about that for about nine seconds and I have decided that I am not going to be the person who finds out how, because I have a flat and a mortgage and I am forty-seven and I have been the person who finds things out twice in my life and both of them cost me somebody." She turned the sheet back round. "I will tell you the other thing, for nothing, because you have read my line out. The money did not go to a person. It went to a registered operator's account, and the operator has a premises and a registration and a set of books and a name, and the name is on every one of those, and the operator has a ward."

"What ward."

"That I am not going to tell you," said Imelda Szabo, "and it is not a secret either, and the reason I am not going to tell you is that in about four days somebody with a warrant and a good coat is going to go and find it out, and you have had four months of doing things on your own and it has cost a woman of sixty-one four nights of a drug and a man of thirty-nine a landing, and I have read about that in a file too, because everybody reads about everything in this city, and I would like you to go and tell somebody with a warrant before you go and tell yourself."

At about ten to ten that morning a sheet of paper with eleven names on it in a doctor's hand, and underneath them a list of eleven things that was longer than the list of names, went back into a drawer in the back office of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic, and the drawer was shut, and nobody photographed it, and it is in there.

At twenty past two in the afternoon of Wednesday the twenty-third of January 2030 Elias Venn sat in a waiting room in Kettle Rows with a book open on his knees and wrote in it, in his own hand, four claim numbers, a date, and the name of an office, and nothing else, and then he put the book in his inside coat pocket and put it next to a plastic wallet with two documents in it that had been unopened since the fourth of November.

The waiting room had nine chairs in it. He had counted them on the step, before he went in, and he had counted them again from the inside, and it was nine both times, and it was not eleven, and he was aware of the number the whole time he was in the room, and nobody in the room could see him doing anything at all.

On the outside of the door, at about the height of a person's hand, there was the temporary protection order, with two drawing pins and a length of tape at the top corners, and the range on it ran **from the twenty-third of January 2030 to the twentieth of February 2030**, and the word *temporary* was in a heavier face than the rest, and her registration number was at the bottom of it, and anybody coming up the path could read it.

Inside, on a board behind polythene at the top of a fan of paper, there was a sheet of paper on its own with four drawing pins in it, and it could not be un-published. Next to it, on the same board, there were four sheets in a doctor's ink, and a registrar's transfer order in a clear sleeve with a bent corner, and a clean rectangle about nine inches by eleven that is a paler colour than the rest of the board with two pin holes in it, and a photocopy of the order that was on the door, with the same date range on it and a name under the doctor's, and the range had an end in it.

Two people were sitting in the room who had been in a working on Tuesday night and who had a name each that they could still say.

One of them was a woman of thirty-eight in a coat over a cardigan with a handbag on her lap, sitting very straight, with both hands round the strap. She had said her own name out loud on the bottom step of a stair at twenty to five that morning to a police officer, and had spelled it, and had been made to spell it again, and about ten minutes ago she had said it once more to nobody in particular, in the voice of a person practising. The other was a woman of fifty-eight from the linen run of the Aldergate Wing, with a blanket over her arm that she had brought for somebody and had not found out who, and she had said hers as well.

Behind the counter a doctor was writing in a book of her own, and it was not a file and it was not a register and it was not a form, and nobody in that room had asked her for it and nobody ever would. She had got the price on the left-hand page and the names on the right, and she wrote down what she had charged, and the number was on a line with a rule under it and there was not a great deal in the way of it, and then she wrote the names she had asked for, one under the other, in her own hand, with the date and the room, because she had asked all eleven of them out loud on Tuesday night with her hand a foot off the nearest shoulder and had written eleven lines, and because she was not going to be the second doctor in nineteen years to have nothing written down.

And on the same page, under the names, she wrote a third line, which was not a price and not a name. It said where the third box was, which is on a wall in a public waiting room behind a sheet of polythene, and that anybody who came in off that path was entitled to stand in front of it and read it, and she wrote that down in the same book and in the same hand, because a police inspector said it on the telephone on a Friday evening to the only other person in this city who writes things down, and he wrote it down, and she has seen what he wrote, and the inspector had been right, and because a woman who has been wrong about her own wall for four months can be right about it in one line.

Then she put the pen down, left the book open on the counter with a sheet of paper over it to keep the draught off, and went out through the back room at about ten past three, and came back into the waiting room a minute later, and did not say anything to anybody, and looked along the row of chairs, and found the one she wanted, and put her hand on the arm of it.

She did not say her name. She put her other hand flat on the arm of the chair so that the woman of thirty-eight could see it coming, and she waited, and the woman got up by herself.
