# Chapter 0091 — The Box in the Bottom Right

The corridor outside the out-of-hours rooms at Vine Street had a bench in it with the paint worn off one end in a long curve, and at twenty past eight on Monday morning the fourteenth of January there was nobody else in the corridor, and Nadia Kest was sitting on the worn end of it with her coat still on.

"I have got my answer," she said.

"You said on the telephone you had a question."

"I have got my answer, and I asked for it at about half past seven this morning in a room with a filing system in it, and I did not tell you, and I am telling you now because you will be standing in a municipal corridor in about two hours and I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out." She had a paper in her hand that was not folded and was not in her coat. "No. A person's consent cannot be produced by somebody who was not present. That is the answer and it took a woman of about fifty to say it and she said it in about four lines and she said it kindly and then she said the other thing, which is the thing I have not been able to get anybody to say to me since November."

"What other thing."

"That the absence is the whole of it. That if the answer were yes then there would be a form, and I would have found the form, and I have looked for nine minutes on a Tuesday and an hour on a Thursday and I have found a page in a procedure manual that tells a practitioner to obtain consent in the presence of a witness and it does not say what the witness is." She put the paper down on her knee. "There is no witness. There is a signature on a piece of paper about six inches long and a person who was not in the room who says the person did say it. And that is not a form, that is a person being brave, and a person being brave is not a category an office can be given."

Elias Venn sat down on the other end of the bench, which was not worn, and put his hands in his coat pockets because it was the fourth day of a cold week in Kettle Rows and the building had a door that opened onto a street it did not shut out.

"Somebody is going to need it in about six weeks," she said.

"You said that on Saturday."

"I did, and I still do, and I have worked out who it is not for and I am not going to tell you, because if I tell you then you will go and look for them, and you are a member of the public with a notebook and a formal reprimand in a drawer and you will look." She looked at him. "I have not asked you about the drawer and I am not going to, and I have not asked you about Sunday and I am not going to, and I would like those two things written down somewhere as a decision I made on purpose and not as something I forgot about."

"They are written down."

"Good." She stood up. "The order ends today. It ends at midnight last night, and the word goes in the box this morning, and you are going to want to be in a building at half past nine and you are not going to be in this one, and I am not going to be able to do a thing about it, and I am telling you now so that you do not spend four minutes working out whether I knew."

---

The Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street shut at three and on a Monday they were open, and the forms and publications counter had a queue in front of it at ten past nine, and Nell Cadby was behind the counter and there was somebody else in the office behind the counter with a drawer open in a desk and a pen on a string.

The clerk was about fifty-five and she was not on the counter and she was not behind the grille, she was in the office with the door open eight inches, and the drawer was open, and the form was in it face up, and there was a box in the bottom right-hand corner with a word in it.

"Two of you," she said. "One of you is a customer and one of you is not, and I have been here since a quarter past seven and I have done the front and I have not done the review."

"I am not a customer," said Elias Venn. "I am a member of the public and I have a question and I am not going to put it in anything."

"Then it is a short one, because the queue is at the grille and I have to be at the grille in about four minutes."

"One question. I would like to know whether anybody telephoned about the form in that drawer."

"About that one or in general."

"About that one or in general."

"No." She did not close the drawer. "There is a telephone on that desk and it did not ring, and it is not going to ring, and the reason it is not going to ring is that the person who could ring it is in her own building and has decided not to be in a municipal building, and I know that she has decided it because her registration number is on the piece of paper in my drawer and it has been on it since the twenty-first of November and I have never met her."

"And the word."

She looked at him for about two seconds and then she pulled the drawer out another inch.

"It is in," she said. "I put it in at half past eight this morning because the box gets filled on the day it ends, and the box is not mine and the word is not mine, and it is not my form, and I have been on this rota nineteen years and the box gets filled on the day it ends and nobody has ever once rung me about it on the day before or the day after, and you may tell anybody you like that a clerk of about fifty-five wrote a word in a box at half past eight on the fourteenth of January and it is *lapsed*, and I will not be sorry about it and I am not going to be asked about it again."

"Why are you not sorry about it."

"Because it is a form and forms get filled and I am a person who fills them." She shut the drawer. "And because there are eleven premises on it and not one of them has a name on it, and it never did have a name on it, and that is what the thing is. It does not name a person. It cannot. It is about eleven addresses and about what may be done in them until a date, and I have read it about four times this year because it is in the wrong drawer, and every one of the eleven is an address and none of them is a name, and you may tell me that is a fault in a form and I will tell you it is a fault in the world and the form is only the part of it I get to see."

Elias Venn stood in the office with the door open eight inches and did not say anything for about four seconds, and then he said, "That is the first useful thing anybody has said to me in a municipal building since the fourth of January and it has taken four months to get it."

"I have not the faintest idea what you are talking about," said the clerk, "and if you come back on Thursday I will sell you a form for nothing, because it is a Monday and it is quiet and I am allowed."

---

Nadia Kest was in a car park on Fenwick Street at half past ten with her hood down and the file of the refusal from October in the passenger footwell where she had put it on purpose, so that it would not be in her hand when anybody saw her.

"Sit down," she said. "I am going to say the whole of it once and then I am going to ring Vine Street, and if you ask me a question in the middle of it I shall stop."

He sat down on the wall.

"October the first. Two hundred words, four numbered paragraphs, refused on the ground that the application does not name the premises. Barred from applying again in the same terms." She turned round in the seat. "That is the first thing and you have had it for three months and you have not built anything on it, and you could have, and I have not told you because I was waiting to see whether you would find it, and you did not, and so I am telling you now and it is over."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is late, and you should be annoyed with me." She put her hands on the wheel without turning the ignition. "A second refusal in the same terms is barred. A first refusal on a ground of jurisdiction is not the same instrument as a first refusal on the merits, because a ground of jurisdiction is about *you* and a refusal on the merits is about *the thing*, and you may bring the same application against the same place twice as many times as you like provided you bring something new, and the something new cannot be a document, because a document is what got the first one refused."

"Then what is the something new."

"Something a person saw." She said it flatly. "A thing that happened in a room, that a person was in, that a person can stand in front of a magistrate and swear to and be believed and be cross-examined about. Not a form. Not a claim number. Not a plan, and I know you have not got a plan because nobody has shown you a plan and if somebody had shown you a plan you would be in my office at half past eight this morning and not on a wall in a car park."

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"That was unkind," Nadia Kest said. "It was also correct, and I have been in this job twenty-two years and I have watched a member of the public walk into a building with a piece of paper four times, and three of those times the piece of paper was useless and one of those times it was evidence and the difference was always a person."

"So the answer is a person who has been in the room."

"The answer is a person who has been in the room, and can be cross-examined, and is not going to be told what to say by you or by me." She turned the ignition and did not put it in gear. "And the other thing, and then I am going to ring Vine Street. The order died at midnight. At about half past eight this morning a clerk of about fifty-five on a rota wrote a word in a box in a drawer in a building you have been in three times. That word is *lapsed*. There is no instrument in this city that puts a police officer through any of eleven rooms from this afternoon, and there is not going to be one by four o'clock, and if somebody in Corrie Street hears about it at lunchtime then whatever is happening in that building on a Tuesday is going to happen with eleven people in a room and no police officer in it."

"What time is Tuesday."

"Tuesday is the twenty-second." She looked at him. "I have not got anything for Tuesday. I have got nothing for Tuesday at ten past eleven this morning and I have had nothing for Tuesday since the first of October, and there is no version of the next eight days in which I have a piece of paper with a name on it, and I am telling you that now so that you can stop waiting for me to be the person who gives you one."

"I was not waiting for you to give me one."

"You were waiting for me to stop saying no," said Nadia Kest, "and I have not stopped, and I am going to ring Vine Street about an order for a different thing entirely, and you are going to walk up that street and I am going to see you on Friday."

---

There were five jobs on the bench in the back room of 14 Kettle Rows on Monday afternoon and he did three of them and the till was standing at fifty credits, which was where it had been since a man of about sixty had put a tenner on a counter on the twelfth of January and told him he had been carrying a clock round the district since November and had been in four shops.

Otto Vahl came up half a flight at about ten past six in the evening with a cup in his hand and did not put the light on, and Elias Venn had the bench and the notebook and a strip of job card the width of a piece of paper in a drawer two miles away, and the drawer was open, and he had not shut it, and it had been open for about four hours, which was longer than it had ever been open.

"Six days," said Otto Vahl from the bottom of the stairs in the dark.

"It has been open since about two."

"It has been open since about two and you have walked past it eleven times, and I have counted, and I am not going to say anything about it, and I have brought you a cup of tea because it is ten past six in January and there is a man in my building who has not eaten." He put the cup on the third step. "And I have one question and then I am going to bed, and it is not about the drawer."

"Ask it."

"What are you doing on Tuesday."

Elias Venn put the strip of job card down on the bench and looked at the stairwell for a while.

"I do not know yet," he said.

"That is the first true thing you have said to me in nine days and I am going to leave it there, because you have told me a thing every day since the fourth of January and I have not asked you one of them." Otto Vahl took his hand off the rail. "Somebody has been in the shop on the parade twice this week asking a man of twenty-five behind a counter whether a room upstairs is let, and the man has told him no twice, and I know that because the man told me, and he told me because I have been buying a paper off him since 2019 and he tells me things. I am not telling you that to be helpful. I am telling you that a man of twenty-five is frightened of somebody and it is not you, because you have a notebook and a coat and you ask questions and he is not frightened of a man who asks questions, he is frightened of a man with a set of keys."

He went up half a flight in the dark and did not say goodnight.

---

Elias Venn got up at half past nine and walked up to the first floor of 4 Corrie Street a day early and on purpose, because a piece of paper had died at midnight in a box in the bottom right-hand corner of a form and there was not going to be another one, and because a man of thirty-nine had said *come back on Tuesday* on the eleventh of January and had said it twice, and a man who means it is rarer in this district than a woman with a rota.

The corridor had a light on at the far end and the bell on the wall on a string that belonged to the dry cleaner's below, and he did not go near it, and there was a light under the door four rooms from the stairs as well as a light in the room at the end of the landing, and the brass bell on that door was brass and about four inches across and it had been polished.

Eamon Brack opened the door before he knocked and he was in a shirt and a jumper with no sleeves because the room was cold and the gas fire was off again.

"You did not telephone," he said.

"No."

"That is three times in five days and I would like to know what the fourth one is going to look like, because if there is a fourth one I would like it to be somebody else, and I would like it not to be a night, because on a night I have got a room and I do not stop." He did not move out of the doorway. "Come in, and I am going to tell you the thing I have been not-telling you since Friday, and I want it on the record that I have not told you anything in six years and I have not asked you for anything and I am not going to."

"You do not owe me a thing, Eamon."

"I know that, and that is why I am doing it." He shut the door and stood against it. "A woman comes at eight on a Tuesday. She has been coming for two years and I have never asked her one question and she has never given me one, and I have never had a name off her and I have never wanted one and I have told you that and it is true." He turned round and put a chair against the door the way a man does when a room has one chair against it for a reason nobody has ever asked him about. "The four times she has come she has written her address in the box on my sheet that says *premises*. It is the same address four times and it is not this building, and I have never asked her why a woman who comes to a room writes where she lives in a box that is for where the work is, and I have never asked her because I do not ask, and I would like that noticed, because I know what it sounds like."

"What is the address."

"That," said Eamon Brack, "is the fourth question, and you are not going to get it tonight, and it is not that I do not trust you, and I am going to give you the reason instead, because the reason is a thing you are going to need and the address is a thing you will only ever use once." He looked at the door and not at him. "I wrote a rule for myself in 2019 and it is one line long and I am not going to recite it to you at about eleven o'clock at night, because you have not earned it and because I have never once recited it to anybody. If I hand you her address on a Monday then you will be at her door by ten on Tuesday morning, and you will ask her, and she has not asked you for anything, and she will not answer you, and in about four years you will stand in a room and say that the address is what did it, and it will not be the address."

"And when do I get it."

"You get it tomorrow morning. Not tomorrow night." He went to the door and put his hand on it and did not open it. "At about a quarter past eleven, and you will knock, and I will make you two cups of tea, because I have made tea for one man in six years and I am not going to begin on a Monday. And then I will recite the rule, and then you will ask me the fourth question again, and then I will answer it, and then you will go and find out what is at the end of it on your own, because there is nothing you can do with it in this room and there never has been."

"And it is always a Tuesday," he said, to the door. "I have said that to you and I have said it twice, and I have not said it to anybody else in six years, and I would like somebody to know that I said it and I would like it to be on a Tuesday, because that is when it is."
