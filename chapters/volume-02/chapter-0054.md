# Chapter 0054 — The Appointment Book

The three conditions were made at the door, on the landing, in the voice of somebody who had made them before to a different person and had refined them.

"Nothing with a form on it. Nothing with a stamp. Nothing with a number, and that includes a receipt."

"Nothing with her name in it," said Elias Venn. "Not her first name, not her street, not her surname. If she is in this book as a number, she is a number."

"If she is in this book as a number then in three years somebody has that number and a date and a fee and a method, and a date and a fee and a method is a person." Rhoda Nesbit held the door. "You do not write anything while she is in the room. You can sit there with a pencil in your hand and look like a man who is helping, which you are, because a man in a room stops a room from becoming anything else. But if I see a line go into a book while a client is here I will put you out, and I will do it politely, and I will not take your name off you, and I will not take a session off a woman who has arranged her Friday around a bus."

"And I do not ask her anything."

"You do not ask her anything, you do not look at her, and if she asks you a question you say *she will answer that* and point at me, and that is all, and it is because in this room I am the only person who has agreed to be responsible for what happens in it and you have not agreed to anything, because you cannot, because you are not a policeman and you are not a clinician and you are a man in a shop with a notebook and I have known you for two hours."

Elias Venn put the notebook in his pocket.

"That is the right answer and I am not going to argue with it."

"Then come in at half past three and not at four, because she comes on the bus and if the bus is late I have to be able to say her name out loud in the corridor and I would rather do it while you are on the stairs."

---

The client came at four minutes past four and was thirty-one and wore a coat she had not taken off, and she sat in the client chair and put her handbag on her knees and held it there with both hands for about twenty minutes while Rhoda Nesbit asked her four questions and wrote the answers in a book with a green cover.

Elias Venn sat by the window with a pencil in his hand and did not write in it, and after about ten minutes he stopped holding the pencil as well, and put it on the table, and read the wall calendar with the Fridays ringed in ink, and then read the plate with the three clips on it from where he sat, because there was nothing else to do and because a man who looks at a woman for fifty minutes is a man who is in the way.

He noticed four things and wrote none of them.

He noticed that Rhoda Nesbit asked the fourth question and the woman answered it in one sentence without stopping, and the answer was a name, and the name was not the name the woman had been using for the person in the chair.

He noticed that the woman put her hand on the back of her own neck when the name was said, and kept it there, and that Rhoda Nesbit saw it and did not comment on it and moved a card game out of the way instead.

He noticed that it took fifty minutes, and that nothing happened for the first nineteen of them that he could see, and that the two of them talked about a kitchen, a piano against a wall, a girl called Aoife who was nine three years ago and is twelve now, and about a bus.

And he noticed that Rhoda Nesbit, at about the twenty-sixth minute, wrote a line in the green book and then stopped and put her pen down and asked the woman a fifth question which was not one of the four, and which was: *and what do you want to be able to say to her on Thursday.*

The woman cried for about a minute and a half, and then said, "Her name," and Rhoda Nesbit wrote that down too, and then read the whole of the page back aloud in flat sentences, and the woman said *yes* to all of it, and at eleven minutes past five she stood up and went out, and the door shut, and the shop below was closed, and the light in the dry cleaner's window was off.

Rhoda Nesbit stood with her back to the window for a moment with her hands in her pockets.

"There," she said. "Fifty minutes. And I am not going to be able to tell you on Thursday whether it held, and neither can she, and last time I did it she came back on the Friday and it had gone by the Wednesday and she came back the Friday after that, and that is the trade, and I have never been able to make that sound like anything other than what it is."

"You did not charge her."

"I am not charging her again until the thirtieth of November," said Rhoda Nesbit, "because I did it on Friday the sixteenth and I have read the tariff and so have you, and there is a note about it, and I have never once in six years ignored that note and I am not starting in November to be a person who ignores a note." She sat down on the client chair. "Now. The book."

---

It was a hard-backed notebook with a green cover and a rubber band round it and about two hundred pages in it, and Rhoda Nesbit put it on the table between them and took the band off, and then she did not open it, and she put her hand flat on the front of it.

"I am going to say eleven things to you and then I am going to read them, and if I read them and you have written down nine of them I am going to ask you to burn the nine, in front of me, and you are going to do it, and I am not being dramatic. If you have nine of them then somebody can take nine of them. I have been the somebody who takes nine of them. I did it for twelve years with other people's forms and I did not have a face for any of them."

"I have a book with nothing in it."

"Then here is what I am going to say, and it is a trade and it is six weeks and I will read it out and you can write the dates and the numbers and nothing else.

"Nineteenth of October, eleven credits, a name for a face. Twenty-sixth of October, eleven credits, a name for a face, same woman. Second of November, eleven credits, the same. Ninth of November, eleven credits, the same, and a man came in the morning, eleven credits, a road he drove on for nine years. Fifth of November, twenty credits because she had eleven, a dog's name. Twelfth of November, eleven credits, a woman in the parade, and that was a Monday because her husband was away and I wrote it down as a Monday. Thirtieth of October, eleven credits, a birthday. Twenty-first of October, eleven credits, a man from the outer wards who could not travel, and I went to him, and that was not a Friday and it was not in this room."

"That is eight."

"It is eight lines and there are eleven things, and I am not going to give you the working out, because the working out is the book, and if you had the working out you would have the book." She turned the book a quarter turn. "The ninth is the ninth, the sixteenth and the twenty-third, all of them in October, and a woman who came three times, and I will come to that one because it is the one I have not been able to put anywhere. The tenth is the ninth of October, at her own kitchen, four lines and a fee, which I will not read out to a man in a shop. And the eleventh is the fifteenth of October, sixty credits, a man I have never seen before or since, and he did not tell me a name, he told me what he wanted, which was that I should hold an order I had been given."

---

She opened the book and turned it round so that it was facing him, and she did not let go of it.

"That one cost sixty credits," she said, "which is the published price of the published procedure, and I have never charged anybody the published price of anything in my life, and I took the envelope and I did the session, and the session was correct. I want that on your paper, if you have a piece of paper. The session was correct."

"Written down."

"Written down in a way I have seen written down, and I have read four hundred pages of other people's ways." She turned the page a little. "He did not want to be helped. That is not how I would say it to a client. He wanted to be told the order he had been given, twice, and then to be asked about it afterwards, and he wanted the asking to be done by somebody who was not him. And when it was over he thanked me and gave me a card and said that if I ever needed anything done in this trade again to use the number on the card, and the number on the card is not a telephone number."

"What is it."

"It is a four-line card." Rhoda Nesbit put her finger on it. "There is a line for the address, which was a room and a floor and a street in Tanners Row, and there is a line for the date, and there is a line for the fee, and there is a line at the foot that says **SUPPLY** in capitals, and that is all four lines, and I have had it in this book for four weeks and I have not rung it and I am not going to ring it, and I am telling you about it because you are going to find out about it in about a year otherwise and I would rather you found out from me."

Elias Venn did not ask her a single question about the man. He sat with his hands flat on the table on either side of a notebook with nothing in it and he did not ask, because a week ago in a front room in Corrie Street he had written *a man* in his own book because that was what she was entitled to have written, and he was not going to undo that on the first evening it was inconvenient.

He wrote one line in the margin, in his own book, with the date and the number of the line, and not a street and not a face.

*Sixty credits, the fifteenth of October, and the word Supply at the foot of a four-line card, and a man who did not want to be helped. A company is a better shield than a man and a worse witness.*

"That's the whole of what I've got," he said, "and I have not asked you anything, and I am not going to, and I want to tell you why, because you gave me three conditions at the door and I have kept all of them and it costs me something."

"Go on."

"There is a trading name in a police schedule in this city. I have never read the schedule, I am not on the file, and I am not going to be told what it says, and if somebody told me this week I would be a man who has a name from a police document and no way of having come by it lawfully, and in about two years that is the whole of a career." He shut the book. "The day I am the sort of man who asks to be told, I am the sort of man somebody hires. I would rather lose the man than the woman."

Rhoda Nesbit took her hand off the book.

"You know that is the first sensible thing anybody in this city has said to me about it," she said, "and I have had it said to me by a registrar and a magistrate and a woman in an office at Concord Hall and none of them was being interesting on purpose."

"Eleven," said Elias Venn.

"What?"

"You said you were going to say eleven things and then read them, and I have written the dates and the numbers of all eleven, and you read eight out and I did not write one word of it, and you did not check the book once. I would like you to notice that. It means you had already decided whether to trust me, and you decided before I came up the stairs."

"I decided while you were apologising on the landing," said Rhoda Nesbit, "and I have been wrong about people about four hundred times and once I was wrong the other way, and I have not told anybody about the once."

---

She read the eleven lines out to him and he wrote the dates and the numbers and nothing else, and when she had finished he said the eleven lines back to her in the same order, and she listened to all of it with her arms folded.

Then she took the book back and turned to a page about three-quarters of the way in, and there were four words on it on their own line, and the rest of the line was the date and the fee and a name he was not given, and the four words were these.

> *came three times. did not come*

"The last of the three was the twenty-third of October," said Rhoda Nesbit. "The ninth, the sixteenth and the twenty-third, and then nothing. And I wrote that line on the second of November, when I finally got round to writing the three of them up properly, and I have been carrying that line round since, and I have told nobody, and you are a policeman who is not a policeman and you have got a book with nothing in it, so there it is."

"You do not know why she did not come."

"I have four possibilities and I hate three of them and I have a fifth one I am not going to write down." She put the band round the book. "One is that it worked and she went away, and that is what happened last night and I do not know yet. Two is that it cost more than eleven credits when it came to the point and she did not have eleven credits. Three is that somebody told her to stop coming. Four is that somebody told her she should not have been coming, which is worse and cheaper and much more common than three." She shut the book. "And five is that she came to somebody else, and that somebody else did something, and she will not come back here because of it. And I have not written down five, and I am not going to, and if you write down five I will tell you to take it out."

"I am not going to write down five."

"I know. That is why I read you the other four."

She put the book on the sideboard, under the plate with the three clips, and then she stood up and went to the dresser and opened a drawer and took out a tin, and it was a tin that had held something else for about thirty years, and it was not the tin on the fourth floor of a building in Kettle Rows, and it was the second one she had shown him in two days.

"There is another thing and it is the last thing tonight," she said, "and I have been sitting on it since the Wednesday, and I have decided that a man who comes up four minutes early and puts a pencil down and never writes a line is the sort of man I can sit on a stool and talk to.

"I used to write counterfoils. Twelve years, every session, three in ten, because that is what we did in claims, and it is the only piece of administration I have ever been any good at, and I have never stopped. They are in a tin. There are about forty. And on three of them, on the top line, in a different hand from mine, there is a number, and I did not write a number on any of them, and I did not know for about a year and then I did know for about a month and now I have gone back to being stupid about it, which is what happens to me every time."

"What sort of number."

"A long one, with a letter in the middle of it, and a service code under the number, and my little boxes underneath." She put the tin on the table and did not open the lid. "I have had a lot of people look at my things in my own flat since Tuesday. You are the fifth. Two of them were from the licensing people and they were not unkind and they were not allowed in the room with the book, and one of them was a woman from a claims office who knew what a number was and told me the number was well formed, and told me that in her line, and then apologised, and then said something else about my kitchen that was clearly meant to take my mind off it."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her it was my number," said Rhoda Nesbit, "which was not true, and she wrote that down, and she was not allowed to write that down, and I watched her not write it, and I have thought about that every day since." She put her hand on the lid of the tin. "I am not opening this tonight. You have not seen the sheet and you are not seeing this either, because you have not got any reason yet, and when you have got a reason I am going to make you say it out loud before I open the lid, and it is going to be a sentence, and I am going to make you write it in your own book and not in mine."
