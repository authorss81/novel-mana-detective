# Chapter 0079 — Two Things and Not One

He knocked once on the second joint up on the door of number twenty-two on Christmas afternoon, at about twenty past three, and it was not about the third floor, and he said so before the door had finished opening.

"It is not about the third floor," he said.

"You knocked once, which is the same as you, and it is a Tuesday, and I have not got a great deal else on." Vilma Craddock had the door on the latch and not the bolt and she had a cardigan over a chair in the front room behind her and a cup on the table with a teaspoon still in it. "Say the thing and then you will go and I will put the bolt on, and I have not had a bolt on since the summer."

"I am going to tell you about a list," said Elias Venn, "and it is not on a piece of paper I can give you, and it is not on a piece of paper anybody can give you, and I want you to know before you decide whether you want to know.

"There is a list on this street. It is in a building four streets from here, in a clinic, in a drawer, in a room with a ledger in it. It has got eleven names on it. It has got a column on the other side that says where to send things, and that column is full, and about half of it is addresses of houses that belong to somebody else. Somebody at some point wrote your building into that column as a place where a woman of sixty-one could be reached, and you did not know that, and neither did the woman, and nobody has ever asked you about it, and I have now told you, and I am not able to untell you, and I am sorry about that, and I have thought about whether to tell you for six days and I have decided that a thing that is about your own address is yours."

She did not close the door.

"Who is the woman."

"I am not going to tell you that."

"That is not the answer I was worried about," said Vilma Craddock. "The answer I was worried about was somebody who told me a name. Come in and shut it behind you, because the stairs are cold and there is a fire and I am sixty-one and I have not had a person in this front room since the summer."

---

The front room of number twenty-two is eleven feet by nine and has a chair with a cushion in it, a table with a cloth on it, a lamp, a landing behind with the stairs going up to the second floor, and the hall cupboard on the wall, and the carrier bag in the hall cupboard, and Elias Venn looked at the floor in front of the hall cupboard and at nothing else on that wall for about two hours.

"There is tea in the pot and it has been in the pot since four and it is strong," said Vilma Craddock. "Take the cup with no writing on it. The other one is a cup somebody left in a pub in about 1988 and it says *Denny* on the bottom of it and I have never in my life thrown it out and I have never once used it."

He took the cup with no writing on it.

"What are you going to do with what you have told me."

"Nothing at all, for about four months."

"That is the correct answer and I am going to tell you why, and then I am going to tell you the second half of it, and then I am going to make you some more tea." She sat down in the chair with the cushion. "The half of it that is correct is that a person cannot be un-listed by anybody who has not told them they were on it, and now I know, and there is no version of this where I do not know, and the only thing I can do about it is nothing at all and be extremely dull about it for a long time, and I have got a good deal of practice at being dull about things.

"And the half of it that is not correct is that you have done to me this afternoon the exact thing that the person who writes my column down there does every fortnight when he comes round with a form, except that he does it to a woman he has never spoken to and you have done it to a woman you have spoken to twice, and I would like you to notice that you did it on purpose, and that you had six days, and that on one of those six days you must have considered not doing it, and I would like to know which day."

"The fourth," said Elias Venn.

"The fourth," she said. "Right. Well, you are the first person in about eleven years who has told me the day."

---

They had the tea, and the lamp, and about forty minutes of nothing in particular in which she told him about the man from number nineteen who had taken the dog out at six in the morning in November and had stood on the corner in the rain for about eleven minutes looking at a gate, and that everybody on the street had decided that he was doing something and nobody had done anything, and that he was a night man and he had come home early.

"Can I ask you one thing that is about the building."

"You can ask me one thing about the building and you can ask me one thing about the weather," said Vilma Craddock. "Ask me about the bag and I shall put the bolt on and it will be the last conversation we have."

"How many flats."

"Four, and the shop, and the yard, and that is the whole of it. Number nineteen, number twenty-one, this one, and the one at the top with the widow in it who is eighty-nine and has not been out since the summer before last." She held the pot over the cups. "Number nineteen is two floors down from me and number twenty-one is two floors up and the top one is three, and the yard stair does not go anywhere anybody in this street has needed it to go since before I came, and that is a sentence I have said to about four people in eleven years and the last one was a man in July who wrote it in a notebook."

"Does the sound carry up from the yard."

"The sound carries up the yard stair beautifully," said Vilma Craddock, "and it comes out on my landing, because the landing is where the stair arrives, and I have heard somebody go up that stair in the summer of every year since I have lived here, and I have not once gone up, and I am not going to tell you that I have not gone up because I am frightened, because I have never established that, and it might be that I have simply never wanted to, and I have had eleven years to work out which of those two it is and I have not, and I have made my peace with not having."

She put the pot down.

"What I will tell you," she said, "because you have been in my front room for two hours and you have not asked me one question about it, is this. There is a ceiling over my head and there is a floor over that, and between them is a landing and a room, and I have had a cupboards' worth of worry about that room since the summer and I have had it in a very particular form, which is that I do not know whether anybody in this building has ever gone up and found it empty. Nobody has ever come and told me. Nobody has ever gone up in front of me. It has simply been there, like a bad tooth, for five months, and the whole of the difference between me and a woman in a first-floor room over a dry cleaner, who I have never met, is that she is afraid of somebody and I am afraid of a ceiling, and I would not be so certain which of us has the worse of it if I were you."

Then he said the thing he had come to say, which he had not come to say, and which came out of him because she had given him a cup and a Tuesday and about forty minutes of nothing.

"Who pays," said Vilma Craddock.

"Who pays."

"That is what you have been holding since you sat down. You have had a face on you since the twentieth of December like a man carrying a tray, and it is not the third floor and it is not your shop." She turned the cup round. "Who is paying for what you have been doing, since you are a man with no job that anybody in this building can see."

Elias Venn put the cup down on the table beside the cloth.

"A doctor took ninety-odd pages off her own wall," he said, "on Thursday, in front of two of her staff, and she is not going to be thanked for it and she is never going to be told by anybody why it was the right thing to do. A ward sister of sixty-one has a minute in a folder in a building four miles away that says she said yes to a stranger, and the minute also says something about her that is the first thing in this city that has ever protected a person for making a record, and she is off the book until the seventh of January and she has four photographs in a drawer that she is frightened of. A woman of fifty-two in a dispensary has a woman of sixty-one who is not going to have a drug for four days because I said a thing out loud in a back room on Friday, and she is not going to be told why. A woman of sixty-four has kept a book of a Tuesday soup for nine years and there are eleven addresses on the back of it in a hand that is not hers, and she has never asked and she is never going to ask, and she is not going to be thanked. A woman of sixty-three has a ledger, and eleven names, and a rack, and it took her nine seconds to put the two pieces of paper side by side, and she has gone home and she is angry with me. And I have got a till with forty credits in it that stood at thirty-one for a fortnight, and I do not get paid for any of this, and I have a finding on me from a panel on Bell Street and a shop that cannot lend anybody a bench."

He stopped.

Vilma Craddock did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"Now you tell me what you have just done," she said.

"I have told you who is paying."

"You have told me who is paying because you could not carry it by yourself and because I am the only person you have spoken to in three weeks who has not asked you for anything." She put her hand flat on the tablecloth. "**Eleven years I have lived in this building and I have told two people nothing and asked nobody for anything. The number of people who have come to this door and told me a thing and asked for nothing is two. One of them is a doctor in a waiting room and the other one is a man with a cup of tea in his hand who has just spent a whole afternoon giving away other people's names to a stranger because he wanted to put it somewhere.**

"So that is two. And it is about the third of an hour since you came in and you have not asked me one single question, and I have had a very nice afternoon, and I am going to tell you the one thing I have been thinking about while you were talking, and then I am going to put the kettle on."

---

"I have been thinking," said Vilma Craddock, "that you are the first person who has ever come in here and told me the truth about a thing that was in his own pocket and left it in his own pocket. That is all. Nineteen minutes since you knocked and a bag in my hall cupboard with a number on it and eleven years on it, and you have not asked about the bag once, and I have been watching you not ask about the bag for about two hours, and I want to say that it cost you something, because I have watched two men in my life not ask me about things and one of them was thinking about it and one of them was thinking about himself, and there is a difference and I could tell you which one you are but I am not going to, because you would use it."

She got up and filled the kettle.

"You will not get the bag," she said. "And you will not get the number, and you have not asked me for the number, which I notice, and I am not going to give you the number as a reward for not asking, because that would make it a bargain and it is not a bargain, it is a shut door, and it is going to be a shut door in February as well as in December."

"That is correct," said Elias Venn, "and it is the only thing in this case I have never once been tempted to change."

"Is it."

"It is the only thing I have never once been able to act on," he said. "There is a difference and I have been on the wrong side of it in about nine other places since September and I have written all nine of them down."

She plugged the kettle in.

"Christmas Day," she said. "I have had a mince pie and you have had a cup of tea, and the gas is on the meter, and you will go home now, and if you are in this street in January you will knock once and I will decide."

---

He did not sleep on Christmas Day. He went up at half past six and put the paraffin heater on and put the cat out of the drawer where it had made a bed in a bag of offcuts, and he lay down on the bench in the back room at eight o'clock with the bell off and a card on the door, which is a thing he had done on the eighth of December, and he slept for nine hours and woke at five o'clock in the morning on Boxing Day and could not get back to sleep.

He got up and put the kettle on the ring behind the counter at twenty past five because a man awake at twenty past five in a cold shop has to be doing something, and there was one job on the bench, and it was a lamp base with a terminal block in it and a bracket with forty years of paint on the screw, and he had done that one already, and the other one was a toaster that tripped a fuse, and it was a short in the crumb tray and it took twenty minutes and it was not difficult and it was not interesting and it was the fourth time in a fortnight he had done a thing competently that had nothing to do with the case.

At half past five, with the shop dark and the gas on and the till with forty credits in it under a folded cloth where he had put it on Christmas Eve, he wrote in the back of the notebook the four lines he had promised himself he would write, and they were not about the tin and they were not about the form.

> *The twenty-fifth of December. Nine hours, the second time since the seventeenth of September, and a bench, and a bell off, and a card on the door.*
> *The middle of January is sixteen days away. A woman of thirty-one and a girl of nine are to be told then and not before, and a woman on Marshall Street has it in a diary and has never once said the word out loud in front of me, and I have not asked her, and I am not going to.*
> *A hand stopped in the middle of its own name in a yard on Fenwick Street and I have got four sentences about it and no page, and a person who is not permitted to give me anything told me in a corridor not to go and look for the hand, and I have written her sentence down and hers.*
> *And I said a thing out loud in a room on the twenty-first of December that was not true, and a woman has lost four days of a drug, and it is not going to be put back by anybody, and there is no version of the twenty-first of December in which I said the other thing first, and the whole of what I have is that I will not do it again, and I cannot promise that either, and I am writing it down anyway because a man who does not write it down will decide in about four years that it did not happen, and it happened, and I said it.*

He shut the book and put it on the shelf, and the certificate was on that shelf above the back bench on its own with a receipt from the municipal building six inches from it, and he did not look at either of them, and there was nothing on that shelf he could not have told you the date of.

Somebody came up the stairs from the ground floor at ten past six, in the dark, without turning the light on.

"That the gas," said Otto Vahl. "I heard it go on at about twenty past five and I have been awake since five and there is no point in either of us pretending."

"You were not up."

"No. And I have not been down in nine days, and you have not been up, and that has been the arrangement since September, and I would like it understood that it is an arrangement and not an accident, because an accident can be stopped by somebody being difficult." Otto Vahl had a cup in his hand and he was sixty-eight and it was half past six on Boxing Day. "So I have come up one flight to put a cup on your bench and to say that I have had the same nine hours of nothing that you have had and that I am sixty-eight and I am going back to bed, and that there is a man coming at nine about the lamp base and I told him on Saturday."

"Then I will be down at nine."

"I know you will," said Otto Vahl, from the bottom of the stairs, in the dark, with a cup. "That is the arrangement. Put the light on when you come down. You have not had the light on in this building since the summer and it is the only thing in it I have ever been able to complain about."
