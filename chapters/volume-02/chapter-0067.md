# Chapter 0067 — The Ward Book

The ward book lived on a shelf under the counter at the nurses' station on the ground floor of the Aldergate Wing, and it was one volume, and it had a spine of blue cloth with a white label on it that said **WARD BOOK — ALDERGATE / BRACKWELL / SANDMARTIN / THIRLMERE**, and the label had been retyped twice.

Elias Venn stood in front of it on Sunday the ninth of December at ten past eleven and asked Sister Ivy Warhurst to let him look at it, which is the first request he had made of a stranger since the third of November, and he made it badly, and it worked.

She was sixty-one and she had been at the wing for twenty-six years and she had come on at half past seven and she was still there at a quarter past three because the day sister was late and she had not gone home.

"No," she said.

"I have not said what I want from it."

"You have got a face on you that has been asking people for things since September and you have never once asked anybody for anything that was not yours, and that face is a bad face to wear in a ward, and I am going to say a thing and you are going to hear it and then you can decide whether you are the sort of man who needed to hear it."

"All right."

"**I am the person who signs for that book.** My name is in the front of it, and the date I last signed it is in the front of it, and if a page comes out of that book and does not have my name under it, then the page is my responsibility and I have never in twenty-six years had that come back to me from an office." She put her hand flat on the counter. "On the Monday I would have said no and I would have been right to and I would have felt good about it. On a Sunday, with a Saturday-day book and a day sister who is late, and with a man standing there who is not a doctor and not a policeman and has told me neither, on Wednesday, on a chair in a corridor, in front of a nurse and a doctor and three patients —"

"You have been told that. I did not know you knew."

"Of course I knew. I was eleven feet away and I have been doing this for twenty-six years, and I would have stopped it in about four seconds if I had not wanted to hear the rest of it." She took the book off the shelf. "Come round this side. You do not touch it, and I turn the pages, and you do not go through it on your own, because the day sister is forty minutes late and if she comes back and finds a member of the public holding a ward book in a ward I will have to write a form about it, and I am too old to write forms about strangers."

---

It is a book of ruled lines and not much else, and it is forty-one years of four wards, and it is the most boring document Elias Venn has ever read.

One line a day for every occupied bed. Date. Ward. Bed. Name. Admitted. Discharged. Procedure number, where a procedure had been given. And two columns at the right-hand end headed **remarks** and **initials**, and the remarks column is where everything that is not a rule went, and the initials column is where the person who wrote it went, and a ward book is mostly initials.

He read about forty minutes' worth before he asked for the page, and when he asked for it he asked for it the way you ask a nurse for something on a ward.

"What I want to see is Thirlmere for September."

"Which September."

"The last one."

She did not ask why. She turned to a divider and back, and back, and back, and found it, and put her finger on the top of the page.

**11 — 19 SEPTEMBER. THIRLMERE CLOSED — WATER OFF AT RISING MAIN. SIX RESIDENTS TO DAY ROOM, MAIN CORRIDOR. WARD REMAINS SIGNED AND SEALED, RESIDENCE AND EFFECTS ON SITE. (MGR)**

Six lines, one for each of the six, in a column headed **temporary placement**, and the six names in six beds' worth of handwriting, and against the six names, in the remarks column, in one hand, a line: *moved 11/9 am. day rm. as above.*

And that was the week. There was nothing on the page for the rest of it. The water came back on the nineteenth and the six went back at four o'clock in the afternoon and there is a line under the nineteenth that says **WATER RESTORED. RESIDENTS TO WARD 15:40.**

Eight days, and it is a page like any other page, and there was nothing on it about anybody being anywhere else.

But in the margin, in a different pen, in a smaller hand, hard up against the stitching where the spine had come away in about four places, there was a second entry against the third name, and it was not in the ward's book-keeping hand at all, and it said:

> *3.30 — own transport. 1 hr. Returned 6.10.*
> *Att. external session. 4 Corrie St. 4.00. 1 hr. Fee 60.00 — **to be recovered.***

Elias Venn read it four times and did not touch the book and did not say a word for about a minute and a half.

"Keep going," said Sister Warhurst. "You have gone the colour of that wall and I have seen a man go the colour of that wall twice in this building and both times it was money and both times it was a form, and I would like to know which it is."

"Somebody has written a fee into a ward book," said Elias Venn, "and a street, and an hour, and the words *to be recovered*, and there is no rule on this page that says a fee goes in a ward book."

"There is no rule that says a fee does not go in a ward book either, and I have been looking at this book for twenty-six years, and about one line in four hundred has a money word in it, and they are all to be recovered, and they are all for a woman who has had no money from her husband since March." She turned the page back. "I have got three of those in the last six months and I have not asked about one of them, because a ward sister who asks where a patient went for an hour is a ward sister who is told where a patient went for an hour every week of her life, and in four months she has nothing. I have made that trade. I made it the first week."

"Who is the third name."

"That," said Ivy Warhurst, "is the part you will not get, and I am going to tell you why in one sentence and then I am going to do something I have never done in this building. **A person who has been in a ward and has come back is a person who has been given a great deal of trust, and you have not earned it, and you are not going to earn it today, and if you had earned it I would still not give it to you in a corridor.**"

"Then do not tell me. Photograph it."

---

"Photograph it," said Ivy Warhurst, "is the first sensible thing anybody has said at this counter in about four years, and I have been thinking about it since Monday and I did not want to do it, and I am going to do it, and I am going to do it my way and you are not going to like my way."

There was a box camera on the top of a cupboard in the office behind the station. It was older than she was, it had a leather case with a strap, and it had been in that office since before she came.

"It is mine," she said. "It belongs to the wing and it is mine in the sense that I am the one who cleans it and it is the only thing in this building I have ever been allowed to be responsible for that is not a person."

"Agreed."

"You do not touch the book. I do not touch the book. **I do not move the book.** I move the camera. Four frames: the page, the corner with the margin, the whole week, and the front of the book with my name in it. Then I develop them in the darkroom in the basement of the old fever hospital, which is a room I have a key to and which is the only room in this building I have a key to, and I will have them on Monday."

"And they stay here."

"They stay here," said Ivy Warhurst. "In the office, in the drawer, in the envelope, and they are not for the department and they are not for a file and they are not for anybody who rings me up. If you want a print, you cannot have a print."

"I did not ask for a print."

"You looked at me when I said it," she said, "and you did not ask, and that is the reason I am doing it, and I have been a ward sister for twenty-six years and I have never known a man not to ask for a print."

---

She left him in the day room at ten past three with a cup of tea from the canteen and a laminated card that said **VISITOR — NO WARD ROUND, NO BEDS, NO TREATMENT**, which Petronella Osei at the front desk had given him on the Wednesday, apparently, and had not mentioned to anybody.

There were nine people in it. The man of sixty with the cup was at a table with a sheet of paper and a pencil, working on something with two hands. The man of forty had a different magazine. The woman of seventy-nine had the crossword board and had got to eleven across and had left four across blank for two days running and was not bothered.

And at the table by the window, on the side with the good light, there was a woman of about eighty-one with a blanket over her knees and a jigsaw tin in front of her and no jigsaw in it, and she had one of the nine missing pieces in her hand, and she was holding it up at the window.

"Aren't you the one who was in here on Monday with the physiotherapist," she said. "You've got the face of a man who has been on the end of a corridor in a blanket."

"I was."

"Sit down," said Doris Sowerby. "You've not got anybody, have you."

"No."

"Then sit down, you're in the way." She put the piece down in the tin, on top of the others, in the wrong place, and then took it out again. "That's a boat."

"That is a boat."

"That's a boat's *shadow*," said Doris Sowerby. "I have been fighting that piece since the twenty-ninth of November and I have put it in three times now and I have got a jigsaw with nine pieces missing and two of them are under a radiator in this building and one of them is in a man's mouth in Sandmartin and I know about that one because he told me."

Elias Venn sat down on the other side of the table, and there was a nurse at the far end of the room refilling a jug and a man of forty reading a magazine, and the day room door was open, and he stood up and said what he said in a corridor on Wednesday, and said it in the same words, because a man who says a thing differently in two rooms is a man who has decided which room matters.

"I am not a doctor and I am not a police officer," he said. "I do not read people. I have never read a person in my life and I am not going to, and I have not been in the same material as anybody in this room for more than a second, and I am saying it out loud because you are all here and it is the only reason I am allowed to be."

"Say it again, but louder, there's a man on the television," said Doris Sowerby, and he said it again, and the man with the cup wrote it down on his sheet of paper without looking up, and the nurse at the far end of the room said "thank you" to nobody.

"Right," said Doris Sowerby. "Now. You've not got anybody, so you're not going to ask me anything, are you."

"I was not going to ask you anything."

"Well, I am going to tell you, and that is not the same thing, and if I am telling you and you have not asked, then you cannot say afterwards that I was got at." She put her hands flat on the tin. "I was on Thirlmere in September and the water went off at about ten in the morning on the eleventh," said Doris Sowerby, "and they moved six of us into the day room for eight days and gave us a fire bucket and a television that only gets the one channel. And I was the only one of the six who went out that week. I went out because the ward had a woman come round with a list, and I have never once been on a list in my life, and I went out because I wanted to be on one. I have been in this building since the twenty-eighth of November with the hip and I am doing all right and I shall be out before Christmas."

"Where did you go."

"Corrie Street. First floor over a dry cleaner. A woman called herself a practitioner and she was fifty-eight and she was better than anybody I have ever paid." Doris Sowerby looked at the jigsaw tin. "An hour. Two hours, maybe. It was sixty credits and I paid it out of an envelope, because she does not do anything that has a card on it, and there is nothing at the end of it, and I have been to eleven of these in my life and I have had a certificate from about two of them and I have not got a certificate from her and I did not know to ask."

"What was it for?"

"I have no idea," said Doris Sowerby, "and that is the honest answer and I have had eight weeks to find a better one. It was an hour on a Tuesday afternoon and it cost sixty credits, and I came back at ten past six and I have no idea what was done to me and I could not tell you if you had a doctor in here with a light."

Elias Venn sat at a table in a day room on Wexford Row with a jigsaw tin in front of him and did not ask the next question, and it took him about eleven seconds not to ask it, and he wrote one line in his own book at the table with a pencil off the crossword board.

> *11 September 2029. A patient of this ward, aged eighty-one, out for an hour on the Tuesday of the shut week, to a first-floor room at 4 Corrie Street, at four o'clock, for sixty credits, and she does not remember what was done to her and she has no certificate. The line in the ward-book margin says the same afternoon and says the fee was to be recovered. The treatment in the room was real and the bed in the certificate was shut.*

He tore the page out.

He did not put it in the book. He put it on the table and pushed it across to Sister Warhurst, who had come in behind him with a jug and had been standing there for some part of it, and she read it standing up.

"Take it back," she said.

"It does not have a name in it."

"I have read it, and it does not have a name in it, and I am not having it, because if I have it then a man has been in this ward and taken a note off a table, and the note has no name on it, and the next person who asks me about it is going to be somebody very clever about the not having a name on it." She put her hand on the page. "I will pin it in the office drawer with the four prints, and there is a date on it, and in nine years it is a piece of paper in a drawer in a shut ward, and that is the correct place for it and it is the only place in this city where it is safe to be."

"Then it is yours," said Elias Venn. "I am not writing it anywhere else and I am not writing the name, and if anybody asks me on Monday I have not got it."

"You have got it."

"I have got it in the same place I have got the certificate number, which is nowhere a person can get it out of me except me, and I am the only one of me."

Sister Warhurst folded the page once and put it in her apron pocket, and looked at the two women at the table by the window for a moment before she said anything else.

Then she said, "It is the third one down from the corner, and it has been in that tin since the eighth of October, and I have got the box in my bedroom at home because it is mine. I have never been able to see the picture and neither of you is going to be able to tell me what is in it, and I have stopped minding."
