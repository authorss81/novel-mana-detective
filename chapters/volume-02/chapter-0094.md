# Chapter 0094 — A Man Who Lets a Room

Ivor Skellern was behind the counter of the shop at nine Calder Place at about ten past eleven on Thursday the seventeenth of January with a set of keys on a ring on his belt and a cigarette he was not going to smoke, and Kieran Slack said he came in on Thursdays and had never once said why, and said it in the tone of a man reporting the weather.

"You are not here about a room," said Ivor Skellern.

"No."

"You are here about the room, and you have been here on Tuesday, and the man on the counter told you about the keys, and I am not angry about any of it, and I am not going to ask you a single question about what you are doing because I decided that on Tuesday afternoon and I have not slept properly since, and I would like that on the front of the conversation so that we can both get on with it."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is a decision, and it was made about half past four yesterday afternoon and I have not slept properly since." He put the unlit cigarette down on the counter. "You are going to ask me things and I am going to answer about one in nine, and I have told the man on the counter that and he will tell you, and if he has not told you then he has been protecting me and he should stop, because I have not needed protecting since about 2026 and I have been extremely boring about it."

"Ask me nothing and I will ask nothing," said Elias Venn. "I am not from the council, I am not a doctor, I am not from the police, and I have got a notebook and I am not going to write a word in it this morning. I have got four questions and one of them I am not going to ask."

"Four and one you are not going to ask. Go on."

"First one. How long is a Tuesday."

Ivor Skellern looked at him for the first time properly, which took about two seconds and involved nothing at all.

"As long as it takes," he said. "About four hours. Sometimes three and a bit. And then I come down." He turned the unlit cigarette round on the counter. "That is the whole of what I am going to say about it and I have said it to two people in four years and one of them was a man in a shop and one of them was a woman with leeks in a bag."

"You sit in the shop for two hours."

"I sit in the shop for two hours."

"You are about to ask the second one and I am going to tell you that I have had four people ask me what I do up there and three of them were owed money, and one of them was a man in about 2028 who was very good at asking and I liked him, and I gave him a cup of tea, and he came back the following Tuesday and he was not good at asking at all, he was frightened, and I have not given anybody a cup of tea since. That is your second one answered before you ask it."

---

"There is a book on that desk," said Elias Venn.

"There is."

"May I look at it."

"No."

"I have not asked you what you do up there and I have told you I am not going to ask, and I would like to look at a book that is about rent, and I have done nothing this morning that you could put in front of anybody."

Ivor Skellern looked at the desk for a while and then he turned round and pushed a hardback book across it and squared it with the edge of two fingers, and the book had about four years of columns in it and a rubber band round it, and the columns were a date, a signature and a number of pounds.

"Rent, weekly," he said. "Every Tuesday. One line. That is the one room, and the other seven are on a pad behind the till and have been since about 2019, and nobody has ever asked me why one of the eight has a hardback with a rubber band on it and the other seven do not, and I do not do this for anybody else, and I have told Kieran and he has said for about two years that I am a lunatic."

"Who signs."

"He does." Skellern turned the book about a quarter of turn so that the first page faced up. "**That is the only page in it with anything of mine on it, and what is on it is a signature and not a name, and it has been there four years, and I have never once asked him to put anything else on it, and there is a book on this desk with two strokes and a loop in it every Tuesday, and I have kept it in a drawer in a shop for four years, and I would like somebody to come and take it off me.**"

Elias Venn did not put his hand on the book. He read the first page from about two feet away, which he was able to do, and it said a date in the form 04/06/26 and a signature that was two strokes and a loop and no other letters, and sixty pounds, and then the next Tuesday, and the next.

"There is a name in it," he said.

"There is a signature in it. I did not write a name. A signature is a thing you do with a pen when you do not want to give a name and you have to give something, and I have been doing that for about six years and I would do it again, and I would like you to go and look at the fourth page where there is a repair on it, because I tore it and I wrote over it and I did it badly, and you will see that I did it in a hurry, and that is because in 2029 I nearly wrote a name."

He took the book back and put the rubber band on it and did not put it in a drawer, and left it on the desk, which was either trust or a decision, and he did not say which.

---

"Your third," he said. "Which I am answering because you have behaved and because you have not asked me the one you said you would not ask. There are two claims in this city with my name on them as the person who did the work, and I did not do the work on either of them, and I am telling you that before you find it out, and I have thought about that for four years and I have not told anybody, and I am telling you in a shop at ten past twelve on a Thursday because you have been in my building twice and you have not asked me the question and because a man of fifty-two who is going to be sixty has run out of ways of carrying things alone."

"Who is on the two claims with you."

"A registered operator with a registration and a premises and a set of books. A company. It is a company and I am not in it and I have never been in it and I have never met a person in it, and I have told you what I know about them and I have told you in about eleven words and I would like you to notice that eleven words is what I have got, and that a man who is owed money can say more in a shout." He put one finger on the book and left it there. "There is a difference between a man who is owed money and a company, and I have never been able to work out which one I am, and I have decided that on the whole I am neither, and I have been getting on with it."

"Why have you never asked them for anything."

"Because the first time I asked a woman in an office for a piece of paper in 2027, she asked me in return whether the room was registered, and I said no, and she said thank you, and that was the whole of the conversation, and I have thought about it every Tuesday for three years." He said it without any weight. "**You do not get angry at a woman who asks you a fair question. You get frightened of a woman who asks you a fair question and then helps you.**"

---

The back room was a kettle, a radiator, a table with a cup on it, a chair, a window that looked at the wall of the next building at about four feet, and a landline telephone with the number written on a piece of masking tape and the tape gone yellow.

Elias Venn did not look at the telephone.

"You are not going to ask me about the plan," said Ivor Skellern. "You have had your back to that table four times and you have not once asked me where it is or when it comes out or whether it is in that drawer, and you have been in this shop for three-quarters of an hour and you have asked me a question about the length of a Tuesday, which is the only question in this city that anybody has ever asked me about the room, and I want to tell you why I have not asked you a single question about the plan, and I am going to tell you because you have earned it and because I have not got anybody else."

"Go on."

"I have not got anybody else, and that is a thing I have found out about myself in about four years and I did not expect it." He turned the cup on the table about a quarter of a turn. "It is the back room of a shop on a parade. I have been in it for four years on a Thursday and a Tuesday and about eleven other days. I am fifty-two. I have a business which is a shop that sells cleaning things and does keys, and I have a set of keys, and on a Tuesday I come in at about half past seven and I go up and I lock the stair door behind me and I let people in, and I am in that room with the people who come, and at about half past twelve I come down and I lock the stair door and I come in here and I stand at this table until Kieran goes at four, and then I unlock the stair door from the shop side and let myself out of the shop at five, and I have done that about two hundred times."

"That is a very long time to stand at a table."

"It is a very long time to stand at a table," said Ivor Skellern, "and I worked out in about the second year why I do it, and the reason is that if I go up to that flat on a Wednesday or a Friday or a Monday, then somebody in this street finds out within a week, because there are four people on this parade who have nothing to do and one of them has a brother-in-law who does the deliveries, and if somebody finds out that I go up there on other days then in about a year somebody is going to come upstairs and ask me what I am doing on a Tuesday as well." He put his hand on the table, not flat, on the edge. "**So I go up on a Tuesday and I come down on a Tuesday, and I stand in this room with the door shut for two hours, and I do it in here on purpose, with a kettle and a cup and a light on, so that if anybody in this street says did Skellern go upstairs last Tuesday, the answer is yes, at half past seven, and then he stood in the back room till four. And they ask why, and I say because I am not a fool, and that is the end of it, and it has been the end of it for four years.**"

"That is not why."

"That is not the whole of why and you have not earned the whole of why, and I am fifty-two and I will tell you one more piece and then I am going to shut this conversation down because it is nearly one o'clock and I have a delivery at two." He did not move his hand off the edge of the table. "**There is a man of twenty-five in that shop and he has never been given a key to those rooms and he has never asked for one, and I made sure of that in the first year, and I have gone on making sure of it, and that is the whole of what I have actually done about any of this and I have done it in about four minutes a week for four years.**"

"You have been protecting him."

"I have been making sure that there is one thing about me that a police officer could not put in a report," said Ivor Skellern. "Everything else in my life is a shop and a set of keys and sixty pounds a week. I could go to prison for any of it and I would deserve to. If anybody in this street ever says that man has a key to a room, then that is the one thing, and there will not be any others, and I have been arranging that since 2026."

Nobody said anything for about a minute. Out in the shop a till drawer went and a man said something about a bag.

"I am not going to tell you that this is all right," said Elias Venn. "I am going to tell you that I have been in this city for four months and I have been looking for a man in a room and there have been four corners to it and every one of them was somebody doing a job, and you are the first one who has told me that he has arranged something."

"I have arranged one thing," said Ivor Skellern. "One. And I would like you to go and find the other three and leave me out of it, and when you have found them, come back and tell me whether the four of you together are worse than I am on my own in this room with a kettle."

---

The stairwell had a bulb in a flex and a meter cupboard and a smell of cold plaster, and halfway up Elias Venn stopped and put his hand flat on the wall, and the wall was cold, and the next act was going up, and it went up, and it went up again, and he got to the top of it and stood in a corridor with eight doors on it and did the thing he could not stop doing, twice, before he made himself go down.

He went down into the shop and asked his fourth question.

"The man who drew the plan of your room," he said. "You drew it."

"I did."

"You drew it in June 2026 and you have drawn it about four times since, and on the reverse there are eleven chairs numbered with a figure beside each of them, and the date and a claim number are in the top corner of the other side, and a claim number is a document, and a document is the only thing in this city that anybody has ever been prepared to believe."

"That is right."

"That is not a drawing of a room."

"No," said Ivor Skellern. "It is not."

Elias Venn stood in a shop on a parade and looked at a man of fifty-two behind a counter for about the length of nine seconds, and the man did not say one word, and he did not nod, and he did not put his hand flat on anything, and he picked up the unlit cigarette and put it in his pocket, which is the only thing a man does at the end of a conversation he has decided to be in.

"You have never asked me the one you said you would not ask," he said, "and I have worked out what it is, and I am not going to tell you whether you have got it right, and if you have got it right then you know that a set of chairs in a room is a set of chairs in a room and I drew them where I watched people sit, and if you have not got it right then you have come to my shop four times for nothing, and I will be sorry for you, and I am fifty-two and I have a business and I am going to be sixty."

---

There was a laundrette at the bottom of the parade with nine machines in it and a woman of about thirty-one at the counter on her own with a collection slip in her hand and a coat that had been on the rail behind her for about an hour, and she had a bag of leeks in the basket of the machine behind her, and it was nine o'clock on a Thursday in January and there was one other woman in there doing a blanket.

"I have not got a machine free till Monday," the woman said, before he had said anything.

"I am not washing anything."

"Then I am sorry, and if you are looking for the one with the card machine, that is not here any more and it has not been here since the summer." She looked at the slip. "I am waiting for nine coats and my mother-in-law is not one of them and she is not doing a wash and she has not done a wash in this laundrette in two years, and I have been standing in here for forty minutes telling myself I was going to do a wash, and the reason I am in here is that it is Thursday and it is the only forty minutes on a Thursday that I do not have to be in a kitchen."

Elias Venn put his hand flat on the counter and then took it away, and it was the second time in two days that he had been able to do it once, and the first time had been on the Tuesday at about ten past six in a shop on a parade, and he had written nothing down about that either.

"How long has she been going upstairs," he said.

"Two years. Since the spring, near enough. Every Tuesday, near enough. I have had her at this table for about four of those and I have never once asked her where she was going, because I have a mother-in-law and you do not ask a mother-in-law, and I have thought about that a lot since about November and I have got to the point where I would like to ask her and she is dead, so that is finished, and there is nothing to be done with it." She turned the collection slip over. "Cash. Never any change. She comes back at about half past twelve looking like she has been out in the dark, and last spring she told me she had a headache and she has told me that four times and I did not know until Tuesday what it was a headache about."

"What does she do up there."

"She has told me it is a doctor and I have told her that is not what it is and we have both been very pleasant about it for two years." She looked at him properly for the first time. "Why are you asking me."

"Because a man of twenty-five in a shop has been asked whether a room is let, twice this week, and I did not ask him, and I asked you instead, and I have got nothing to give you and I have not got anybody to be angry at, and you are the first person who has said to me in four months that you have known for six months and you have not asked, and I would like that to be the same thing."

"It is not the same thing at all," she said. "But I will tell you the thing I have actually got, since you have been honest with me and it costs me nothing. **She asked me, in April, whether I had ever had a thing done to get a name or a date back, and I said no, and she said that was all right, everybody should have one, and she said it the way you would say everybody should have a coat.**"
