# Chapter 0068 — Four Hours and a Rubber Stamp

The second lift at the Aldergate Wing was locked from ten until eleven on a Monday for the linen run, and Petronella Osei at the front desk told him so at twenty past nine, and told him also that the operator's offices were on two and that the archivist came in at nine on a Monday, because the building is shut at weekends and the file room is read on a Monday morning by everybody at once.

"So you have about an hour," she said. "And the day room on two is open, and there is a jigsaw in it, and I am not going to pretend that is a thing I enjoy saying to a person with nothing to do for an hour."

---

The day room on the second floor was smaller than the one on the ground. It had a different television and a different jigsaw. There were eleven people in it and about nine of them were playing cards, and the man of forty with the magazine was in this one as well, and the magazine was still the one from August.

A rehabilitation building has two day rooms and a jigsaw in each, and neither jigsaw is ever finished, and on Wednesday he had worked out that the reason neither is finished is that nobody in the building is going to sit down and do the last eleven per cent, and that this is not a fault in either of them and is a fact about both of them.

At four minutes to eleven a woman of thirty-one came out of a door at the end with a folder under her arm and a ring of keys in her hand, and stopped, and looked at him.

"You're not on the list."

"I have not got an appointment."

"No," she said. "And I have got the file, and I got it out of the box on Friday night and I have had it on this table since half past nine, because it was on the minutes of the funding review on Wednesday morning, item six, and item six went to the whole room, and my name is on the minutes as the custodian, and there is now a person in this city who knows that there are certificates outstanding on a series I look after." She put the folder down on the table. "That is not an invitation. It is a fact and it is why I have got it out, and if you are here at five past eleven I will show you the top copy and turn the pages myself, and if you are not here at five past eleven I will take it back down to the box and I will not think less of you than I did at ten to eleven."

---

The office was a room with nine desks in it and eight of them with nothing on the desks, and a microfilm reader, and forty-one boxes on a shelf along one wall with a list of the numbers in a frame, and a table in the middle with a lamp on it.

"Dorothy Wray," she said, when she came back. "I have been an archivist here three years and before that I was at a records office in a hospital for six. What do you want."

"I want to know whether there is a claim file for a service on the eleventh of September 2029."

"That is a good question and you have asked it correctly, which is to say that you have not asked me for a claim, and the answer is that there are about nine hundred claim files in this room and I cannot tell you without two things, and the two things are a date and an account, and you have given me a date."

"Harle——"

"No," said Dorothy Wray. "I know what you are about to say, and no, and I want you to hear the no properly, because the no is not me being difficult, the no is the shape of the thing.

"**A claim file is a document of a claimant. A claimant is a company. A company does not owe a document to a stranger, and you are a stranger, and if I handed you the file because you were a nice man who had asked nicely then in four years there would be a member of the public in a room with an operator's claim file in his hand, and there is no form anywhere in the world that says where he got it, and I would have to be the one who explains it.**"

"And you will not."

"I will not, and I would like it recorded that I would not, because I have watched two good archivists leave jobs over exactly that and I am not going to be the third." She took her coat off and hung it on the back of a chair. "But it is on this table, and it is going to stay on this table, and I am going to turn the pages and you are going to read across the table, and not one piece of it leaves this room. **And that is not a favour. That is the difference between a favour and a procedure, and a procedure is the only thing I have got, and I would like to be able to do it about eleven times a week instead of never.**"

---

She squared it on the table with both hands and then did not open it, and there were two rules and she said both of them before she said anything else.

"One: you do not touch it, and I do not touch it while you are reading, and I do not touch it at all except to turn the pages, and the reason is not the claim. The reason is that a file that has been handled by a visitor cannot be shown to anybody afterwards without a line in a book about who handled it and when. **And this is the operator's own top copy. The district office on Harrow Lane holds an office copy of it and has held one since October, and I have never seen the two side by side and neither has anybody else in this city, and neither of them is the certificate.**

"Two: you do not photograph it. There is no camera in this room and if there were it would be in a case and there is a man in an office in Concord Hall who would like to have it."

"I have not got a camera."

"You have not got a camera on your person and I have not got a camera in this room and neither of us is going to say anything further about it." She opened the file. "There. Read."

---

It was a claim, and it was a real claim, and it was in the shape that Rhoda Nesbit's three counterfoils were in, and the shape was the point.

A number at the top in a hand that leaned to the right and pressed hard. A claimant. A premises. A date of service. A patient schedule box with **THIRLMERE 4** in it. A procedure box with **D-2** in it and the words *grounding and anchor, two hours* typed under it. A fee box with **60.00**. A countersignature at the foot, and the countersignature was not in the hand of the clinician named on the form, and Elias Venn had been told that on a Friday by the person who held the money, and he had written four words about it on a Saturday, and he did not go looking.

And it was all of it true. Every part of it. There was a number, and the number was well formed, and the claimant existed, and the premises existed, and the schedule reference existed, and the service code was the code for the procedure that had been billed, and the fee was the tariff fee for that procedure, and the fee had been paid, and somebody somewhere had put a rubber stamp on a paying-in slip with the date and the amount on it and a stamp pad that had been re-inked in about June.

"Was this paid," said Elias Venn.

"Yes."

"Out of what."

"Out of an account, on a paying-in slip, with a date and an amount and a stamp." Dorothy Wray did not look up from the file. "I do not know whose account. I am an archivist. I have got nine hundred files and about four thousand slips and I could tell you the date and the amount on any of them and I could not tell you a single thing about any of them that is not printed on them."

"How long is a claim file kept."

"Seven years after the account is closed, and the account is never closed, and so a file of 2029 is in that room and so is a file of this morning." She turned a page. "There is no rule in this city that says a claim file must be looked at by a person. It has to be *correct*. I have never in three years had anybody ring this office and ask me whether a file was correct, and I have twice been told by somebody in a corridor that the files are a very good set of files."

---

Then she said the thing, and she said it while she was closing the file, and she said it in the voice of a person reading out a shelf mark.

"I can put those two on a form," said Dorothy Wray. "The bed and the code. **This afternoon. On this desk. And it would go out on Monday and it would be paid on the second of the month, because that is what the machine does and the machine does not ask whether a woman went to Corrie Street.**"

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"I want to be exact with you, because you have asked me correctly and I owe you the same. I do not need anything. There is no code. There is no name. There is a box with a bed in it and a box with a service in it and a rubber stamp and a date, and what has to be in a claim form that is not in those two boxes is a person, and a person is not a requirement, it is a habit." She put the file down. "And I have done it once before. In 2027, in about four minutes, and it was a legitimate correction because a clerk had put a ward number in that had been renumbered, and the correction was to put the number the file ought to have had. And nobody asked me to do it. A legal person asked. A man came and stood where you are standing, and he was pleasant, and he did not give a name, and I did the four minutes and I have thought about it perhaps twice in three years and not once in a way that has kept me up."

"Have you ever been asked to do it twice?"

"No," said Dorothy Wray. "Once. And I am not going to tell you who, in 2027, because you did not ask, and because I have been in this room three years and I have watched a woman at a claims office in a different building in this city get a career out of knowing a man, and I am not going to be the second thing that happens to her either."

---

He asked her for a pencil and a blank sheet, and she gave him both, and he did not take the file and he did not copy the file, and he wrote four sentences in his own hand, and they were not a list.

> *There is a bound volume on the ground floor of this building and a woman who signs for it, and it says who was in a bed. The room I am sitting in holds nine hundred files and eight people and a rubber stamp, and it says a bed and a code and a fee, and whether the money moved. The payer's office on Verge Row says whether the claim is worth paying, and it has already decided that it is, and it has a note in it from the fourth of November with a name in a box at the top. The office that issued the certificate says that it put a number into the world on the second of October and cannot remember doing it, because the number is in a drawer four streets away and the drawer is the only copy there is.*

> *Nobody in this city may ring anybody in any of the other three. There is no rule that says they may not. There is simply no rule that says they may.*

> *The whole of the operation from a bed to a paid claim is four hours and a rubber stamp, and it has not been done in three days for exactly one reason, and the reason is that a woman of fifty-eight in Corrie Street wrote a counterfoil in 2029 and did not hand it over, and she was not thinking about any of this when she did it, and she has four words in her own book about a different woman who came three times and did not come, and she has never once written down the fifth possibility, and she made me promise about it in November, and I have kept the promise and I have not thought about it once this week because I have been in a hospital and a laundry and an insurance office and a registry and a ward and a lift.*

And then he wrote the arithmetic, which was his own and had been his own since the twenty-first of November, and which he had told himself in a car park was forty-nine credits and was not a person.

*She charged eleven and she wrote eleven in a book and the claim says sixty and the sixty was paid to an account by somebody and the difference was forty-nine credits. And the reason the claim can exist at all is that the practice is illegal for being undercharged, and the reason somebody else's certificate is standing up is that a woman in a first-floor room was cheap. **The wall is the price. I have been walking round a building for three weeks looking for a door and the door is a price list and it was in a library on Corrie Street in November and it cost three credits.***

He read that last sentence twice and then put a line through it and wrote it out again without the word *door*, because a man who begins a sentence with *the door is* is a man who has started to enjoy it.

---

The canteen was down a corridor and down four steps and it took eleven minutes to find from the second floor because the sign said **DAY ROOM 2** over the door it wanted and **CANTEEN** over the door it meant, and there were two women in it at half past twelve and a woman on the hatch who was not the woman from Monday.

"Two teas," said Elias Venn, and put twenty-two credits on the tray because that was twenty-two credits, and did not notice until the woman had taken them.

"That is what it is," she said. "Nobody has ever been surprised by that. What has happened is nothing. That card has been on that table since before I came on this hatch, and the last line on it is a matron's, and it is in a different typeface from the price list above it, and it says that anybody who could not pay was to say so and they would do something about it." She put the two cups down. "You have not said you cannot pay. You have paid, and I have taken it, and I am not going to pretend that I noticed."

"Twenty-nine years on the folding board at the laundry on Verge Row," said Elias Venn. "She raised hers from eight to eleven in March and told me about it on Saturday and told me why, and the reason is a woman on Aldermere Road who pays in coins out of a jar."

The woman on the hatch looked at him for a second.

"Then she will know what this card is for," she said, "and she is the only person I have ever wanted to explain it to, and I have never seen her, and I do not know her name and I am not going to ask you."

Dorothy Wray had come down the four steps behind him and was standing at the door with her coat on, and did not come in, and said:

"I am going. It is half past twelve and I am going, and I have said a thing on a table that I have said once in three years, and I would like to record that I said it to a man who did not ask me to and who did not ask me anything else, and I would like to go home, and if you come back up to that room on Monday I will not be here and the door will be locked and that is not a thing about you."

"I am not coming back," said Elias Venn.

"Good," said Dorothy Wray, from the doorway of a canteen on the second floor of a rehabilitation building, and went up the four steps.

He drank the tea. It was no better than the one on the ground floor and it was no worse. He sat at the table by the window with the card in its plastic frame in front of him and a pencil in his hand and he did not write anything, and the two women at the other end of the canteen were arguing about a television programme in the low voices people use when they are comfortable, and the day room on two with the second jigsaw was twenty feet away through a door that was open.

Then he put the pencil down, because a man holding a pencil at a table in a canteen is a man about to write, and there was nothing on the page he was going to be able to put in front of anybody. Half an hour ago a woman of thirty-one had told him that she could take a bed and a code off a file in a room four streets away, put them on a form in four minutes, and have the form paid on the second of the month, and that the machine does not ask whether a woman went to Corrie Street. The sum on top of that was his own and he had done it in a notebook in a shop: from the bed to the money, four hours and a rubber stamp, and he was the only person in the city who knew it, and there was nothing at all he could do with it on Monday, or on Tuesday, or on any day he could reach, and in about four years somebody would rely on that certificate to refuse somebody a claim, and it would take them four minutes to refuse, and nobody would ever find out that the bed had been shut.

So he put his hand flat on the card in its plastic frame instead, under the matron's line about anybody who could not pay, and he left it there for the length of a cup of tea, thinking about a woman of fifty-nine on Verge Row who had told him on Saturday that there is no rule anywhere in this city that says a laundry has to charge less, and who had been right about that too, and whom he had not thanked either.

At about ten past one he got up, and put the cup on the hatch, and said thank you to a woman he had never spoken to before today, and she said nothing at all, because she had put the card there.
