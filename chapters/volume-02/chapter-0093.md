# Chapter 0093 — Refused on the Merits

The waiting room at Vine Street had six chairs in it and a radiator that had been on since before anybody came and a fire bucket with about two inches of sand in the bottom of it, and at eleven o'clock on Wednesday the sixteenth of January there were four people in it, and one of them was a man of about forty-four with a briefcase and a bad coat, and one of them was Nadia Kest, and one of them was a woman of about seventy with a shopping bag full of paperwork that was going to be dealt with badly, and the fourth was Elias Venn, who had been sent to wait with her because there was one chair short.

Nadia Kest had got there at a quarter past ten and had told him, in the corridor, that he was about to be the worst thing in the building.

"I am not going to be in there."

"You are going to stand outside a door for twenty minutes while I say the same four sentences twice, and then you are going to be allowed in for about ninety seconds to be sworn, and in those ninety seconds you are going to say a date and a place and that you are a member of the public, and I am going to be standing there while a woman of about sixty reads your application back to you in a flat voice and explains why it is not good enough." She looked at the fire bucket. "And on the first of October I was in a different corridor in this building being told the same thing in the same flat voice with a different set of words, and I have been sick about it twice in three months, and this time I am not sick, because this time the answer is the answer and not a wall."

Magistrate Dyment was fifty-seven and had a cardigan on under her gown and read the whole of the application twice, and there were two pages and one of them was a printout of an address.

"You have given me a first-floor room above a shop at nine Calder Place and a date and a time of day, and you have given me a man and you have given me that he is a member of the public with a finding against him in December." She put the second page down. "What else have you got?"

"He has a plan of the room," said Nadia Kest. "He has not got it, because nobody who is entitled to anything has got it and I am not entitled to it either. I am aware of it and I am not relying on it."

"Then set it aside." She turned the first page round. "Officer, is there anything before me that is not a document."

"No, my lady."

"Then I am going to refuse this, and I am going to refuse it in about forty seconds, and I am going to do it now so that the officer can get on with something. An application of this kind is not refused because it does not name a place. It is refused because the only matter standing in front of me is a suspicion formed out of a piece of paper, and a suspicion formed out of a piece of paper is not evidence, and more importantly it is not *swearable*. I cannot swear to it. I have not seen the room. I have no idea what happens in it." She picked up her pen. "What would assist me is an officer's own grounds. Not yours, officer — the applicant's. I mean that the officer in whose district this is must be able to say, on the oath, that she has herself seen something, or that a person she has spoken to has seen something, and that the thing seen was of a kind that a magistrate can act on. Not what anybody has been told. Not what anybody has read. Something a person was in a room for."

"How long would that take," said Nadia Kest.

"You know how long that takes."

"I do," said Nadia Kest. "Thank you, my lady."

"Refused. And officer — the refusal is on the merits, and not on jurisdiction, and I want that distinction written into the note, because an application that names a place may be brought again and again, and an application that does not name a place may be brought once. That is not a favour to you. It is a rule about what the form is for, and the form is the form's fault." She signed it. "Next."

Elias Venn was allowed in for ninety seconds and said a date and a place and that he was a member of the public, and when the magistrate, who was fifty-seven and a good deal older than he had expected, read the refusal back he asked one question.

"Ma'am, is there any part of a document that would have helped."

"No," said Magistrate Dyment. "And do stop calling me that, I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have a name and it is on the wall behind you in a frame with a date range on it, which is more than most of us get."

---

They were in a corridor outside a records office at half past four in the afternoon and she had a folder under her arm and he had his hands in his pockets and neither of them was sitting on anything.

"I am going to be in that room on Tuesday," he said.

"No."

"I have not finished."

"You are going to be in that room on Tuesday and I have been not letting you for four days and I have thought about it on a stair on Friday afternoon and on a Sunday night in my own kitchen, and I am going to tell you the whole of my reasoning and then I am going to tell you the answer and then you are not going to raise it again until after the twenty-third, and that is not me being stubborn, that is me spending four minutes of a good man's attention so that neither of us has to spend it at two in the morning."

"Go on."

"The first reason is that you are not a constable and you are not a clinician and you have been formally reprimanded for touching a thing you were not entitled to touch, and if there are eleven people in a room and something goes wrong in it, the only document anybody will produce on the Wednesday will be a document about a member of the public being in a room, and it will not have one word in it about the eleven." She turned the folder over. "The second reason is that you are the only person I have ever met who can walk into a room and tell me in nine seconds whether something that happened in it is the real shape or the copied shape, and if I put you in that room, then from the moment you walk in, the only thing anybody in the building is watching is you. And if a thing goes wrong in that room, they will not look at the room. They will look at you. And a room of eleven people who are all frightened and all in the wrong has got no use for anybody watching them."

"I will not be watching them. I will be watching the frame."

"You will be watching the frame *in a room with eleven people in it*, and the frame is on a table in the middle of the room, and you are a man in a good coat with a notebook and a faculty, and every one of those eleven people is going to spend the whole of the evening being aware of you in it." She put the folder under her arm the other way. "And there is a third reason and I have kept it back since Friday and I am giving it to you now because you have been decent about the drawer and I owe you one. **If you go into that room and you cannot do it, there will be nothing done. Nothing. Because the working will not stop for a person who fails, and it will not stop for a police officer either, and it will certainly not stop for a document. The only thing in this city that has ever stopped one is somebody in a corridor putting a hand on a door, and that was you, on the eleventh of January, and it did not stop it, and a woman had to come down a stair and an inspector had to put her shoulder to a door, and I am not going to build Tuesday around a man in a coat and a hope.**"

They stood in the corridor.

"I am the only person who can tell a false start from a turn," he said.

"Yes."

"And you have to go in without me."

"Yes, and I have thought about that for four days and here is what I have arrived at, and I would like you to know that I have arrived at it on my own and not because you argued me into it, because you have not argued me into anything in four days and I have been waiting for you to." She looked at the floor. "**If you are in that room, then you are in it as part of a working. Not as a witness. Not as a man who has been brought in to look. You will be in it as a hand on a thing, and if the working comes round the way it came round a door in Corrie Street then you will be in it, and the difference between that and Friday is that on Friday there were two people in that corridor who had agreed in advance that one of them was going to be in the room, and on Tuesday there will be eleven people in that room who did not agree to anything.**"

"So it is not no."

"It is not no," said Nadia Kest. "It is *not from this afternoon*. It is not from a corridor outside a records office on the sixteenth of January, and it is not from a man who has just been refused. It is going to be from me, in writing, on a piece of paper with my name at the bottom of it, on Monday, and it is going to say what I will and will not have, and you are going to read it, and you are going to agree to all of it or I am not going to the room at all."

"I will agree to all of it."
"I know you will. That is what I have found out about you in four months and it is the reason I am going to do it."

---

The dispensary on the parade shut at seven and had a counter with a metal grille half down and a woman of about fifty-two behind it who had fourteen years' of a green cardigan and a stamp pad and no queue, because it was a Wednesday and it was a cold one and everybody in that district was at home.

Elias Venn had come in for a tin of tea and a chemist's bag for a meter reading in the morning, and he put them on the counter, and the woman took the tea off the shelf without asking him a question about it, which is a thing a woman behind a counter does when she has decided a person is not going to make her life difficult.

"Twenty-two months of those readings and nobody has ever asked me what the meter is for," said Doreen Tillery. "You would not believe me and it does not matter. It is a thing I say at the counter about four times a week and I have never once been asked whether it is true."

"You have not asked me what the meter is for," said Elias Venn.

"No, and I am not going to, and you are the first person in about two years who has come to that counter and not asked me for a thing I could not give him." She rang the tea through and put it in a bag. "You are the fourth one this month. The first was a man who wanted to know why his wife is different on a Tuesday and the second was a woman who wanted to know whether a thing she read in a magazine could come off a shelf, and the third one was a lad of about nineteen who wanted to know if it is a crime to not finish a course of something."

"And the fourth."

"You have not told me a question yet." She gave him the bag. "That is four to me and two to you, and I will tell you what I have learned about it, and then you can go home. **Everybody who comes to that counter has one question and it is about a person, and there is no shelf in this building that has ever answered one, and I have been standing behind the other side of that for fourteen years.**"

He stood at the counter for a moment with a tin of tea in a bag.

"How late do you close on a Thursday," he said.

"Ten past seven. Why."

"A man I want to see is in a shop on the parade at about half past eleven tomorrow and then he is not, and I would like a room with a door and no window in a warm building." He put a pound note on the counter. "You have got a back room?"

"I have got a back room with a table and a kettle and a heater and a woman in it on my own time after seven, and it is not a room I can lend to anybody." She took the note and did not give him change and he did not ask for it. "I will tell you what I will do and I will tell you why, and then I will not come to it again. **I will let you sit in this building on a Thursday between seven and ten, and there is a chair and there is a heater and there is a woman behind this counter, and that is the whole of what you are getting, and it is not lent and it is not given and it is not anything at all.**"

"Why."

"Because in December a lady I have known for eleven years came in and filled in a form in front of me, on her own, and she put her own name under the doctor's in the fifth line where it said *who told the patient*, and she had never done that in eleven years, and I have not stopped thinking about it, and if there is going to be somebody in this building on a Thursday who needs a chair then he can have the chair and he can leave the form alone, and that is the whole of my condition and I have not thought about it for a second before I said it, which means it is true."

---

"What can a person swear to," he said, at about ten past eight that evening, standing in a corridor in Vine Street with a piece of job card in his hand.

"Anything they saw with their own eyes and can remember without help." Nadia Kest was at the other end of a telephone in Vine Street and there was a room behind her with people in it. "Not what they think. Not what they have been told. Not what they have been told afterwards by anybody, including you, and I want that in your own writing and I want it in mine, because a witness who has been spoken to is not a witness, she is a person reciting, and a person reciting can be taken apart in about nine minutes by somebody who does this for a living and I have watched it happen to a man of about sixty in a room with a table and a glass of water on it."

"She will have to go in and look at a room with people in it."

"She will have to go in on a job. She is a district nurse. She has an evening list and she has a woman on the ground floor of that building on the list at a quarter past seven, and if the stair is open and a door at the top is open then a nurse who has a woman on the ground floor and has a hoover bag goes up a stair. That is not a crime and it is not unusual and it is not a favour, it is Tuesday." A pause. "She will have to be the one to ask the man with the keys, and she will have to do it on her own, because if a police officer asks a shopkeeper for a key to a flat above his shop, that is an officer, and a man of twenty-five will hear it as an officer and will lock up and say nothing and I will lose a Tuesday."

"Then I ask him."

"You do not ask him. She asks him, and he says yes, because a nurse asking for a stair is a nurse asking for a stair, and it is the only thing in this case that has worked in four months and it is the only thing in this case that is not a document." The line went quiet for a second. "And she will be frightened, and she will do it, and she will be owed something by somebody for the rest of her life and it will not be you and it will not be me."

The woman of about seventy came back through the corridor at about ten past eight with her shopping bag against her chest and her coat over her arm, and she sat down on the chair she had been sitting in for three hours, and she put the bag on her knees.

"You were the other one sitting with me this morning," she said. "The young man with the bad coat. I have had two papers done and one of them is in my other bag and it is not what I came in with, and the woman behind the grille has told me it is correct and has put it in an envelope for me, and I am seventy-one and I have been to this building about forty times and I have never once been given an envelope."

"No."

"No," she said. "I did not think so. I have come back in because you are the only person in this building who has looked at my face while I was talking, and I would like to ask you the thing I have been sitting here for." She put her hands flat on the bag. "**How many of these are true.**"

"I could not tell you."

"That is the first time anybody has said no to me in this building in about four years," said the woman of about seventy, "and everybody else has said *of course, love*, and I have gone home and taken it out of the envelope and looked at it, and I have not the faintest idea, and I would rather go home with a no." She got up. "I am going to walk to the bus. It is cold and I am not going to wait for a taxi and I would rather walk."

She went out into the street with the bag against her chest, and Elias Venn stood in a corridor in Vine Street with a piece of job card in his hand and the drawer two miles away still open under a counter that was not his, and he was not going to think about it until he had got home.
