# Chapter 0072 — Tuesday

The hall had a swimming class on Thursdays and a soup on Tuesdays, and the reason for the arrangement, which nobody in Gallow Street could explain and everybody in it believed, was that the pool needed the water on one day and the kitchen needed the other, and between them they had the building for nine years.

There were about thirty people through the hatch between a quarter past eleven and half past one, and not one of them was anything, and that was the point of the room.

The queue went the way a queue goes in a building that has been doing it for nine years: it formed without anybody organising it, it went round the end of the table by the door because that is where the end of the table is, and it was four minutes for a bowl on a good Tuesday and about seven on a wet one. There was a man of about sixty who came every Tuesday and had come every Tuesday since before the pool, and he read the noticeboard while he waited, and he had read the same noticeboard for nine years, and there was a man of about thirty with a crutch and a child of about five on his hip who came on the second Tuesday of the month and had never once been late, and there was a woman who did not go in the queue at all and came to the side door and took a plate off the pass and said thank you and was gone in about forty seconds, nine times out of ten, and Bernice Ollerton never once said good morning to her.

Elias Venn stood in it twice and was not asked anything by anybody, and he noticed that this was the second week running that had happened to him in a building in this district, and he did not know yet what it was, and would not have said so if he had known.

Elias Venn got in the queue twice. The first time he was served a bowl of something with a potato in it and he paid eleven credits for it, which he thought was a lot and did not say, and he ate it at the end of a bench with a piece of bread and a spoon that had a worn place in the handle.

The second time he was in the queue, Bernice Ollerton looked at him from the other side of the hatch with a ladle in one hand and a scale in the other and said, "You have had that."

"I have had that."

"You have had that and you are in the queue again and you have not got a tray, and a man in a queue with no tray at this hatch is either a man who wants a second bowl or a man who has come about something." She put the ladle down. "Which is it, and I am asking because I have got about forty in here and I would like to know now rather than at the hatch."

"A man who has come about something."

"Then take your bowl and go and sit at the end of the bench by the door, because that is where I put the ones who are here about something, and where they can see the door and the door is not in anybody's way." She picked the ladle back up. "And if anybody asks you what you are doing you are eating my soup, and that is the truth, and it is a better answer than the one you were going to give."

---

He ate the soup and then he waited until the rush was over, which took until twenty past one, and then he got up and put his bowl on the hatch and asked Bernice Ollerton a general question, because he had learned in a claims office on the Verge Row side in December that a general question is free and a specific one is a document.

"How long have you done this?"

"Nine years in this room. It started in a church hall on Ferrier Street with four of us and a camping stove and it has been here since the second year." She was wiping the pass. "Thirty-one on a good Tuesday, nineteen in August, and it does not matter what we cook, it is what we have got."

"Who comes?"

"Everybody on this side of Gallow." She stopped. "That is a free answer and you have had it, and if you ask me for a name I will tell you the room is not a register, and I have been asked for a register in this room before, twice, and one of them was a man with a clipboard."

"I am not going to ask you for a name."

"Then ask me something else and say it plainly, because I can tell from the way you are standing that you have got a list in your head and you are trying to get it out of me sideways, and I have four hundred of these a year and I know the posture."

"I have got a column in my head," said Elias Venn. "And the column is not a name. The column is an address, and the address is not the person's address, and there are eleven of them and they are in a drawer in a clinic on Marshall Street."

"Then that is a nice thing for you to have and it is not going to help you, and I will tell you why it is not going to help you before you have spent an hour in this room." She put the cloth over her shoulder. "Because I do not know where eleven people live, and I do not know where about four hundred people live either, and the reason I do not know is that I have written down the names of every one of them since the second year and I have never once written down where they sleep. And that is on purpose. That is the only thing in this room I have been careful about for nine years."

"Why?"

"Because the day I write down where they sleep is the day this room stops being a kitchen." She said it without any weight at all, which was how she said the things she meant. "I write down who I feed. I do not write down where they went. And I would like you to go and be surprised somewhere else, because there is nothing to be surprised at in here."

---

She let him look at the book, and the letting was the price of the soup and she told him the price herself: *you can look at it on that table and you cannot have it and you cannot touch it and I will be standing here the whole time and if you ask me a question about a page I will answer it about the page.*

It is a kitchen book and it is nine years of it, and it is not a diary. It has the weather in it and what came on the market on a Monday and what was cooked and how much of it and who came.

The column that matters is the one on the right-hand side of a Tuesday page and it is headed **carried out** and it is names.

"About a third of the food does not leave this room," she said. "Somebody takes it out. A plate here and a plate there. Two of them go to the Ferrier Street rooms on a Wednesday because the rooms have no kitchen and the people in them are the kind of people who would rather be hungry than say so. One goes to the yard at the back of number thirty-one because there are four men in there who have not been inside a building since the summer."

"How do you know which plate goes where?"

"Because somebody tells me, and I write it down, and that is the whole of the arrangement." She turned a page with the back of her hand. "And it is not a rota in the way you are imagining it with a manager in it. It is a name in a column and a plate under it, and the plate is on a tray, and the tray goes out with whoever happens to be on the hatch. And nobody is put on a list for doing it. Nobody is paid. Nobody is thanked except the ones who say thank you and about a third of them do not say it, and I have never once minded, and that is not saintliness, that is that if you start minding it you start asking."

"And the telephone?"

She looked up. "What about the telephone?"

"The currency of the arrangement."

"It is." She said it as though he had finally got to the right page. "It is a telephone. Nine years of this and about eighty per cent of it is a telephone. A woman rings and says my brother is not well and can somebody take a plate on Thursday, and I say yes, and she says his name, and sometimes I know it and sometimes I write it on the plate and it goes out with the tray. And nobody asks her the brother's address. Not because it would be discourteous. **Because if you ask a woman where her brother lives she has to decide, on the telephone, in about a second, whether to tell a stranger, and everybody who comes to this hatch has learned not to ask, and I have learned not to ask, and the not asking has been in this room longer than either of us.**"

---

The back page of the book came open on its own, because a book that is used gets soft at the places it is opened, and the back page is where Bernice Ollerton writes the things that are not food.

There is a column of shopping on it and a column of who did the shopping and a line at the top in pencil about a gas bill, and in the bottom half, below a ruled line, in a second hand, there were eleven lines and every one of them was an address.

Elias Venn read them and did not write them down and did not take his hands off the bench.

The first ten were four streets apart and then a bus ride, and were the kind of addresses people give when they have been asked once and have not thought about it: a door and a street, a street and a floor, a house with a gate, a house with a gate and *and you know the one with the blue door.*

The eleventh was a street he knew and a building he had stood in the yard of on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh of November with a plastic sleeve in his coat and a sheet of paper on a clinic wall behind him and a door that did not open.

**22 Cutlass Row, 3rd fl.**

"Who wrote that."

"I do not know." Bernice Ollerton did not come round the pass. "And before you ask me the next thing, no, it is not mine, I have written in this book every week for nine years and I have got two hands and one of them shakes when I am tired and that is not the one that did that."

"When did it appear."

"March. It was not there in the second week of February and it was there in the fourth week of March and I have never been able to find the page where it arrived, which is a thing I have wanted to say out loud for about nine months." She put the ladle in the sink. "And it has not been added to since. It is eleven lines and it was eleven lines in March and it is eleven lines now, and I have looked at it about four times a year for nine months, and I have never asked, because it is on the page where the bread is written down, and the bread has to be written down."

"You think it is older than the book."

"I think somebody put it in this book and it has been through a washing-up," said Bernice Ollerton. "That is all I think and that is all I am going to say about it, and if you ask me one more question about that page I will take your bowl away and you will not get another one in this hall ever."

"That was not a question."

"That was a no," she said.---

He did ask her one more question, and he asked it at half past one with the hatch shut and the room empty and the two of them standing either side of eleven trays.

"Have you ever sent anybody anywhere?"

"No," said Bernice Ollerton.

"No, you haven't sent anybody anywhere in nine years?"

"No. I ring a woman and she rings a woman and the plate goes out with whoever is on the hatch and the tray comes back and that is the entire mechanical content of nine years of my life and there is not one address on my side of it." She began stacking the trays. "No. And I know what you are after and I am going to give you all four of them and then you can go and be wrong with the other four."

She took four breaths and it was a short speech and she made it well.

"One. I do not know where they live. Two. It would not help anybody if I did. Three. The day somebody in this room starts using a list of addresses I will lose about four hundred people in a fortnight, because the whole of what this room is, is that it is the one place in this district where a person can turn up and not be asked a single question. Four." She put a tray on the stack. "**Four: if I could send somebody anywhere I would not send them to a place with a name on a piece of paper, and neither would you, and the reason you would not is that you have spent four months writing things down so that a room full of officers would believe you, and you do not want to be on the other side of that.**

"There. Four. Now go away before the pool comes in and has to be mopped."

---

She did not let him go away before the pool came in. She made him wait until a quarter past two, when the room was empty and the trays were stacked, and then she took four plates off the top of the stack and put lids on them and gave him the tray.

"You have eaten two bowls and you have looked at my book and you are going to carry one out," she said. "Not because it is fair. Because you have not asked me one single thing about a page and I want you to have carried something out of this room before you decide what you think this room is."

"How far."

"Back yard of number thirty-one. Two minutes past the church, left at the wall, and the gate is stiff and you have to lift it while you push it." She tied a cloth round the tray handle so it would not slip. "There are four men in there and I do not know what three of them are called and I have known one of them since the second year. You knock twice and you say it is Tuesday, and if nobody answers in a minute you put it on the step and you go, and you do not go in, and that is the whole of the rules and there are only three of them."

The gate was stiff and it did take lifting and pushing. The yard was about eleven feet across and there was a stack of pallets against the far wall and a hose on a bracket and no window at all, and there were four men in it, and not one of them looked up, and the one who did look up was about thirty and said "Tuesday," and said it the way you say the name of a day you have been expecting since the middle of the morning.

Elias Venn put the tray on the folding table that was under a lean-to of corrugated iron and came back four minutes later with an empty tray, four empty plates, and four lids.

He was in the gate when the voice came from behind the pallets.

"Was there anything on the bread."

"Two slices and a bit of the crust."

"Right," said the voice, and that was all, and did not turn round again.

He was on Gallow Street at four minutes past three with an empty tray in his hand and Bernice Ollerton's voice behind him saying, "Set it on the pass, not the table, the table is where I write, and go on, and I will see you on a Tuesday if I see you on a Tuesday."

And it had taken four minutes, and there had been four hours between the plating and the eating of it, and nobody had written a word about the four minutes, because the plate goes out with whoever is on the hatch and the tray comes back.

---

The bus queue on Gallow Street at twenty to three had eleven people in it and a timetable with a corner off, and the milk float came round twice. He stood in it and did not get on the first bus, and he worked out, standing there, that eleven on a continuity sheet and eleven on a back page in a kitchen were eleven and eleven, and that there was no document in the world that joined a name to an address in either of them, and that he had noticed the two numbers and that the two numbers meant nothing at all that he was able to prove.

The rain came. He took the second bus and got off at the wrong stop because he had not been paying attention, which was the first wrong thing he had done in a week.

At ten past three he wiped the table down, because Bernice Ollerton had told him to, and the book was still open on the pass where he had left it, and somebody had turned a page.

The eleventh line was smudged at the corner where a page is turned with a wet thumb, and there was a thumbprint on it, about half an inch long, and it was not any use to anybody, and it belonged to whoever had been in the kitchen on a Tuesday in nine years or in March or last week, and it was the only mark anybody had ever made on that page, and it would be there on Wednesday.
