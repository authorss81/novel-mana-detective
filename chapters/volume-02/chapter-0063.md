# Chapter 0063 — The Card at the Foot of the Bed

The funding review was a public meeting in a public building, which was printed on the notice in the entrance hall in eleven words and meant, as far as Elias Venn could work out, that anybody could stand at the back of a room and say nothing for an hour.

He got there at ten to ten and there were about thirty people in a room with the chairs pushed back in rows and a table at the front with four chairs and a jug of water, and a woman from the company that ran the building doing the reading, and a man from the funder with a folder and a table of figures on a card propped up where half the room could not see it.

He stood at the back, against the wall, and was not asked a single question by anybody for two hours and ten minutes.

The first item was a fire door. The second was a plant. The third was the six funded beds on Brackwell, and the funder's man said the word *continuation* four times and the word *accommodation* twice and never once said the name of the funder out loud, and somebody at the front asked him to say it, and he said it.

"**Aegis Mutual.**"

Elias Venn stood against a wall in a room with thirty people in it and heard the name of a company he had not said out loud since the third of November.

The last time he had seen it, it had been in a photograph of page fourteen of a daybook, taken by a sergeant on a bench in a rented workshop in the low cut on the twenty-second of October, camera moved, objects not moved, the original staying where it was, and on the first of November the daybook had been burnt in that workshop and the page had gone with it, and the only reason the words *a/c Aegis Mutual* existed anywhere in the world was a photograph in a police file, and the only reason he had a copy of the photograph's existence was that he had been in a room with a sergeant who had put a photograph in a file because a file that has a photograph about itself in a corridor is a file somebody has decided not to know something.

Since the first of November. And the name was here, on a Wednesday morning, on a card, in a licensed building, doing exactly what an insurer does on a Wednesday morning, which is to decide whether to pay for six beds.

The funder's man was forty-one and he was good at his job. He did not say no. He said that the continuation of six beds on Brackwell was in scope, that the assessment had been carried out on the ninth of November, and that the outcome would be notified in writing to the registered operator and to the individual patients affected. He said *individual patients affected* in a voice that had said it a hundred times, and Elias Venn stood there and understood that there was a letter going to six people about six beds and that none of the six people knew this morning that there was a meeting.

Item four was a ramp and item five was staffing and neither of them was interesting. Item six, at twenty past eleven, was the heading **CERTIFICATES OUTSTANDING — ACCOUNT SERIES 44-31**, and the funder's man read four numbers off a card and said the words *not yet received* after each one, and then he said the sentence that put the whole of it on a page for the first time in a licensed building with thirty people in it:

"The office issues a certificate where the procedure is one that has to be certified rather than billed, and the certificate goes to the payer and not to the patient, and where the certificate has not come back we cannot close the account on the claim, and the account stays open, and an open account is the only thing in this process that costs anybody anything."

"How long is outstanding before it is chased," said a woman at the front who had not introduced herself.

"Six weeks from the date of service, and then the operator chases it, and the operator chases us, and if we have not got it, we chase the district office." He put the card down. "Which is why I have asked for item six to be minuted, because in January somebody will ask me why an account is open and I would like to be able to say that I asked."

Elias Venn stood against a wall thirty feet from a card with four numbers on it and did not move and did not put his hand in his pocket, and did not write anything down, and had a number in his head that was not one of the four, and worked out, standing there, that the date on it was the eleventh of September and today was the fifth of December and that was eighty-five days, and that eighty-five days is a shade over twelve weeks, and that twelve weeks is twice the six weeks the man had just said out loud, and that the only reason he knew which claim it was, and the only reason he was standing in this room at all, was that a woman of fifty-eight had written the address of this building on one line in a green book in six years.

He did not ask. He had a number and no entitlement and a date and a room full of strangers, and asking in that room would have been a man putting a claim number into the air in a public meeting, and in four years there would be four hundred people who had been at a funding review on the morning of the fifth of December and the number would be in about eleven of them.

He wrote one line on the Monday, in a notebook, and most of a page on the Tuesday, and nothing at all on the Wednesday. He noticed the Wednesday at about ten past four, on a shut floor, with his hat in his hand, in the way a man notices a thing he has quietly stopped doing.

---

He got out into the corridor at twenty past twelve and a nurse of about thirty, in a cardigan with a ward badge, stopped him with a hand up and said, "You are not lost, you are early, everybody is early. Sit there. Have you been walking?"

There was a man in the entrance hall at the desk at that time with a carrier bag and his hat in his hand, and he had been told to wait, and Petronella Osei was dealing with him, and Elias Venn stopped at the end of the corridor to listen because he could not help it.

"I have come for my mother. She is on Brackwell and she is not — I need to know if she is on Brackwell."

"I can tell you whether there is a Brackwell. I cannot tell you who is on it. That is a different thing and it is the ward's and not mine, and the ward is round the corner and they are on their break until two."

"She is eighty-eight. Her name is Doolan. Maureen Doolan."

"I am not going to write that down, sir, because I have written it down before for people and it has gone on a piece of paper that goes upstairs, and I do not know what happens to it up there and neither do you." She came round the side of the desk, which is the first thing she had done all day that was not the counter. "What I will do is walk you round to the ward door at two o'clock and ask them to come out and speak to you, and if they will not, I will not be able to help you and I will not pretend otherwise. Do you want that, or do you want the truth?"

"The truth."

"Then the truth is that I do not know, and that I have said so, and that you may come back tomorrow and I will say the same thing in the same voice." She went back behind the desk. "Corridor, four o'clock. If you are not here at four I will not have remembered you and I would not want to pretend that I have."

The man said that he would be there at four, and did not go away, and stood in the entrance hall with his carrier bag for about an hour and ten minutes and was still standing there at half past one, and Petronella Osei did not ask him to, and brought him a cup of tea at half past twelve without having decided to, and Elias Venn stood at the end of the corridor and watched a member of the public wait in a hospital over a name, and thought about a page of a public manual with a third box in it, and about the four hundred people who had been at a funding review that morning, and about how a thing is a document or it is a man and there is nothing in between and a great deal of the trouble in the world is in the nothing in between.

"I have been standing," he said, to the nurse.

"Then sit down, because you are white." She put a blanket over the back of a chair in the corridor and made him sit in it, and did not ask him one question in the whole of it, and went away.

He sat in a corridor with a blanket over his knees for about fifty minutes.

He did not read the notices and he did not go into the day room twice and he kept his feet in front of him, and at about twenty past twelve a nurse came the length of the corridor and gave him a cup of tea without having asked him whether he wanted one, and he said thank you, and understood while she was still walking away that this was the whole of the arrangement: nobody had asked him what he was, and so nobody had had to decide whether to keep him.

He had been assumed to be somebody's husband for eleven minutes and he had let it stand, and he worked out while he was sitting there that it was the most useful thing that had happened to him in four days, because **a person who does not ask what is being done is the most useful shape a witness can have**, and he had spent two months learning that off a woman of fifty-eight who had learned it out of a form.

At five past one a doctor came down the corridor with a folder and stopped at the chair.

"Right," she said. "You are the husband."

"I am not anybody's husband."

"Then you are the husband of somebody on Brackwell who has not come, and either way you are the chair." She said it kindly and she had already turned to go. "I am going to ask you something and I would rather ask you than guess, and it is not about the bed. **Who are you.**"

"I do not read people," said Elias Venn. "I have never read a person in my life and I am not going to start this afternoon. I am not a police officer and I am not a clinician. I am a member of the public and I have been formally reprimanded for touching something I should not have touched, and I have not put a hand on a person or a thing in this building, and that is the only reason I am allowed to be sitting in this chair."

The doctor stopped.

"Say the last part again."

"I have not put a hand on anything in this building," said Elias Venn. "Not a document, not a door, not a bed, and not a person. I have been in the day room since Monday and I have read a noticeboard and a laminated card and a menu and that is the whole of what I have touched, and on Monday morning I held a door for a woman with a trolley because she asked me to."

"That is not a sentence anybody says in a hospital."

"It is a sentence I have been saying in various rooms since September," he said, "and I have said it out loud in front of a police officer and in front of nineteen people in a waiting room on Marshall Street, and I am saying it out loud in a corridor because there are three patients behind that curtain and a nurse, and you are a doctor, and I would like there to be a record of it that I said it here first."

The nurse at the end of the corridor had stopped with a tray.

"Say the bit about the reprimand again," said the doctor.

"Departmental censure on the twenty-third of October and a formal reprimand on the third of November, both of them mine, and both of them about the same afternoon, and the second one is on a shelf in a repair shop six inches from a certificate that says a man of seventy-eight performed a complete dangerous working in a clinic and got a licence out of it. My access to four sample numbers is withdrawn and my trade account is gone and I have a till with twenty-two credits in it. That is the whole of my standing in this city and I am telling you all of it in a corridor because you have asked me twice."

The doctor looked at him for a moment and then said, "I do not have any way of checking any of that and I am not going to try. If you touch a person in this wing I will have you removed and it will not be a conversation. If you do not, you may sit in that chair. The blanket is clean and it goes back on the trolley."

She went. The nurse with the tray went. Three people behind a curtain said nothing at all, and one of them coughed once.

---

He was on the Aldergate ward between half past one and half past two because Marguerite came and found him and said "you can come and watch, and if anybody asks you are the husband, and I will say you are not and you can say it yourself."

He had never been on a rehabilitation ward. He had been on one in a building in Kettle Rows on the seventeenth of September and again on the twentieth and again on the thirtieth of October, and he had never once been on one where nobody was in danger.

**It is a room with fourteen beds in it and eleven people in it and a great deal of time.**

There was a man of about sixty in the second bed who was being taught to hold a cup with two hands, and it was going badly, and the physiotherapist was saying "no, love, not that one" every forty seconds, and neither of them was enjoying it and both of them were staying, which was the entire craft and neither of them would have called it that.

There was a woman of about seventy-nine in the ninth bed who was on her fourth day back after a hip and who had brought a crossword board with her and was doing it in bed, and who asked him to move a table, and who said sorry for asking, four times, in about ninety seconds, and he moved the table four times.

And at the foot of the ninth bed there was a card in a plastic holder, and on the card there was a name he had not seen since the first of November.

> **FUNDER OF RECORD: AEGIS MUTUAL — CLAIM NO. TO FOLLOW — ACCOUNT SERIES 44-31 — WING: ALDERGATE — BED 9**

Everything else on the card was blank, because everything else on it was filled in later, and the pen on a string from the bed to the card was tied to the bed frame so that a patient could not reach it and could not lose it either, and the woman in bed nine had not tried.

"She asks me every morning," said Marguerite. "Whether it means her daughter is being charged. And I say no, and it is not a lie, and it is also not the whole answer, and I have been saying *no* to that question for six years and about forty women a year, and I have never once been able to finish the sentence."

"What finishes it?"

"Nothing finishes it. That is the problem with it." Marguerite put the cup down. "The card is right. The funder is right. The bed is right. The account is right. And the woman asks a question that is not on the card and cannot be on the card, and there is no other card, and I am the only person in this building who can hear the question, and I cannot answer it, and I have stopped pretending that I am going to."

---

He stayed all afternoon and he did not say anything of use to anybody, and at ten to four the linen run started and the second lift, which is locked between ten and eleven in the morning and open the rest of the day because the linen goes down in the morning and up in the afternoon, was standing open with a trolley in it.

He got in. It was a mistake. He had been in the building six hours and he had not asked a single question of a single patient and he was tired in the way a man is tired when he has been careful for six hours, and the lift was open and there was a trolley in it.

It went up.

The third floor was a corridor with six doors and a notice on the stair head and nothing in the corridor at all.

> **THIRLMERE WARD — CLOSED 7 OCTOBER 2029 — 6 BEDS DECANTED**
> **RESIDENCE AND EFFECTS REMAIN ON SITE. NO ADMITTANCE WITHOUT THE MANAGER'S WRITTEN AUTHORITY.**

Below it, in a different hand and a different pen, somebody had written on the same sheet of card with a blue biro, and the word was *still*, and the *still* had been underlined twice.

The doors were locked. He did not try them. There was a chair of wood pushed up against one of them and there was another chair of wood against the wall at the end of the corridor with its legs in a rectangle worn in the lino, which is what a chair looks like when it is put in the same place every day and then put back.

He stood in the corridor and looked at six doors for about a minute, and then a linen trolley came up the corridor from the other end, and the woman pushing it was the same woman from Monday morning with a different trolley, and she did not stop, and she said, as she went past:

"You're on a shut floor, love. There's nothing up here but what's in the rooms."

She went on up. The trolley's wheels went quiet about thirty feet away at the end of the corridor where the floor turned, and after that there was nothing at all on the third floor of the Aldergate Wing for about two hundred yards in either direction, and Elias Venn stood on it on a Wednesday afternoon at ten past four with his hat in his hand.
