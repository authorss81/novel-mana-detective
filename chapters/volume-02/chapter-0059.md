# Chapter 0059 — A False Start

The room over the supermarket on Sallow Lane had a bell on the door and a table and two chairs and a lamp, exactly as it had been described to him by a man standing in a street eleven minutes' walk away who had not gone in, and Elias Venn thought about that for the whole of the four weeks afterwards and never once said so to anybody.

It had six people in it at twenty to four on Tuesday afternoon. It also had a card table with a cash tin on it and a printed sheet on a clipboard and a queue on the stairs, and it had a smell of hot sugar and a chair against the wall for anybody who wanted to sit out, and it had a poster on the wall about the cost of living that had been up since August and had a corner turned over and put back about nine times.

There were three men and two women and a child of about nine sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and a book on her knees, and the child was the only one in the room who had not come to be worked on.

"You are late," said the woman at the card table. "You are on at four and it is ten to four and I do not have you on the board, and I do not have your name."

"My name is not on anything," said Elias Venn. "I am not on your board and I am not a practitioner and I am not a clinician and I am not a police officer, and I have told the person who sent me all four of those things in that order, and she told me to say them to you in that order, and I am saying them in that order because if I do not say them in that order somebody in this room is going to be harmed on Friday."

The woman at the card table looked at him for about three seconds and then said, "Sitting-out chair's behind you. Don't touch the table. Don't talk in the session. If she asks you a question you say *she'll answer that*, and if you can't manage that then say *not now*."

---

Mara Quill was on the stairs with her case closed and her hood up.

"I have set four things and you agreed to them in a corridor at Saint Orra on Sunday, and I am saying them again on a staircase because a staircase is where I can say them."

"Four."

"One. I am not in the room as a practitioner, and she has agreed. Two. I am not touching anything, including a table. Three. I speak once, at the beginning and not at the end, because at the end it is a thing everybody says. Four." She shifted the case. "**If the room starts to run I will say your name and you will not turn round, and I want that agreed now while there are no witnesses, because you will not be able to hear me otherwise.**"

"Why would I not hear you."

"Because on the seventeenth of September you were in a room with a woman on a couch and a man on the floor saying *don't let go of that* twice with your eyes shut, and I was counting seconds out loud from the doorway and I had got past five hundred and forty." She did not soften it. "You do not hear anything for about four minutes. So I will say your name, and if you do not come round then I am going to have to decide whether to touch a stranger's letter in a room with a child in it, and I have thought about that for two days and I do not know what I would do, and I am telling you that I do not know so that you know it too."

"Agreed."

"Good." She came down two steps and then stopped. "And I am not going to be able to help you in there, and I want that said on a staircase and not in a room, because in a room people say *of course I will help you* and then they do not."

He had been in a room with forty-one people in it on the thirtieth of October, and the walking stick had gone out of a gate four feet past a doctor and a nurse, and it had worked, and it had worked because four other people had done it. He said that out loud on a staircase on Sallow Lane and Mara Quill said yes and went down.

---

Nadia Kest was on the pavement at ten past four with a folded sheet of paper in her inside pocket and the second sheet rolled in her hand.

"Before you go up," she said. "I am going to be in this room at about half past and I am going to put a man through a door, and I have got a warrant in my pocket and I signed for it at Vine Street at two o'clock this afternoon, and I have got an application in my own hand with a time on it, and the application is on the strength of the protection order that came back on Friday and a statement from a doctor, and I am going to read the statement and the order in a room with six people in it because that is the procedure and because I have watched two men in this case be told no by a room.

"You are not on the application. You are not a witness. **If you are in that room you are a member of the public asked in by a stranger, and if it goes wrong that is the sentence, and I cannot stop it, because a stairwell is a common part and I have no power over who stands in it.**"

"You are angry."

"I am extremely angry and I have been angry since Wednesday, and I am going to be angry at you in a room in about twenty minutes, and I would like it on the record that I told you here first." She put the rolled sheet away as well. "What I am not going to do is tell you to leave. A man who says *I am not a police officer* to a woman at a card table in front of six people is doing something useful, and I have been on the other side of that door twice in twenty-two years and I have always wanted somebody in the room who had said it out loud first."

---

Denny Roche was fifty-one and he had come in at ten past three and he was on the second chair and he had a coat folded on his knee and his hands on the coat, and he was not the practitioner.

"Eleven months," he said, when Elias Venn got a stool. "Since the March before last. Kerry's the girl. She's thirty-one. Since March she has been calling me by her aunt's name, and I have corrected her, and she has been *upset* about being corrected, which is worse than the first month, because in the first month she did not know. Now she knows and she does it on purpose because it is two minutes and it means the world is the right way up.

"And the aunt died in the February. So I have got a woman who is thirty-one calling me by the name of a woman who is dead, and the woman who is dead is my sister, and I have been to a doctor about it and about two other things and the doctor's word is *grief* and I have been to a third one and the third one's word is *referral*." He did not look up. "And this lady downstairs says she can do it for ninety credits and it is on the card and it is cash, and I have got ninety credits, and it is the twenty-sixth of November, and I have been coming since the middle of October to see whether I could do it in my kitchen for nothing and I cannot, because I do not know the order of it, and there is a book about it in a shop on Cutter Street and I have not got the nerve to sit in a library."

"She can do it, and she can do it today," said Elias Venn, "and I have not got anything to do with it."

"Then why are you sitting here."

"Because somebody has to," said Elias Venn, "and because the child is in the room and nobody has asked the child."

---

The session began at four and there were four people in it.

The child on the floor was called Aoife and she was nine and she had a book about a pony in it, and at one point she read a line of it out loud to nobody, and nobody told her to be quiet, and the woman at the card table looked at her once and did not say anything, and Elias Venn thought about that for a long time afterwards.

There was a sheet of paper on the table with a line on it for a signature, and the sheet was not a form and had no heading and no number, and on the line, in a careful hand, somebody had written a name. On the table beside it, flat, was a letter.

The letter was in a child's hand and it was folded in three and it was about six inches by four, and it was creased through the signature, and the signature was the name on the sheet.

Elias Venn did not touch it for eight minutes.

He sat on a stool by the wall with his hands in his lap. A plate is not admissible unless it is on a register, and Mara Quill's is not, and a piece of paper in a room above a shop on Sallow Lane was not even a plate, and he was a member of the public with a formal reprimand on him, and there was a man in the room who had put ninety credits in an envelope and had been told three times that it was not a thing anybody can do.

At four minutes past four he put two fingers on the corner of the letter, on the fold, and only on the fold.

---

The room went up out of the floor the way it always did, and it was not a room, and there was no furniture in it and there were no faces in it, and there was a sheet of paper and a letter and a child's handwriting and a child in it who was not the child on the floor and had never been.

There was an anchor, and it was the letter, and it was in a child's hand, and it was about eleven years old and it had been read more times than any other object in the building.

There was an address, and it was a nine-year-old girl on the floor with a book about a pony in it, and that was the worst thing Elias Venn had ever been in the middle of, and it was the worst thing because it was ordinary and legal-looking and required nobody to be evil.

There was a turn, and it had been done. It had been done properly. It was a well-made turn, done by a competent person who knew the method and had a room and a bell on the door and a printed sheet on a clipboard, and the residue of it came up out of the letter like a tide going out.

And then the order **stopped.**

It stopped about a fifth of the way in. It did not fade and it did not get thin. It stopped, and there was a mark where it had stopped, and the mark was the *beginning* of something and not the middle of it, and the whole of the order stood there with a fifth of itself in front of it and nothing behind it and a mark in the gap that was the mark of a thing starting.

And then it started again, from a different place. Not from where it had stopped. From about a third of the way in, on the other side, and it ran on to the end, and the end of it was a release, and the release was seated, and it was waiting for a hand with a pen in it to put a signature on a line on a sheet of paper on a table.

Elias Venn came off the letter with his back against the wall of a room above a supermarket, the left side of his head gone the way it goes, and said it out loud in order, in the flat voice of a man saying numbers aloud so that somebody else does the arithmetic.

"Anchor. A letter in a child's hand, about eleven years old, read more times than anything else in the building, and it is not the child's who wrote it and it is the address. **Address. The girl on the floor.** I have not touched her. I am not reading her, I have not been in the same material as her for four seconds and I have said that out loud for a witness. Turn. Done. Properly done, by somebody who knows the method, and I cannot tell you whether it was done to her or done for her and that is not a distinction anybody in this room can make and I am including me in that. Release. Seated. It is not going to happen when the girl leaves the room. It is going to happen when somebody puts a signature on that line with a pen, and then the letter will be complete, and the complete letter will be in a file, and the file will say that a woman of thirty-one called a man by the wrong name and that a woman of fifty-eight made it stop, and the girl will be in the file as the place it was done."

"Who is going to sign it," said Nadia Kest, from the doorway.

"The woman at the card table. She is the only other person in this room who has a chair and she has had one since I came in, and she has not written anything down for twenty minutes."

"And can you stop it."

"No." Elias Venn said it before anybody had finished asking, and he said it out loud in front of a police officer for the second time in ninety days, and the first time had been on the thirtieth of October in a room with forty-one people in it. "**I have told you six times since September that I cannot stop a working and I am telling you in front of a witness in a room with a child in it. I cannot stop it. There is nothing I can do to that letter and there is nothing I can do to that girl. I can tell you the order of it, and I have just told you the order of it, and it is the only thing I have, and it has been the only thing I have since the seventeenth of September.**"

Nadia Kest had her hand on the door frame and she did not move for about two seconds.

"Keep going," she said. "Keep standing there and keep talking. Two minutes. I am going to do a thing and I am going to do it fast."

> **MARGIN — The order starts, stops, and starts again from a different place. The gap is not a pause. Nothing in this room has a pause in it.**

"Elias."

He did not turn round.

"That is a false start," said Mara Quill, from the doorway. "That is what that is, and I have been looking for one since September and I have never seen one, and neither of us can tell one from a turn, and I am telling you the word because somebody has to and it is not going to be you. The order began and it was stopped and it left its first mark in the material, and the mark in the gap is a *begin* and not a *middle*, and that is the whole of what I know and I have known it for two months and I have never had anything to look at."

"Then say it in a sentence somebody can act on," said Elias Venn.

"I cannot act on it," said Mara Quill. "**Neither of us can act on it. You cannot stop a working. What you can do is say out loud what is about to happen before it happens, in order, so that the rest of us can act on it, and you have just done that, and I have been in this building since I was twenty-two and that is the first time I have seen anybody do it.** Do that and stay out of the rest of it."

---

Nadia Kest went out into the street and came back in with a folded sheet of paper and said the words on it, and the words on it were a warrant for the arrest of a person unknown in respect of a restricted procedure, and they were valid for a building on Sallow Lane because the building was one of eleven unnamed premises in a temporary protection order made at the request of the director of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic and served on the fourteenth of November, and the thing that stopped the working at twenty-five to five on the twenty-sixth of November was a piece of paper with a date and an end date on it that a doctor had put her registration number to and a member of the public had walked eleven minutes out of the rain to ask for.

The working stopped at 16:31. Not because the turn was undone. Because the release needed a signature, and the person who was going to give it was in a corridor with two officers either side of her and a sheet of paper in front of her, and a release that is not committed is not a release. What was left in the letter was a turn with nothing on the end of it, and that has to be dealt with by somebody with a pen, and nobody in the corridor had one.

The daughter kept her name. She has been calling her father by her aunt's name since March, and from half past four on the twenty-sixth of November she has been calling him by his own, and they have both been crying in a stairwell on Sallow Lane, and neither of them knows yet what has been taken, and will not until about the middle of January.

Denny Roche's own name did not come back. That is the honest sentence and there is no better one. He went to the woman downstairs the following Friday and she did the ninety-credit thing and he paid it, and at the end of March he was still not able to say his own name out loud without stopping first, and he has told nobody but the woman and one person in a shop on Marshall Street who buys kettles and is not a policeman and did not ask.

---

It was twenty past six when Elias Venn got to the back room at 14 Kettle Rows and the paraffin heater was on and the cat was asleep on the bench on the certificate, and he did not go near either of them.

Nadia Kest had written in her notebook, at half past four, in the same hand she had used since the thirtieth of October, and had put it in the file, and had read it to him in a shop doorway later and let him write it out, and it is in his notebook in his own writing and not hers.

> *Reading reported by the officer, from contact, 16:04 to 16:19, on one sheet of paper on a table, in a room with six people and a child in it. No person was examined. No living trace was entered. No object was removed, photographed or seized. The witness, M. Quill, was in the room at the officer's request and at her own conditions and touched nothing. I have not estimated a duration and there is no estimate in this note.*

He wrote his own four lines underneath it, which is the practice, and which is four lines rather than one, because a man who has lost the use of an afternoon ought not to be allowed one tidy sentence about it.

> *26 November, Sallow Lane. Sixteen-oh-four to sixteen-nineteen. A false start. I did not know the word and M. Quill did and she was in the room because I asked her to be and on four conditions she wrote herself, and the first condition was that she would say my name and I would not hear it, and she was right about that as well.*
> *A turn done properly with no release on it at the end of it, and a child on the floor, and a sheet of paper with a line for a signature on it, and a woman downstairs who has been to three doctors' waiting rooms and a card table and did not put her own name on anything.*
> *I did not stop it. I said the order out loud and two officers and a document with an end date on it stopped it, and if the order had not been said out loud at sixteen-oh-four they would have gone in at four and there would have been a pen on that table at twenty to five.*

Then he got out the pencil and turned to a fresh page, because he had written three lines and he had a habit of writing the fourth one about himself, and the fourth one is the one he is supposed to write, and on this page the fourth one was not about himself and he did not know it for about ninety seconds.

> *And then I sat on the stool in the back room and it came back, the way the order of a day came back in a corridor in October and the way the route came back on a shop step in the same month, and it is a thing I have had three times in four months and each of them has been about how long, or where, or in what order, and this one is different and this one is not a memory of a thing that happened.*
> *I remember a car park. I remember it being about a car park outside a building on Morrow Hill and it being a Sunday, and there being a woman in a coat, and I remember saying that I would not read a living person again, and I remember her not arguing, and I remember putting my hand on the roof of a car and saying that if I ever did it I would want somebody to stop me and that it should be her. I remember it in her voice. I remember the sentence and I remember it being said twice.*
> *That conversation has not happened. I have looked. There is no car park in this book on a Sunday in November and there is nothing in it about a living person and there is nothing in it at all after the twenty-second and before the third, and the third of November is a Saturday, and I have read four months of my own handwriting from the back of the book forward twice tonight and I have found no Sunday and no car park.*
> *I do not know what this is. I have got three readings in four months and two of them cost me something I wanted back and this one has cost me the distinction between a thing that happened and a thing I intend to do. I am going to write the date down because a man who will not write the date down is a man who will decide later that it did not happen. It is a Sunday. It is the sixteenth of December 1929. It is nineteen days away and I am going to have to get through nineteen days of a conversation I have already had, and I have not got anybody to tell.*

The pencil went down on the bench at ten to nine at night on the twenty-sixth of November 1929, and the cat came down off the certificate and went along the top of the till and looked at the drawer, and the till had twenty-two credits in it, and outside on Marshall Street a woman in a coat was walking past a shop with a bell on the door four streets away and did not go in.
