# Chapter 0076 — Four Days of Tablets

There was one envelope on the wire rack on Saturday morning at ten past ten that Doreen Tillery had been looking at for about forty minutes without touching it, and it had a paper clip bent double on it, and it was the eleventh one down, and it was not Mrs Ferrers's, and it had not been Mrs Ferrers's since the middle of November.

"You put it face down."

"I put it face down because it is Saturday and the dispensary shuts at half past twelve and Mrs Ferrers does not come on a Saturday, and because the man who is covering the day shift has not been in since Monday and has now told me he is not coming on Monday either." She put her hands in the sink. "And because you are standing there."

"Has she phoned?"

"She has not phoned. She has never once phoned in four years. That is what I have been telling you about since Monday and you have not been listening to any of it." Doreen Tillery shut the tap. "Elias. A repeat is released against a completed form. That is not a rule anybody made, it is a rule because a prescription is a request to a pharmacy to give a person a drug, and if a person has not told anybody where she lives then the pharmacy has got nowhere to send the warning about the batch, and the warning is not a formality, it is a card with a hospital's telephone number on it, and one woman in this district died in about 2019 of a drug that two pharmacies sent to a flat where nobody lived, and nobody's name was on the envelope of either one of them.

"So a completed form. And a completed form is filled in by the person, in the room, with a member of staff, out loud, and the whole reason it is filled in out loud is that I do not fill in a box on behalf of a person about a thing I have not asked them. And on the form that Mrs Ferrers has had in her bag for three weeks there is a box in the wrong place and a head on it that is not mine, and I have said *not that one* every Friday for three weeks and she has said *please* every Friday for three weeks, and yesterday a man stood in my back room and told me there was a working on it."

"Did he say a working."

"He said an order, and a release, and a thing called a false start, and then he said he had got the false start wrong and that it was a figure written twice." Doreen Tillery turned round. "**And I have been in this room fourteen years and I believed him for about four seconds before he said the second half, and I want you to understand that those four seconds are the whole of my professional life and they are not going to leave.**"

---

Ahmed came in at half past eleven to do the door and the bins and the six o'clock check, and he stood in the corridor with a mop and listened to two people argue for nineteen minutes and did not go into either of them, and at one point he said, "This is the same as the thing on the board," and then he said nothing else at all, which was the most useful thing he could have done.

"You are going to be the reason a woman misses four days of a drug," said Doreen Tillery, "and I want you to hear me say that to you in front of a witness, and the witness is him, and I am saying it because I have been saying the soft version to myself since yesterday afternoon and the soft version is that a man was wrong in a room and a woman was careful, and the true version is that on Friday at ten past four I looked at a member of the public in my own dispensary and decided that a woman I have served every Friday for four years was less important than a piece of paper I do not understand."

"I have not asked you to weigh anything."

"You did not have to ask me. You said it in the room." She took her glasses off. "And here is the other half of it and I am entitled to it. You came into a building on Friday afternoon and said a thing out loud that I repeated to my colleague at the front counter at five o'clock, and I repeated it because I was shaken and because he asked me directly and I did not have a better answer, and by Saturday morning there are three people in this building who know that a man in a shop can see an order in a piece of paper, and one of them is Ahmed, who is a porter and who is not qualified in anything at all, and he is the one who is going to be standing at that counter on Monday morning when the next one comes in."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair at all," said Doreen Tillery. "It is the correct order of it. The fair version of what happened on Friday is that a woman of sixty-one has a form she cannot get filled in and a man came and looked at it. That is the fair version. The version that is going round this building is not that. **And you did that, and I gave you the room, and neither of us is going to be able to explain the difference to Mrs Ferrers on Monday morning, and she is not going to be the one who has to.**"

"Then let me tell her."

"No." Doreen Tillery shook her head. "You will not. Because what would you say? A man looked at a form and said there was something on it, and then said he had got the something wrong? That is a sentence with no floor under it and I have watched four people say a sentence like that in this city and every one of them was believed for about a day. The only sentence with a floor under it is the one I am going to say to her on Monday morning, and it is going to be about a box in the wrong place on a form that is not ours, and it is going to be true, and it is going to be the only thing she is told."

Elias Venn stood in a corridor of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic on a Saturday morning at a quarter to twelve and found that he had been right to look and wrong to say, in the same four minutes, by two women who were not being kind to him, and that both of those were going to have to be true for the rest of his life.

"Then can she have her tablets on Monday," he said.

Doreen Tillery looked at him.

"That is the first question anybody has asked me about any of this," she said, "and it is the right one, and here is the answer and it is not a good one. She can have them on Monday morning if I can get a form into her hand, and I cannot get a form into her hand, because a note on a file is not a form. A repeat comes off that rack against a completed form. That is the whole of the rule and I did not write it. I have told her Monday, because I have told that woman Monday for four years, and I have told her every week since the middle of November, and Monday is going to be the fourth week in a row that I have told her a thing I could not do, and I have done that three times."

---

There is no Saturday dispensary at the Kettle Rows Community Clinic and there has not been one for six years, and about a quarter past three on the twenty-second of December a woman of about thirty came in with a child of about four and a prescription in a plastic wallet and asked whether it could be done, and the answer was no, and it was said kindly, and the woman did not argue, and she said *of course* and then stood in the waiting room for another two minutes before she left, holding the wallet, doing the arithmetic on what Monday was going to be.

That is in the record of the day because Dr Okafor writes down every Saturday in the waiting-room book for the staffing count, and it is the only line in it that is about anybody at all, and it reads *one prescription returned, one child, no other.*

Sana Okafor was in the waiting room at three o'clock with a cup of tea she had not made and about a list of eleven names in her head, and she did not ask him to sit down.

"Two things," she said. "One. I have not told Doreen. I have not told Enid. I have not told Ahmed and I am not going to, and I want you to hear the reason, because you will not like it and you are not going to be able to argue with it.

"There are three people in that dispensary. If it goes round that there is something about the forms, then in about a fortnight the sentence will not be *a man in a shop said a false thing in a back room*. It will be *there is something about the forms.* And then nobody fills in a form. Not a false one, not one with a head on it that is not mine, not any of them. Because the sentence that is left will be *we do not know about the forms* and that sentence is a wall, and I have watched a wall go up in a waiting room in about a fortnight twice in nineteen years and I did not manage to take either of them down." She drank the tea she had not made. "So the false thing stays in a back room with me and one member of my staff, and the true thing goes to a woman of sixty-one on Monday morning in about eleven words, and eleven words is the correct price and it is a cheap price and I have got no argument about it and I am not looking for one from you.

"Two. You had one thing to do and it took eleven seconds. You had four days."

"I know."

"You did not know. That is what I want to be fair about, because I am going to be thinking about this for a long time and I would rather think about it fairly." She put the cup down. "There was a woman in the doorway with a form in her hand and there was about forty seconds in which you could have come out of that room and stood in this corridor and said *there is a form and I am going to look at it*, and I would have stood in the doorway and I would have watched you be wrong in front of a patient, which is what happened, and it would have been a different thing entirely, because a man who says *I am going to look at this and I may be wrong* is a man who can be wrong again in four years, and I can work with that. **You did not say that. You said the thing.** And there is no version of this where the thing you said was not a false thing, and there is a version where it cost a woman of sixty-one four days of a drug, and there is a version where it did not, and I do not get to pick."

"I do not either."

"No," said Sana Okafor. "You do not, and that is new, and I have had a member of the public argue with me in this waiting room four times since September and every one of them was a man explaining why a thing he wanted was different from a thing it was."

---

Enid Marlow was in the back office at half past three with the drawer open and the sheet in front of her and the wire rack's list on the pad beside it, and she had her finger on the *send to* column.

"Nine seconds," she said, before Elias Venn had said anything. "I want that on the record in whatever way you are keeping records, because I have been nineteen years in this building and I have never needed nine seconds for anything and I have just needed nine seconds for this, and I would like it noted that it took nine seconds and that nobody made me do it.

"That woman has a repeat and she has never been able to give you an address, and the address column on this sheet is full, and I write it down every time, and there are eleven names on it and none of them has ever had their own name on that column. And a repeat comes off a shelf when a form is completed, and a form cannot be completed without an address." She put her finger on two lines about three inches apart. "Eleven names and eleven envelopes. They have been the same eleven for about four months and I have never once put the two pieces of paper side by side, and I did it this afternoon because a member of the public stood in my office and looked at both of them and I am not going to be a woman who has had a man's eyesight in my office for two hours and has not used it."

"Has anybody else in this building ever put them side by side?"

"No."

"And nobody will now."

"Nobody will now," said Enid Marlow. "And that is not a scandal, that is a building. There are about forty people in here and four of us know what is on that sheet and three of us knew the count on the rack, and none of the three of us is going to put the two together out loud in a corridor, because the minute we do, somebody in a corridor will do it in a corridor, and it will be in a corridor in a fortnight, and then we will have told the eleven."

She shut the drawer.

"And I want you to go away and take that with you and not come back and ask me about it," said Enid Marlow, "because you have the two pieces and you have not put them together, and I would have put them together on the first of December if you had come in on the first of December, and I have thought about that for an hour and it is your fault and it is not your fault and I do not know which, and I am sixty-three, and I am going to go home now."

---

He did not go to 4 Corrie Street, and a woman was working on the first floor of a building four streets away, and she had told him not to come, and he had sent a third copy of a fair document to her by post on the eleventh of December with four stamps on it and his own name on the outside, and she had read it on the fourteenth before work and had written back one line, and the line said *Read it. Kept it. Do not come.*

He got to Marshall Street at ten past six and the day man had still not been in and Ahmed was doing the door.

"You are the last one," said Ahmed. "I have been the last one since Monday and there is a form of words for it and the form of words is *covering*."

"You were in the corridor this morning."

"I was in the corridor this morning and I heard about two thirds of it and I have not said anything about the third of it I did not hear, and I have been thinking about the two thirds all day." Ahmed had the gate lamp in his hand and was holding it the way a man holds a thing he is about to put back. "I am going to say one thing and then I am going to put this lamp back and go home, and I have never said anything to anybody in this building that I have not been asked for.

"I have told two people what you said. Not about the false start. About the other thing. The part where you said the order out loud so that somebody else could do the arithmetic. I have told two people in a dispensary that there is a man who comes in and says what is going to happen before it happens, and I have said it because in nine weeks of working in this building it is the first true thing anybody has said in it, and I do not know what to do with that, and I would tell them again, and if it turns out to be a woman writing a figure twice then it is a woman writing a figure twice and I shall have to live in a building with that.

"And I have not told Doreen that you were wrong, because she knows you were wrong, she was in the room." He put the lamp back on its bracket. "You were wrong on Friday, and the wrong thing is going to be the only thing anybody in this building remembers in a year, and you knew that when you said it, because you have been in this city for four months and you are not a fool.

"So there is the price. You will not be able to fix it. I have watched about nine men in my life say *I will put it right* and not one of them ever has, and the ones who were any good did not say it. So do not say it to me, and go home, and come in on Monday at nine when it is quiet, and if it is a pen then it is a pen, and if it is not a pen then you will want to be in this building on Monday at nine."
