# Chapter 0065 — The Floor

Aegis Mutual's claims assessment office was on the fourth floor of a building on Verge Row with a brass plate and a revolving door and a man on the door who was not anybody in particular and let him through without a card.

She had written back on a card. It was the size of a postcard and it had a printed heading and a ruled box for a time, and the time was Friday the seventh of December at ten o'clock, and under the box it said *ten minutes* in a different type from the box, and at the bottom it said *no further correspondence will be entered on this file unless it concerns the file.*

"She is on time," said the man on the door, before Elias Venn said anything. "Second floor is claims and third is claims and the assessment side is fourth, and if you have been put in the second-floor waiting room, go up."

---

The room was on the fourth floor and had a glass wall on two sides and the city going past it, and a table with four chairs and a jug of water that nobody had poured from, and a woman of about fifty at the end of the table who had a coat over the back of her chair and a telephone on silent and two files on the table in front of her, and she had been in the room before he came in, because the room was warm and the water was not new.

"Mr Venn. Sit down. I am Selene Rook and I am a claims director, which means that on a Friday I am the person who decides whether a file is worth arguing about, and I have decided about yours in about four seconds, and I would like to start by telling you that, so that you do not spend the ten minutes hoping."

"I have not got a file."

"You have got a file," said Selene Rook. "You have had one since Thursday morning, because you wrote to this office on Wednesday evening, and I read your letter on the Thursday, and there is a note on it in my hand that says *a member of the public, one letter, no form, no account, asking whether a certificate exists for a bed on a day, and whether the funder's name is on it*. And I will tell you for nothing that the second half of that letter is why you got an appointment, because the first half of it I get nine of, and the second half I get none."

"I have not."

"Good. I will be right about that by the time you leave and I will not tell you how."

She put the two files into a drawer and shut it, which is the first thing that happened in the room that was not a conversation.

"Now. What do you want."

"The certificate."

"No."

"You have not asked what I want it for."

"I do not need to, and if I asked, you would tell me, and it would be true, and I would still say no, and we would both have wasted four minutes." She turned the water jug a quarter turn so that the handle was facing him, which is a thing people do with their hands. "**A certificate is issued to the payer. You are not the payer. A member of the public is not a party to a claim, and if you were, this conversation would be a different one in a different room with two of my colleagues in it.**"

"Then what are you able to do."

"I am able to tell you that the certificate exists, because you asked me a general question in writing and a general question in writing is free. I am able to tell you that it was issued on the second of October by a district office on Harrow Lane, and I am not going to tell you the number, and you did not ask for it in the letter, and I notice that you did not ask for it, and I have been in this job nineteen years and a man who does not ask for a number in a first letter has usually worked out that he cannot use one."

"I could not use it."

"You could not use it. That is correct and I have never known a member of the public who could." She uncapped a pen. "I am also able to give you two documents, and I am going to give you both of them, and I want to be exact about why, because if I do not say why, then in about four years somebody will say that a claims director handed a member of the public two documents and it will sound like a leak. **They are mine. They are about my own department's money and my own department's mistake. If they were about anything else you would get nothing and I would be right.**"

---

She gave them a plastic wallet with two sheets in it and did not let go of it until he had it, and the first was headed **INTERNAL MEMORANDUM — ASSESSMENT — CLAIMS 44-31 SERIES** and was dated the fourth of November, and the second was on the same paper and was dated the sixth of November, and the first had a name in a box at the top and the second did not have a name in any box at all.

He read them on the table and did not move them.

The first said that a claims officer, in the course of a routine review of a rehabilitation claim on the 44-31 series settled on the eleventh of September, had observed that the countersignature on the claim form was not in the hand of the attending clinician named on the form; that the clinician had confirmed that the form was not signed by her; and that, the officer said, in a single line which he read twice, **while a bed number and a service code are each sufficient for settlement, the countersignature on this form is the only attestation of a person, and I am obliged to record that it is not one.**

The second said: *No further action. The claim is within the schedule and the schedule reference is sufficient.*

Elias Venn put the wallet down on the table between them.

"You raised it," he said.

"A claims officer raised it. I did not raise it; I dealt with it two days later, which is a different and worse thing and I am not going to pretend otherwise."

"You closed it."

"I did not close it. **I did not close it, and that is the part I have never been able to say out loud in nineteen years, and I am saying it to you now, in this room, on a Friday, because you are going to be asked about it by somebody who is cleverer than me in about two years and I would rather you had my sentence than the other one.** I dealt with the memorandum. I applied the schedule. The schedule says a bed number and a service code are each sufficient for settlement. The schedule is a real document, it is printed, it is two years old, and it does not say that a counter-signature is necessary. I had a document in front of me that told me what was sufficient and I applied it and a matter went away, and nobody signed the closing, because there is no box on the second sheet for a signature, because **the second sheet is not a decision. The second sheet is a note. Somebody wrote a note and put it in a file and went home and it is the reason there is a certificate in a drawer that nobody in this building can see round the edge of.**"

"Who closed it?"

"I have told you the answer. There is no name, and I have looked, and there is a box on the first sheet and the box has a name in it, and I am not going to give you the name, and not because I am protecting anybody, and I want that to be clear, because you are about to think I am protecting somebody." She closed the pen. "I am protecting the shape of a form. **If I give you a name, then in four years there is a document in a room with a member of the public's name on the outside of it and an officer's name on the inside of it, and that document is the only thing in this city that says what a person in this building did, and the first person who reads it will be a person with a reason, and the reason will be the case, not the money.**"

She let that sit for about four seconds, and she did not look at him while she let it sit.

"There is also a hand," said Elias Venn.

"There is a hand."

"That is not a person."

"It is a hand," said Selene Rook. "And I have compared it, and it is not the clinician's, and I cannot tell you whose it is, and I have had it looked at by two people who do that and one of them thinks the loop at the top of the *f* is a clerk's and the other one thinks it is a machine and they have not spoken to each other since. **A hand is not a name and I am not going to give you a name out of a hand, and if I did, every bad letter in this city would have a name on it by now.**"

"The clinician."

"Dr Halvorsen. Fifty-four. Consultant in rehabilitation medicine, on the operator's roster, and she is the best clinician I have ever had an argument with about a bed." Rook put the pen down properly, which meant she was finished with the pen. "She has never been told a certificate exists. I have had two opportunities and I have not taken either, and I want to explain why, because I would like at least one thing today to be something other than a floor.

"If I tell her, then a doctor who has spent nineteen years signing for beds finds out that a signature of hers was not made by her, and she will want to know who did it, and she will ask me, and I will not be able to tell her, and she is a person who cannot carry *I don't know* — I have watched her not carry it about a missing swab in November and it took eleven days off the ward. **So a person who will not be harmed by not knowing is being kept not knowing, and that is a decision and I made it and I would make it again, and I am telling a member of the public about it because a decision like that should be expensive to make and mine is not.**"

---

"Say the thing," said Elias Venn.

"There is no thing to say. I have not done anything and nobody has. That is the thing." She uncapped the pen again, and did not write anything with it, and held it. "Mr Venn. I am going to say a sentence in this room that I have said in four rooms and I have never once had written down, and I am saying it because you have got a record of issue in a notebook and a fair copy of a claim form in the same notebook, and neither of those two things can get you what you came for, and that is the reason.

"**Every part of that certificate is true.** The claim was made. The claim was settled. The schedule number is a schedule number and it has been a schedule number since the nineteen-seventies. The service code is the code for the procedure that was billed. The registrar-counsel who signed it has never examined a patient in her life and neither have I and neither did the claims officer. The premises is registered and the company is registered and the company has a registration number and a set of books and a premises, and the company did nothing, because a company is not a thing that can do anything. **And if the treatment did not happen, then nobody in this building has done anything wrong.** That is not an excuse. I have been trying to say it in a way people could hear for nineteen years and it is a floor, and it is the floor this whole thing is standing on, and I would like you to understand what you have actually found, because it is not a scandal. It is a floor."

She put the pen down.

"And I will say one more thing, and then I am going to ask you for something, and then this is over."

"Go on."

"A release is addressed." She said it carefully, the way people say a thing they have had to think about for a long time. "A release commits a change and closes a working, and it is addressed to whoever is holding the other end of it. Somebody with standing receives it on behalf of a body that can carry what comes back. **In a room above a shop in Kettle Rows there is nobody to receive it, and that is why what happens in that room is a crime. In this building there is a tray and a standing and a drawer, and that is why the same act in this building is a service.** Same act. Different room. And nobody has ever written that sentence down anywhere, because a sentence like that ends a career and it does not end a company."

She let that go too, and then she picked the pen up and put the cap back on it.

"I am not going to tell you how a schedule came to say a bed in a ward that was shut," she said. "I have looked for nine weeks. I have had four claims officers on it and two of them have left the department. I have not found it, and I am not going to stand in a room and guess in front of a member of the public, and if I have not found it then either it is not in this building or I am not the person who can see it, and I would honestly rather say that to you than have you spend six months believing I knew."

---

"Then what I would like from you is four reference numbers," said Selene Rook, "and I want to be clear that I am asking and not taking, and that I would use them properly and that I would tell you what I did with them."

Elias Venn looked at the table.

"Twenty-first of November," he said. "There was a public enquiries counter in a claims administration office on this side of the ring road, and there were four people in front of me, and I have not got their names and I have not got their reference numbers, and I have not got a great deal else."

"No," said Rook.

"No."

"Then I have asked for something you cannot give me, and I would like to know why not, because you have spent two months writing down everything anybody says to you and I have read four pages of it in a car park, according to your own letter."

"Because I did not write them down," said Elias Venn.

The room was about eleven degrees warmer than the corridor outside it.

"Because I did not write them down," he said, "and I want you to know that I have thought about that for about four seconds and I am not going to pretend that I did it for a good reason. I wrote down what each of them said and I did not write one name and I did not write one number, because I am a member of the public and if I have a name and a number in a notebook then I am a man with a file, and a man with a file is a door somebody knocks on. **I have been a door somebody knocks on since the seventeenth of September and I have watched what it does to the people behind it, and I decided in a car park on the twenty-first of November that I would not do it to a woman at a counter who had told me the truth about a man of eighty-two. And I was right, and you have just asked me for the one thing I have got to give, and I have not got it, and that is the correct price of the thing I did.**"

Selene Rook sat back in her chair for the first time in eleven minutes.

"Yes," she said. "That is the correct price, and I have been in this job nineteen years and about four people have ever given me a sentence like that one, and I would like you to write it down somewhere, and I am not going to ask you where."

---

At twenty-five to one he went down to the ground floor in the lift, and there was a counter down there with a woman behind it of about twenty-six, and there was a rack of leaflets by the door, and the leaflets said **AEGIS MUTUAL — WHAT WE DO AND WHAT WE DO NOT COVER**, and nobody was in the queue.

"Are you the one who was in the room on four?"

"Yes."

"There is a card," said the woman at the counter, "for when you come out, so that you can take the leaflets and so that I can write on the card that you have been, and it is a courtesy and it is not a form and it is not anything."

She wrote on a card in a box: **in — 10.02 — four — rook**. She turned it round so he could read it, which nobody does.

"Have you got a claim reference, sir? If you are a party, I can give you the reference and there is a procedure if you want to raise something."

"No," said Elias Venn. "I am not a party."

"No, sir."

"Thank you."

"You are welcome," said the woman at the counter, and put the card in a tray with about forty other cards in it, and the tray was on the wrong side of the wall from the door, and he went out into Verge Row at ten to one with no certificate and no reference number and two documents in a plastic wallet in his coat that he had been given by the person who held the money, and the rain had stopped, and the bus came in four minutes and he got on it and sat on the top deck because it was empty.
