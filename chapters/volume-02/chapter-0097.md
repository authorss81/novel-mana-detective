# Chapter 0097 — She Went Up for the Truth

Yolanda Speke was fifty-one and had been a district nurse for twelve years and she had one bag and an evening list, and she came to the door of a room behind a first-floor flat on Sunday the twentieth of January at ten past ten in the morning and shut the door behind her and put her hand on it. On the table were a wireless telephone, a wall calendar from a veterinary practice, a bowl with two grapes in it, and a page of blue paper with nine names on it in a hand that leaned forward and a column for the time.

"You have got coffee," she said. "Sit down. I am going to say no, and then I am going to make you hear the whole of it, and then I am going to say yes, and I would like the yes understood."

"You have not been in this room before and I have not been in this building," said Elias Venn.

"You have not, and I have not, and I know who you are because a woman who works in a building with a board on it has told me about you, and she is right about about two things out of three, which is better than most people say about their family." She sat down. "No."

"That is very quick."

"It is not quick, it is four days old, and I said no to somebody else on Wednesday and I have not said no since." She turned the blue paper round and round. "You have come to me because you want a person who can go into a room on Tuesday night and come out and swear to what was in it. And I have thought about it since Wednesday night and here is my answer in three parts and the first part is no and the second part is that the no is the whole of it, and the third part is that I am going to say yes, and I would like it noticed that the third part arrived on its own about four minutes ago and I did not call it."

---

"The rule I have is that I do not go into a building on my own after dark," she said. "That is not a rule anybody wrote down. It is eleven years of a rule. I have been bitten, I have been let in by a man with a television on at full volume, I have come out of a flat on Wexford Row in 2021 with a key that did not work and no way of getting back in, and none of that is anybody's fault and all of it happened because I was on my own in a stair at nine at night with a bag of dressings."

"You would not be on your own."

"You would be," said Yolanda Speke. "That is the entire problem and I have thought about it for two days and here it is, and I am a nurse and I am not stupid. If I go up that stair on Tuesday and there is a police officer at the top of it, then I am not a district nurse, I am a person who has been put on that stair, and I go up because somebody with a badge asked me to. And the moment that is true, everything I see in that room is something I saw on behalf of somebody, and when a man in a wig stands up in about four years and says *you are a professional witness, what were you doing there* — and that is the phrase, I have read about it, everybody in the health service knows that phrase — then I have to say *I was doing what I was asked*, and that is the end of me."

"You would be on your own on a job."

"I would be on my own on a job," she said, "and a job has got a patient in it, and the patient does not know about you, and the job is the only reason I am allowed in that building at all, and if there is no job then there is no nurse in that building and there is only a woman with a bag and a man with a notebook."

He did not answer that for about ten seconds, which she counted.

"Go on," he said.

"There is a woman on the ground floor of that building on my list at a quarter past seven on Tuesday. I have been going to her for four years. She is seventy-nine and she does not get out and she has a hip and a man who does not come." She put her finger on the page. "I have to tell her I am not coming. That is the lie and there is no way round it and I have been sitting in this room since Wednesday working out whether there is a way round it and there is not, because I cannot send her a letter and I cannot ring a neighbour and I cannot cancel a visit the week before, and I am going to have to stand in a front room at a quarter past seven on Tuesday and tell a woman of seventy-nine that a district nurse with a bad back is not coming, and there is no back, I have got a knee, and she will know there is no back."

"What will she say."

"She will say *oh*." Yolanda Speke looked at the ceiling. "She will say *oh*, and she will say don't bother about it, and she will say it in the way she says it which is as if she is being fair to me, and then she will be on her own until Wednesday, and on Wednesday I will go and I will not tell her what I did on Tuesday and I will lie to her about that as well, and I am telling you both of those things now so that there is a person in this city who knows what I have done before I do it, and I have never in twelve years needed anybody to know anything and I have done it in about four seconds so do not make a speech."

---

She wrote the whole of what she had agreed to say on the back of a pad in her own hand, read it twice, struck out nine words, and handed it over.

> *I am a district nurse. I have an evening list. I go to that building every week for a patient on the ground floor and I go in at the front door and the stair is inside the door. On the twenty-second of January I will go up the stair at about ten to eight because the door at the top will be open and I will have a bag and there will be a woman on my list in the building. I will go in. There will be people in the room. I will not stay. I will not touch anybody. I will not speak to anybody except to say I am sorry and that I will send somebody in the morning. I will be in the room about four minutes. I can say how many people were sitting down. I cannot say what any of them were doing and I am not going to be asked to.*

"That is a draft, and it is in the future tense, and it is going to stay in the future tense until I have been upstairs, and if I bring it back in the past tense then I have been upstairs and I have not lied to you about the tense, and that is the difference between a nurse and a witness and it is the only thing I have got to give them."

"Nine words," she said. "**I cannot say what any of them were doing.** They cannot have those, because if they have those then I have been asked what was happening in a room, and I did not know what was happening in a room, and I have not the least idea now, and I would like that written down before somebody in a wig asks me."

"You have understood it exactly."

"I have understood that you have brought me a piece of paper and asked me to take a thing out of it," said Yolanda Speke. "That is what everybody does. Every single person who has ever asked me for anything in twelve years has wanted something out of me, and the ones who were honest about it were the ones who got it."

---

Ivor Skellern was behind the counter of the shop at nine Calder Place at about ten past three on Sunday afternoon with two customers in the shop, a woman with a basket and a man buying keys, and he put a sheet of A4 on the counter face up and put his hand flat on it and did not say a word about it.

"There is no reason you should believe me," he said, to the woman with the basket, who was looking at it. "There is a reason he should. I drew it in June 2026. I have drawn it about four times since. It is a plan of a room I let. It goes back on the counter in ten minutes and it does not leave this shop and it is not going in an envelope, and I would like both of you to be able to say you saw a sheet of paper on a counter in a shop on a Sunday, because that is exactly what it is and exactly where it was, and if anybody in four years wants to know whether I had it, there are two people in this shop who saw it flat on this counter and one of them bought keys."

He put his hand on the sheet.

"There is one thing I am not going to let anybody do," he said. "I am not going to let anybody photograph it, and I am not going to let anybody keep it, and I am not going to say a single word about the corner."

The sheet was a plan of a room in ink. It had a door and a meter cupboard and a couch against one wall and a table and eleven chairs. Eleven of the chairs were numbered from one to eleven in a second pen on the reverse, and the numbers were in a line down the left-hand side, and beside each number there was a figure, and the figures were not eleven identical figures, and one of them was a great deal larger than the others.

Seven of the eleven places were drawn once. Four of them were drawn twice, faintly, as though a man had gone over the plan with the same pen and less pressure. And one of the eleven places was drawn twice with a gap between the two lines, the way a man draws a thing and then cannot finish drawing it.

Elias Venn stood in a shop on a parade with his hands behind his back and read it, and in the corner there was a date and under the date a code in a hand that leaned right and pressed hard, and he did not say one word about the corner, and nobody in that shop knew there was anything in the corner to say a word about, and there was nobody in that shop who could have told him whose hand it was, because there was nobody in that shop but a man who sells keys and a woman with a basket and a man buying keys.

> **MARGIN — One of the eleven places on this plan was begun and not finished. It is not a turn. It is the shape a working takes when somebody stops halfway and starts again, and there is one place on this plan where the person doing it did not start again. I can see the room. I cannot tell you what the act is and I cannot tell you who is to do it, and there is nobody standing in this shop who could do it.**

The man in the shop with the keys paid for his keys and left. The woman with the basket read the back of the sheet upside down and said it was a nice bit of drawing and went out into the rain. Ivor Skellern took the sheet off the counter, folded it in four, and put it in his inside pocket.

"You have said nothing," he said.

"I have said nothing."

"You have read it in about nine seconds and you have said nothing, and in four years nobody has read anything of mine in nine seconds." He put the counter straight. "I am going to ask you one question and I would like an honest answer and I do not care what it is. Are you going to be here on Tuesday."

"I am going to be in this building at half past four on Tuesday and I am going to be in that room, and I have not told anybody that except you, and it is because of a sheet of paper that was flat on your counter in daylight for ten minutes this afternoon, and I have not said so to a living soul."

"That is the second person in three days who has told me where they are going to be on a Tuesday," said Ivor Skellern, "and I have said it to nobody, so you may work out for yourself how many people in this city are standing about a Tuesday, and I would like the other one to be somebody in an office with a telephone."

---

Nadia Kest was on the wall telephone in the shop at about a quarter past five and he told her about the plan and she was quiet for about four seconds, which was the longest she had been quiet in four months.

"Right," she said. "Then I am going to tell you the whole of what that is, and then I am going to tell you the whole of what it is not, and then you are going to make up your mind and I am not going to have another conversation about it until Monday. What it is: there is a person who has drawn a room eleven times and a room with a place in it that was begun and not finished, and a man who can see that, and a man who is a member of the public with a finding against him. What it is not: it is a suspicion formed out of a piece of paper, and I have had that refused in front of me in this building four days ago in about forty seconds by a woman with a cardigan on. I have not got a warrant and I am not going to have one on Monday, and on Monday I am going to have an officer's own grounds sworn, and the officer is going to be a district nurse with a bag and a list, and she is going to say *I went in and there were people in the room* and I am going to have to stand in a room and be told by a magistrate that that is enough, and I think it probably is, and I have not got a single piece of paper from you to help me do it."

"I am not going to say it was a plan."

"I know you are not going to say it was a plan. That is the difference between the two of us and it has been the difference for four months, and it is the reason I will let you into a room on Tuesday and it is the reason a man in a wig would take me apart in about nine minutes if I ever let you into one before." She let that sit. "**A man who has read a plan is not an officer who has seen a room. And on Monday the thing that gets me into that room is a woman who has seen a room, and she has seen it for four minutes on her own job, and she is going to be frightened, and the last thing on earth that she needs is a man in a good coat telling her in a corridor that she has read something that means he has been there too.**"

"Then I will not be on her list."

"She will put you on her list," said Nadia Kest, "because I am going to have to, because there is a woman of seventy-nine on the ground floor of that building and I am not sending a police officer to a front room on a Tuesday evening. You are going to be on that list between about a quarter past and half past seven, in a car, going nowhere in particular, and you are going to be the second person in that building after a district nurse, and I want you to think about what that looks like from the front room of a flat where a woman of seventy-nine lives."

"I have thought about it."

"I know you have. That is why I am letting you do it."

---

She came down the stair at about twenty past six on Sunday evening with her bag over her shoulder and put it down in the hall of the building where she lived and stood there with her hand on it, and Elias Venn had said he would walk her to the bus and had not been able to talk her out of it.

"Right," said Yolanda Speke. "I have lied to a woman on the ground floor of a parade building and I would like somebody to be told that I have done it, and I have told you, and you are the wrong person entirely and there is nobody else, and I would like that on the record as well."

"You have not lied. You have not been there."

"I have told a woman of seventy-nine that a nurse with a bad back is not coming to her flat on Tuesday evening, and there is no back, and I have got a knee, and I have been letting her think I have got a back since about 2019 because it was easier than telling her about the knee." She picked the bag up again. "That is a lie and it is a small one and it is eleven months old and I have never once regretted it, and I have never once told anybody about it, and I have just told a man in a good coat who is not a nurse and not a doctor and not anybody, at twenty past six on a Sunday, in about ten seconds, at a door."

She went out into the rain.

"And I am doing it anyway," she said, from the step, without turning round. "The rest of it is the police's business and I have found that sentence in a book about a man in a wig and I have been saying it to myself since Wednesday and it is not true, and I am saying it out loud so that you can hear that I know it is not true."
