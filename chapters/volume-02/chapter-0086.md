# Chapter 0086 — The Address of the Working

The shop under the two rooms is a locker's shop that shut in the second week of October, and the glass is papered over from the inside with a page of newsprint about a bridge, and there is a board in the window with a lease on it behind the glass, and the lease is dated to the last day of March and has an agent's telephone number on it and the name of an estate office on the top line, and there is a second sheet under the lease that says the premises are let weekly and are to be left as found.

Elias Venn walked past it at ten past two in the afternoon on Wednesday the ninth of January and there was a woman standing on the pavement outside it in a coat with a clinic bag in her hand, and she was not waiting for anybody, and when she saw him she held up one finger.

She held up one finger for about four seconds. Then she said, "You are going to be late, and I am going to start without you, and that is not a rebuke, that is the arrangement, and I want you to hear the arrangement and not a speech about it."

"You have not said a word to me about a date since November."

"I have not said a word to you about a date since November because I have a date in a diary and I have not said it out loud in front of you, and that is a rule I made for myself and you did not make it and you have never once broken it, and I have noticed that." Sana Okafor had her registration number on the outside of her coat, in a bag, because it was January and there was a rule. "Two rooms up there. A man, fifty-one. His daughter, thirty-one. And a girl of nine who is his granddaughter and is not the daughter of anybody in that building and turns up on a Wednesday and a Saturday and is fed by whoever is cooking."

"And you are going up there to tell them."

"Yes."

"Alone."

"Yes, and in the room, and with the door shut, and it is going to take about an hour, and there are three of them and one of them is nine, and the nine-year-old is going to be told something about herself that she has not been told for seven weeks, and I have practised it, which is a ridiculous thing to say about a thing like that, and I have practised it."

---

He agreed in nine seconds. He did not ask a single question in those nine seconds, which is a thing he has been getting better at since a corridor on Vine Street in December, and which cost him a woman of sixty-one four days of a drug in a dispensary back room in December, and which was the only decent thing he did all that week.

"Three hours," said Sana Okafor. "You will be bored."

"I have a bench."

"You have a landing." She looked up at the first floor. "One door in from the yard stair, and the second one along is the one they use, and there is a light on the landing that has not worked since about the summer and the bulb is not in it, and there is a window at the end that looks at a wall, and I have sat on that landing in November and it is a perfectly good landing and you will not be able to see a thing from it, which is the point of it."

Then she went up and the yard door was not locked and he let himself in and went up a flight and a half and sat down on the second step from the top with his back against the wall.

---

A landing is a bad place to sit for three hours and a good place to sit for three hours if what you have to do is not do anything, and he had done it in November on the ground floor of a building four streets away in a corridor at the end of a session list, and he had been useful in that corridor, and it had ended with a woman of sixty-one four nights without a drug.

He took his notebook out. He wrote the date at the top of a page, and the time, and then he shut it and put it back in his coat and left it there for three hours, which is the longest he has gone in four months without writing anything down, and which he would think about for a long time afterwards and could not explain.

What he had instead was the landing. It had a window at the end of it that looked at a wall about four feet away, and the wall was the side of a building with a drainpipe down it and a bracket on it about eight feet up with nothing in the bracket, and the light that did not work, and a mat by the third door that said nothing at all, and a smell of a gas fire that had been off since about eight o'clock in the morning.

At about half past two he heard a chair go back inside. At about twenty to three he heard a kettle go on and come off. At about three he heard a woman laugh, once, and stop, which is the length of a laugh that a person cuts short on purpose.

At about ten past three a child came out onto the landing with a book and stood at the window and looked at the wall, and did not look at him, and stayed about four minutes, and went back in. The book was about a pony.

At about four o'clock somebody knocked on a door two doors along from the one they were using, and a young man of about twenty-two came out on the landing in a dressing gown and a pair of socks and shut the door behind him and said, "Is that the agent?"

"No."

"You are on the step."

"I am on the second step."

"Right." The young man leaned on the wall opposite, which put him about two feet from Elias Venn and about four feet from the light that did not work. "Sorry. I have been waiting for the agent since about November and I keep thinking a knock is him, and it has been a woman with a leaflet and a man about a leak."

"About the heating."

"About the heating and the water." He said it as though Elias Venn had asked. "The meters are on the shop's supply, all three of them, and the shop shut in the second week of October, and the supply is still on the shop's name, and the shop is empty, and there is a man from the estate office who has been four times and each time has said the supply is not his department. And I have worked it out, I have worked it out since November — the meters want reading and if the meters are not read the supply gets cut for the whole building, and it is not going to get cut because somebody will be cold enough to go to the agent, and it will get cut in about February, and then four of us are cold until somebody comes."

"How many of you."

"Four flats and the shop and the yard, and there is an old woman at the top who is eighty-nine and has not been out since the summer before last, and she is the only reason anybody cares." He pulled his dressing gown closed. "I have written to them. I have written four times. My name is on the letter and I have paid for the stamp, and that is the entire amount of power I have in this."

"That is the entire amount of power anybody has in this."

"Yeah, well." He looked at the door they were using, and then at Elias Venn, and decided not to ask, which Elias Venn noticed. "Sorry, are you a friend of the family?"

"No."

"Right." He unhooked his dressing gown at the neck where he had hooked it on the latch. "It is the girl that comes on the Wednesdays. She is all right. She is a bit loud about the pony and that is nine."

He went in. The landing had a window at the end of it that looked at a wall about four feet away, and the wall was the side of a building with a drainpipe down it and a bracket on it about eight feet up with nothing in the bracket, and the light that did not work, and a mat by the third door that said nothing at all.

At about half past four somebody said a name through the door and somebody else answered, and one of the voices was the girl's and one of them was an older woman, and the girl's voice said something he could not hear and the older woman said something he could not hear either, and the girl came out again for a second time at about twenty past four and went to the window and stood at it and did not look at him and did not go back in for about a minute and a half, and Elias Venn did not say anything to her, and did not move, and did not look at her directly, and she went back in.

That was all he did. That was the entire content of three hours of his afternoon: he sat on the second step of a stair in Kettle Rows and did not say anything to anybody and did not write anything down and did not put his ear anywhere near a door, and it was the hardest work he has done since the seventeenth of September, and the reason it was hard is that he could have gone in.

He could have gone in at half past two. There was no lock and there was a man of fifty-one on the other side of that door who had paid ninety credits into an envelope in November and had stood in a room in Sallow Lane in a working that had been addressed to a child of nine, and there was a woman of thirty-one on the other side of that door who had kept her own name and lost four months of Sundays, and there was a girl of nine on the other side of that door who had been the address of a working on Monday the twenty-sixth of November and had not been told, and nobody in that room had asked for a police officer and there was no police officer in that room and there never would be, and if a man had opened that door and said *I am the one who was in the room in Sallow Lane and I have been trying to find out who ran it for nine weeks* then he would have got more in ninety seconds than he had got in four months.

He sat on the second step from the top until half past five.

---

They came out into the stairwell at about twenty past five and there were three of them and the man was in a cardigan and the woman of thirty-one was not crying and the girl of nine had the pony book under her arm.

Sana Okafor said, to the girl, "This is Elias. He is a friend of mine and he is not a doctor and he is not a policeman, and he has been sitting on that step for three hours because I asked him to, and he would like you to know that he did not come up here to ask anybody anything."

"He has not said a word to me in three hours," said Aoife Roche, nine, and went downstairs.

The man said thank you to Elias Venn and meant it, and did not say anything else, and went down. The woman of thirty-one looked at him for a moment and said, "You were the one with the sheet on the wall," and he said he had been, and she said, "I read it in about four months ago and I did not understand one word of it and my uncle used to read it out," and went down, and that was the whole of it.

---

Sana Okafor came back up four steps and stood above him on the landing with her bag in her hand and did not sit down.

"Well," she said.

"You do not owe me a report."

"I am aware of that and I have thought about it for seven weeks and I have not gone back and forth on it once." She put the bag down on the step above his. "I told them. I told all three of them. I told the girl what was done to her in a room she was in on the twenty-sixth of November and that nobody told her at the time and that she was not harmed and that she is nine and that it is not going to be explained to her better today than it was going to be explained to her in November. I told the woman what was taken, which is four months of her own Sundays, and that they are not coming back, and that I have told her that in about nine minutes because it is nine minutes' worth of a thing and it is not going to get smaller by being said slower. And I told the man that his own name has not come back and that he is not the only one and that the other one was seventy-eight and it is not the same thing but it is the closest thing anybody in this city can offer him at a table."

"You have not said a word about a fifth box and you have not said a word about a claim."

"I have not said a word about a fifth box, because they have not had a fifth box, because there is no fifth box, because their lives have never once come near a form." She picked the bag up again. "And I have not said a word about this case, and you will notice that I have described it to you three times without saying one word about it, and I did that on purpose, and I would like you to notice that I did it on purpose."

"I have noticed."

"What did you do for three hours."

"I sat on your stair and did not go in and did not write anything down."

"That is the whole of the correct answer," said Sana Okafor, "and I want to tell you what it cost, because you will otherwise think it was nothing. It cost me about nine minutes of a conversation I had planned to have with you afterwards, and it cost you the only thing you have ever actually wanted, which is a room with a person in it who is frightened and who will talk to you. You did not have that room, and you did not open the door, and I would have put my hand flat on the door myself and I would not have opened it, and the difference between us is that I am a doctor and you are a man with a notebook, and neither of those is a reason to open a door to a child."

She went down two steps and then stopped and said one more thing.

"There is no question I can answer now that would make that afternoon better," she said. "There is not one. And you are going to want one, because you are the sort of man who treats a refusal as a locked drawer, and I am telling you in advance that this one is not locked. There is nothing in it. It is not a door. It is a thing that happened, and it happened in a room with a child in it, and I have done it, and the two people upstairs have a right to have it not be made into a case by a man who cannot get in anywhere else."

---

At twenty past five Elias Venn came down the yard stair, and the door at the bottom was not locked and the light on the gate was not on, and he stood in the yard for a minute in the cold.

The girl of nine was on the step outside the shop, in the wind, with the pony book under her arm, and she was not waiting for anybody and she was not looking at the door. She had her thumb in the middle of the pages to keep her place, which is a thing children do and adults do not, and she was looking at the gutter, where there was about an inch of water in it from the rain that had come through at two and gone.

"You were on the step," she said.

"I was on the second step."

"My granny says the second step is the cold one." She turned a page and did not read it. "Are you the man that put the paper up?"

"I put a paper up in a waiting room in October and it is still up, and I did not mean it to be, and there are about four people in this city who have had a worse year because of it and you are one of them because your aunt read it out at your tea table and you are nine."

She thought about that for a bit, with her thumb in the middle of the pages.

"My uncle can't say his own name," she said, "and my aunt can't do the Sundays, and the doctor has just been in there for an hour, and you have been on the step for three hours." She closed the book and held it against her chest with both arms. "I have got a pony in this book called Dooly and he is not real and everybody in the story knows he is not real, and that is the whole point of him, and if he were real nobody would read the book." She looked at him for the first time. "Is the man who put the paper up allowed to be somebody who was on the step?"

"I am not allowed to be anything at all," said Elias Venn. "I am a member of the public."

"Right," said Aoife Roche, and went in, and the door on the ground floor shut behind her, and the gate lamp was still not on.
