# Chapter 0074 — Shelf Behind the Polythene

There were four kinds of paper behind the polythene on the board in the waiting room of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic at twenty past nine on Thursday the twentieth of December, and Elias Venn stood in front of it for the better part of an hour and looked at all four of them, which was a thing he had not done since he put the fourth one up himself on the morning of the twenty-seventh of November.

The ninety-odd copied pages were at the bottom of the fan. They went back forty-one months, one sheet to a month, and they were copies of the clinic's own maintenance book, and they were put up because a ward in a dispensary had failed in a way that put a woman in a chair, and Dr Okafor had wanted it written down in a place where the public could read it.

The four sheets in a doctor's ink were above them, with four drawing pins in the corners of the first one, and Dr Okafor's name under his, and they had been there since the eighth of October and had been photographed once by a sergeant who had asked for no form.

The registrar's transfer order was in its clear sleeve with a bent corner, and it had been on that board since the twenty-first of September, and it was nobody's business and had never been taken down.

And at the top of the fan, on its own, with air round it on all four sides, was his sheet, with the three lines Dr Okafor had written underneath the heading and her registration number under his, and four drawing pins that were his own and had come out of his own pocket.

He had not stood in front of that board and looked at it since the day he put it up. He had looked at it about forty times from the doorway.

"You have been reading my board for an hour and a half and you have not said anything to me," said Sana Okafor, behind him, "and I have been in the corridor for about forty minutes letting you do it, because I wanted to see whether you would notice, and you have, and you are going to tell me in the wrong order. Go on. What did you notice first."

"The fan at the bottom."

"Go on."

"Forty-one months of every ward in this building failing at a date." He said it flatly. "That is not ninety-odd pages, Mrs Okafor. That is a map. A map of every day this building was under-staffed, with the day on it, and every one of those days has a name against it somewhere in this building, and the names are not on the fan but they are in the book the fan is copied from, and the book is in this building, in a drawer, in a clinic, and any person who wants the fan only has to ask the clinic for the book, and you cannot refuse because the fan is on the wall and the fan is the copy."

"Yes," said Sana Okafor.

"And you are going to take it down."

"Yes."

"Can I help."

"No," she said. "That is the whole answer and I would like you to sit with it for a second before you decide what you think it means, because I have thought about it since Monday and I have not slept properly and I am going to do it at eleven o'clock this morning in front of two of my own staff and you are not going to be one of them and you are not going to be asked to hold anything."

---

She did it at eleven.

Doreen Tillery and Enid Marlow were in the waiting room because she told them to be in the waiting room and neither of them argued, and Enid Marlow held the folder the copies were in and did not want to hold it.

"Forty-one months," said Sana Okafor. "One of you two tell me what that is."

"It is a record," said Enid Marlow.

"It is a record of a book that is in this building and unseized and always has been," said Sana Okafor. "Which means that any day between the eighteenth of April and the eighteenth of April before it, a member of the public can come in off that street, read ninety-odd sheets, decide which month they are interested in, and then ask me for the book, and I have to either hand over a clinic record to a stranger or refuse, and a refusal in a waiting room is a story."

"Or somebody comes in with a form," said Doreen Tillery.

"Or somebody comes in with a form." She took the two drawing pins out and the fan came apart in her hands like something that had been folded by a person who was tired. "In October I put four sheets on that wall with my own name under his, and eleven weeks later a certificate was served on this address because this address is on a connected certified record, and a sequence desk in a building four streets away read one line off a sheet on that board and came here. I have had eleven weeks to think about that and I have arrived at a sentence and I am going to say it once and I would like nobody to say it again in this building, including him, for about a year.

"**Protection and a map are the same piece of paper. The only difference is who is holding it.**"

Nobody said anything.

"They do not go in the skip," she said. "They go in the box in my office, on the shelf, under the maintenance book, and the key to the office stays on my own belt, and that is the correct place for a map in this district and it is the correct place for a record as well and the only difference is who has a key."

Enid Marlow put the folder on the desk and said, "Do you want the pin holes filled."

"No," said Sana Okafor.

---

There were two pin holes in the board and a rectangle of a paler colour than the rest of it, about nine inches by eleven, with a soft edge round it where forty-one months of dust had built up on everything else and had not been allowed to build up on that.

The four drawing pins holding the sheet from the twenty-seventh of November were his own and were still in it, and the sheet was still at the top of the fan, and the fan was a fan of three kinds of paper.

"Twenty-five days," said Elias Venn.

"Twenty-five days on the order in the municipal building and a drawer with my registration number under it," said Sana Okafor. "And a woman on the first floor of a building in Corrie Street who has worked for six years for eleven credits and has a sheet of A4 under glass with a third box on it that is not in the manual, and there is exactly one address anybody in this district knows for that and it is hers. And on the fourteenth of January somebody writes a word on a form. I have had that in my head for five weeks and I have not been able to put it down anywhere else and I would like you to take it out of my head, because you are the only person I know who does not write things down, and it is unbearable to be sitting on it alone."

"I will take it out of your head," said Elias Venn, "and I will not write it down, and you should be aware that that is a thing I have said to two other people in four months and both of them now know something I could have held."

"Two of them are the reason I am doing this, so do not flatter me with them." She pulled the polythene flat and turned the corner of it over, which was a thing she did every morning and had been doing since before him. "And now the condition, and I have thought about how to word it and I am going to word it badly.

"**Before you say a thing about a document out loud in this building, you tell me, and I am entitled to stand in the doorway.**"

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it, and I have wanted to put three clauses on it and I have put them on because you would have accepted all three and I would have got nothing. The board is mine. It has three kinds of paper on it and one of them is yours and the other two are older than you are. You are not going to add a fifth thing to that board and you are not going to take the sheet down, and if you ever ask me for a wall again I shall say no in front of six people, and I have not said that before and I am saying it now, and I am not going to argue with you about it in a corridor." She let the polythene go. "And you will tell me. That is all of it. Tell me before you say it, and let me stand in the doorway."

"That is a hard condition."

"It is a condition about eleven seconds," said Sana Okafor, "and I have had it in my head since Monday, and you are going to accept it or I am going to stop having this conversation, and I am not going to be the woman who is pleasant to you in a corridor and difficult in a meeting room, so do it now, in here, while there is nobody in."

"I accept it on your terms," said Elias Venn. "I would not have known to ask for them."

"Nobody ever asks for them. That is what I have found out in nineteen years of this building and it is the single most useful thing anybody has ever taught me and it was taught to me by a man who is not a doctor." She went. "And it is half past nine, and the fan is down, and I have got a clinic."

---

The back room of the Kettle Rows Tool Registry at number eleven on Corrie Street is eleven feet by nine, has a window that looks at a wall, a bench with a vice on it and a calendar from 2027 on the wall, and a smell of oil that has been there for two generations. Dr Okafor had borrowed it for the afternoon by telephone at half past twelve and the keeper of the register had lent it because she asked him in person, which is how anything gets lent in this district.

There were three people in it and one of them was not eligible to be there.

The keeper of the register had come in with the key and put it in the door and stayed for about forty seconds and had asked nothing, and when Sana Okafor thanked him he said that the room cost nothing because nobody had ever wanted it, and that in about nine years somebody would want it, and that on that day he would want to know what for.

"He said that about a wall and about a vice," said Elias Venn. "He is probably right about this as well."

"Sit down and say nothing for a while," said Sana Okafor. "You are the fourth person and you are the only one who is not on my staff and you have got no standing in this room, and if anything I say in here is put in a file later then I would rather it was a conversation that three people who work here had."

"I have not got a notebook."

"I know you have not got a notebook. You have got four hundred and thirty pages of your own handwriting in your coat that you have told me about once, and I have never asked to see it, and I am not going to." She put two pieces of card on the bench. "On the left is one of ours. On the right is the claim form. You are not going to see the right one. Enid is going to read it out, from a copy that has nothing on it, and you are going to hear it, and then you are going to leave the room for four minutes, because you are the only person in this room who has both of them in his head and I would like the three of us to be sure."

He left the room for four minutes and stood in a passage between two shops in the rain.

When he came back the three of them had not moved and Enid Marlow had a sheet of the clinic's own writing paper in front of her and five words written on it and no more.

"Say it," said Sana Okafor.

"Five questions, in the same order," said Enid Marlow. "**Where. What. When. How much. Who told.** Ours asks them in that order. Theirs asks them in that order. Ours is A5 and theirs is A4 and the type is not the same and they were not printed by the same people and I have been at this building nineteen years and I am telling you they are not the same paper and I am telling you that they are the same question.

"Our first box is where. In eleven years I have never once completed it. Not once. There are eleven names on that sheet and the address column is full of other people's houses and the first box on our form is a box I have never been able to fill in.

"Their first box is where, and on a claim it is a ward and a bed and a date, and that is a where, and it is a complete one, and it took somebody four hours and a rubber stamp in October.

"Our second box is what, and it is a code off a public three-credit sheet, and so is theirs. Our third is when. So is theirs. Our fourth is how much, and it is a fee, and on about four hundred sessions in six years it is nil or it is eleven credits, and on theirs it was sixty, and the fifty-one credits went somewhere that neither of these women has ever seen.

"And the fifth." Enid Marlow put her finger on the bottom of the sheet. "Ours is **who told the patient**. It is a name, it is a doctor's name, it is always filled in, and it is the only box on either piece of paper that anybody in either building ever completes. Theirs has no fifth box at all. There is nowhere on a claim form to say who told the patient anything, and there does not have to be, because their first box is full."

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds, which is not very long.

"That is the whole of it," said Sana Okafor. "And it is not a scandal and there is nobody in it who has broken a rule, and I want that written on the page that belongs to this clinic and not on his. **The market did not build a channel into this district. The channel is our own form with a different head on the top of it, and the first box on it has been empty for eleven years, and there is a queue of people in this city who would be very glad to fill it in, and every one of them would be doing their job.**"

"That is a sentence," said Enid Marlow.

"That is a sentence I have not said before and I am not saying again. Write the five questions and the two papers and nothing else."

"It is the fifth box that I cannot get past," said Doreen Tillery, from the window. "You have written down that their form has no fifth box, and you have written down that ours is always filled in, and you have both of you written those two things side by side on one page as though they are two facts about paper, and they are not. **Everything on that sheet that keeps one of those women alive is also the only reason there is no address for her.**"

She turned round.

"If I gave one of them a proper address in her own name tomorrow she would come off the sheet. And I would not know where her medicine went. And neither would the woman who carries it, and she would carry it to that house for about nine weeks and then she would be doing what everybody in this district does, which is take a plate to a gate."

"That is the sentence of this month," said Sana Okafor, "and I am not having it in a room with four people in it, and we are all going to write the five questions and go home."

---

He wrote the five questions in his own book on Thursday the twentieth of December, with no form number and no address and no name on it, and then he shut the book, and the wall in Marshall Street had three kinds of paper on it and a rectangle on the board that was a paler colour than the rest of it and two pin holes in it and four drawing pins that were his own.

He had a condition now, and a condition of that kind is a thing that is worth exactly nothing until the minute comes when not keeping it costs more than keeping it, and he knew the date of that minute, because she had told him the room and the time in November, and it was a Friday, and it was tomorrow.
