# Chapter 0052 — Three Credits

The reference library on Corrie Street was open on a Sunday because reference libraries are open when the building they belong to is not, and it had eight people in it and two of them were asleep and one of them was eating a sandwich out of a paper bag with the kind of concentration that meant it was somebody else's sandwich.

Elias Venn had come in at ten with a notebook and a pencil and no bag, and he went straight to the shelf, because he had spent the whole of Saturday evening working out that the question he had to ask on Monday was not *is this legal* but *what is the price of it*, and a price is on a shelf somewhere, and in a city where a licence class is a document and a tariff is a document, a tariff is a book on a shelf with the date of the printing on the front and nobody watching it.

It was where the card in his wallet said it would be, which was nowhere, because the card said the forms counter at Concord Hall, and what was on the shelf in the reference library was the **Schedule of Registered Procedures and Fees**, Civic Concord, Clinical Practice branch, annual, the printing for 1929 in a grey board, three credits, with a shelfmark in the old hand on the spine and a list of superseded editions in a card pocket at the back where the books had all been reclassified and nobody had thrown the card pockets out.

He took it down and read it standing up, and then he read it sitting down, and then he read it a third time with the whole of page eleven open on the table, and by half past eleven he had a thing in his notebook that he could not put down and did not want to.

**Schedule D — memory and identity work.** Fourteen procedures. Number 1, *record review*, one hour, eighteen credits, no supervision required. Number 2, *grounding and anchor*, two hours, **sixty credits**, registered practitioner, Module 4 certificate current. Number 3, *account of a sequence with the person who gives it*, one hour, twenty-two credits. Numbers 4 to 8 were variations on arrangements, bookings and confirmations, and they were priced between fifteen and thirty-five credits and none of them was restricted. Number 9, *identity recollection support*, one hour, twenty-six credits. Number 10, *recovery of a name or date to a person who has lost it*, one hour, **twenty-six credits**, and there was a note beside it, and the note said: *where the person has previously attended for this procedure and the name or date has been lost again, the fee is not to be charged a second time within fourteen days.*

Number 11 was *preparation for a hearing or an assessment*, two hours, seventy credits, and there was a line under it that made Elias Venn put his pencil down. Number 12 was *removal of a persistent association*, one hour, **ninety credits**, restricted, and the restriction was set out in full: a registered practitioner holding a current supervisor's countersignature, a written record of what was asked for in the words of the person asking, and a **minimum interval of fourteen days between a preparation and a removal in the same matter**. And under number 12, in the tariff notes, the first sentence was this.

> *4. No claim may be made under this schedule in respect of a procedure at number 2 to 14. A charge for a procedure to which this note applies is not a claim, is not to be settled by a third party, and may not be receipted by a person other than the practitioner performing it.*

He read it four times and then read number 10 again, and number 10 was the one that undid him.

Number 10 was *recovery of a name or date to a person who has lost it*. It cost twenty-six credits and it was not restricted and it needed no supervisor and no countersignature and no fourteen-day interval. It was the cheapest useful thing in the whole of Schedule D, and it was the thing that a woman on the top floor of a building in Kettle Rows had been getting four times in three weeks for eleven credits, and the difference between the two numbers was fifteen credits, and fifteen credits was the whole of the offence.

Everything else on the page was a method that was printed in a book he had already bought and read end to end in a fortnight in October, in a book with eleven modules, one of which had a page eleven with two boxes on it, one of which said *a supplied order is not an account and may not be recorded as one*. A woman in a first-floor room in Corrie Street was doing a published procedure, at a published price minus fifteen credits, in a room that was not a clinic, on people who could not afford the published price, and had been doing it for six years.

Elias Venn sat at a table in a reference library on a Sunday morning and found that he could have done it himself. He could have done number 2 on himself, lawfully, for sixty credits, and he could have done number 10 on a man in his own shop for twenty-six credits, and there was a woman on the top floor of his own building who had been paying eleven.

He wrote it out.

> *The procedure is legal. The method is published and free. The practice is an offence in two particulars and no others: it is not registered, and it is not charged the registered fee.*
> *That is all. There is nothing else in it. There is no concealment, no false account, no name taken and no door forced. The only concealment anybody is doing is the concealment that happens when a legal requirement is not complied with because complying with it would cost money.*
> *Nobody prosecutes this. Nobody can prosecute it in a way that does not look like a woman being fined for being kind. And nobody is frightened by it, and that is the thing I keep arriving at and cannot get past: this is not frightening because it is a market that works.*

He got to the bottom of page eleven and stopped, because there was a fifth tariff note and it was not about claims.

> *8. Where a procedure at number 2 to 14 is performed in premises that are not those of a registered practice, the practitioner shall record the premises, the date and the time, and shall state whether the person travelled to the premises or the premises were brought to the person. The commonest stated reason is that the person cannot travel.*

Elias Venn put his hand over his mouth for about four seconds.

It was Module 4. It was page eleven. It was the second box, in a different book, in a different office, in a book that cost three credits and had a grey board and was on a shelf in a room with eight people in it. **The commonest stated reason is that the person cannot travel.** Eight words, and a man had been taught them twice, in two buildings, by two publishers, and the sentence was about a room, and a room is exactly what an unregistered practitioner does not have and exactly what a person who cannot travel needs not to.

Two of the four people he had been about to go and see that afternoon were people who could not afford to travel to a registered practice. One of them was a man on the top floor of his own building who had been told by two doctors that it was not a thing anybody can do.

---

The woman at the desk had been watching him read for an hour and a half and she came over at ten past one, which was either the end of her tolerance or the end of her shift, and she was about sixty and she had a badge on a lanyard and a card tray on the desk with four pencils in it, all sharpened.

"You cannot take that out."

"I was not going to."

"Somebody always says that," said the woman at the desk, "and then they ask me what the shelf number is so they can come back at eight in the morning and photograph it, and then there is a queue on a Monday." She said it without any weight at all. "It's a reference book. It has a printing on the front and the printing is this year. The older ones are in the store and you can ask for those in the store and they will want to see what for, which is reasonable, because if you wanted the 1927 one it is because a patient is on it and a patient is not on a tariff."

"The reason I'm asking is not a patient."

"Then it is a person, and the answer is the same, and I am not being unkind, I am being the person who has this conversation four times a week." She put her hand flat on the grey board. "Everything in that book is in it because somebody published it, on purpose, for anybody to buy. That's the whole design. The Clinical Practice branch thinks it's terrible that people can just buy a schedule and know what a thing costs. That's the point of it. If you have to ask a woman at a counter what a grounding session costs then the woman at the counter decides whether you can afford it."

Elias Venn looked at her for slightly too long.

"Do you have a relative who does memory work?"

"I have a sister-in-law who had a grounding and anchor after her mother's funeral and she went to a proper place on the Verge Row side and it cost her fifty-eight credits because she went in at short notice," said the woman at the desk. "It was worth it. She could tell you why and I could not. And she did it registered, and the woman who did it was on a list, and if the sister-in-law had started feeling strange about her mother's name in six months' time there would have been somebody to ring." She straightened the card tray. "That's the bit nobody puts on the front of the book. Sixty credits isn't for the memory. It's so that there is somebody to ring."

"Yes," said Elias Venn.

"So you'll be back for something or you won't," said the woman at the desk, "and if it's a schedule it's three credits at the rack and you can buy that one anywhere, and I'll tell you where because I'm not going to let anybody queue on a Monday for a grey book."

---

He went back to the table and wrote a note to himself about number 10, and then he read tariff note 4 a fifth time, and then he did the thing he had come to do on Saturday evening and had not admitted to himself until the reference library.

He wanted to know when note 4 went in.

---

The form for an earlier printing was a slip of buff paper the size of a bookplate and it had four boxes on it and one of them said **REASON** in capitals, and Elias Venn stood at the desk with it and put his thumb on the word and said, out loud, the sentence he had practised on the bus.

"I want to know whether a tariff note was added in a revision, and if so in which year, and whether the change is in front of a committee minute. It is not about a patient. I am asking because the note says no claim may be made for a list of procedures, and I want to know when that was true and what happened to claims for those procedures before it was."

The woman at the desk read the form upside down and then turned it round.

"That is a real answer," she said. "That is the first real answer anybody has given me about a tariff since July. Sit down. Store requests are done by the second one and the second one is Mr Oyelaran, and Mr Oyelaran does not like being asked for things on a Monday afternoon, so we have got twenty minutes before he finishes his sandwich, and I am going to write *tariff revision* on this and not write anything else, because if I write what you actually want I will have to say *no*, and I do not want to say no to the only person who has asked me a question about a schedule in nine years."

Mr Oyelaran was sixty-eight and had been in the store for thirty-one years and he did not like being asked for things on a Monday afternoon, and at ten past two he went down the stairs at a speed that suggested he did not.

He came back at two thirty-five with a grey book in a plastic sleeve with a sticky label on the front, and he put it on the table with the sleeve off and did not open it, and he stood there.

"Seventeen twenty-seven printing. Third schedule, second revision, forty-one pages. Two hundred and nineteen leaves in this one and the two thousand and nineteen printing is the same length, so no note has been added or taken out since, and no page has been reprinted, which is more than I can say for most of them." He turned the book round so the tariff notes were facing up. "Note 4 is not in it."

Elias Venn looked at the four notes on the page.

"How do you know it is not in it, rather than not numbered 4?"

"Because I have been doing this for thirty-one years," said Mr Oyelaran, "and because somebody has done the pencil. If you take a book out of a store sleeve you are not to write in it, and I am telling you that in advance so that I can be annoyed later. Read it."

There was a date in soft pencil at the foot of the notes and a tick beside it, and the tick was a shopkeeper's tick, the kind a man puts against a line in an order book.

*Printed 1925. Revised 1927. Revised 1929.*

The 1929 revision was in ink, and it was not a printer's ink.

"This is not a printer's mark," Elias Venn said. "A printer marks a revision before he breaks the page."

"No."

"Then somebody filled this in after the book came off the press."

"Somebody filled it in after the book came off the press, and it will be the printer's, or the branch's, or somebody's at a meeting who was asked to be accurate and was not." Mr Oyelaran put the sleeve back on over the cover but did not put the book in it. "The revision itself is genuine. I can tell you that because the 1927 and the 1929 are the same forty-one pages and the 1929 has four notes and the 1927 has three, and I have been handling the same shelf since before your aunt was at school, and three notes went to four between two editions and it is not because somebody wrote one out by hand at a meeting."

"So note 4 is two years old."

"Note 4 is two years old as a *note*, and that is not the same as a thing that was not true before it was printed, and I am not going to be the man who says it was not true before it was printed, because I have not got that document and neither have you." He put the book back in the sleeve. "If you want to know what the 1927 printing says about claims, it says nothing at all. There is no note and there is no schedule cross-reference and Schedule D in 1927 is nine pages and stops at procedure 9, and procedure 12 is not in it, and *removal of a persistent association* is not in the 1927 book at all, and it is in this one, and I do not know who put it there and neither does the printer, because the printer is a man in a shed in the outer wards and he does what he is told."

Elias Venn wrote it down exactly, in the four lines he had allowed himself on the back of the buff slip, and then wrote a fifth line because the fifth one mattered more than the other four.

*1927: no note 4. Schedule D is nine pages and stops at procedure 9. Procedure 12 is not in the book at all. Revision is genuine; the date on it is in ink and not a printer's mark. Somebody at a meeting was asked to be accurate and was not.*

He gave the slip back and thanked her and she said, without any edge at all, "Three credits is nothing for that, and I have to tell you that the person who can tell you the rest of it is a committee and a committee is nine people in a room in March, and none of them is a shop."

"I am not going after a committee."

"You are not going after a committee on a Sunday," said the woman at the desk. "Come back on a Tuesday and be boring at somebody."

---

The rack was by a radiator on the ground floor of Concord Hall, where it had been in October when he had stood in front of it for forty minutes on the seventh of October reading a manual that admitted in print that it could not tell a perceived order from an expected one.

It was the same rack. It was the same man behind the table, or a man of about the same age behind the table, and there was a new wire basket on the end of it and the basket had a price card on it in ballpoint.

**SELECTED SCHEDULES, TARIFFS AND ANNUAL INSTRUMENTS — 3 CREDITS EACH.**

The Witness Training Compendium, Part Three, was in the middle basket at four inches from the left-hand end, spine out, third printing, revised 1926, three credits. The Schedule of Registered Procedures and Fees was three inches from the right-hand end, grey board, 1929 printing, three credits. He stood in front of the two of them for a while and a woman came and stood beside him and read the spine of the Compendium because that is what people do in front of a wire rack.

Elias Venn bought the Schedule for three credits and did not buy the Compendium because he owned it, and the man at the table said the price was the same for all of them and he was not going to be persuaded out of it.

"Which is the more dangerous of the two?" the woman beside him said, to nobody.

"Sorry?"

"The rack, not the books." She had a folder under her arm and a badge on a lanyard and she was about thirty-five. "There's a rack of publications about working in magic in a public building where a person can buy any of them for the price of a coffee, and I've walked past it for nine years, and it's the only rack in this city that nobody argues about. My sister did a grounding and anchor and she did it properly and it cost sixty credits and it was worth every one, and I've never once in nine years heard anybody in this building say *you could have done that for a fiver in a front room*."

"They might have heard it somewhere."

"They have. Everybody's heard it. That's not the same as it being on a rack next to the book that tells you how to do it properly." She looked at him. "You're the one who was on the paint bins."

Elias Venn stood quite still in front of a wire basket in Concord Hall.

"I am."

"My uncle was in that group. He's still in it." She shifted the folder. "I never joined it and I'm not going to and I want to say one thing about it while I've got you, and then I'll leave you alone. In that group people say *they put my brother on paint bins*, and he's got a face, and he's in the group, and he can say *no*. And the woman on the top floor of the building has got a tin with four pieces of paper in it and she has told one person this week, and that person's got a bag, and if that goes the way those things go, then in about two years the argument about whether a man went round Kettle Rows fitting things to walking sticks is going to be *how many of us did he get*."

She put her two credits on the table and went to the basket and picked up the Compendium and paid for it, and went out through the doors into the light off the steps, and she was right, and Elias Venn stood in the ground floor of Concord Hall for about a minute afterwards with a grey book in his hand that he had just bought for three credits and four folded slips in his coat that a seventy-four-year-old woman had given him in a flat on the fourth floor of his own building because she had read something he had written on a wall and worked out what it had cost him.
