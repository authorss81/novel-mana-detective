# Chapter 0089 — Four O'Clock in the Morning

He got up at ten past three on Saturday the twelfth of January because he could not lie in the dark, and he was not able to say afterwards whether he had been asleep or asleep and dreaming about a door, and he wrote that down in his own book at seven in the morning in two lines, and the two lines said *either* and then said *the same thing either way*, and he did not underline it.

He had been sleeping on the bench in the back room of the shop since the eighth of December, which is a bench with a card on the door and a bell off, and on the ninth of January the first night he had got a blanket off the chair because the cold in Kettle Rows in January is not a temperature, it is a thing with a draught in it.

There was a gas fire on the ring in the ground-floor kitchen at 14 Kettle Rows and a table with two chairs at it and a chair with a cushion in it and a kettle, and the ground floor was Otto Vahl's and Otto Vahl was sixty-eight and was asleep upstairs, or was supposed to be.

Otto Vahl was at the table with a cup in his hand and a cardigan over the back of the second chair.

"You have not knocked," he said.

"You have not bolted."

"One is not a courtesy, it is a lock." He moved the cardigan off the second chair with his foot. "Sit down. The gas is on the meter and it is not on my meter either, and it was not on at four o'clock, and I heard it go on at about twenty past three, and I have been awake since two because I am sixty-eight and I do not sleep past five any more, and I came down at half past because I could hear somebody moving about up there and I have a rule about that."

"What rule."

"Do not go up." He said it the way you say the address of a place. "I have had a rule about it since September and you have not asked me about it once, which is why we are still living in the same building, and I am not going to explain it at four in the morning and neither are you."

---

The kitchen table had a cloth on it with a burn in one corner and a set of keys on the cloth and a plate with the crusts off a loaf on it.

Elias Venn put his right hand flat on the table to get his weight up off the chair and his hand went to the drawer under the table instead, and he pulled it out, and there were a tea towel and a battery and a screwdriver and a tin of screws in it, and he shut it, and his hand went flat on the front of the drawer and pulled it out again, and shut it, and pulled it out again.

Four times.

The fifth time he got his hand off the drawer and put it in his lap and held it there with the other one, and the drawer stayed shut, and he sat at a kitchen table at ten past four in the morning with a man of sixty-eight watching him and did not say anything about it for about a minute and a half.

"Right," said Otto Vahl.

"Yes."

"I have been in sheds and on gates and in this building for nine years," said Otto Vahl, "and I have watched a man in a very good coat come down this street about forty times, and there is one thing about him that I could not explain to anybody at the time and I have never told my sister, and it is that he puts his hand flat on everything. Door frames, the bar at the off-licence, the rail at the sorting office. Flat, like that, before he goes through." He put his cup down. "And I have thought about that for nine years and I decided it was a habit, and I was right, and I have never once in nine years thought that it might be the thing itself instead of the habit. And I have just watched you do it eleven times to a drawer, and I am not saying a word about your drawer, and I am going to put the kettle on, and you are going to tell me in the morning whether it has stopped or it has not, and I am not going to ask you now."

"You have just asked me."

"I have not," said Otto Vahl, and got up, and filled the kettle, and put the kettle on the ring, and came back and sat down.

---

They had the tea at about twenty past four with the kitchen light off and the one over the sink on, and it was the first time Elias Venn had sat in that kitchen since the middle of September and he had been in it once and had been thrown out of it once and both of those had been in the same afternoon.

He said who was paying, because it was the only thing in his notebook that had not changed since December and because a man of sixty-eight had a cardigan on the back of a chair at four in the morning and had not asked.

"A doctor on Marshall Street took ninety-odd pages of her own clinic's maintenance book off her own waiting-room wall on the twentieth of December and put them in a box in her own office and the board has got a clean rectangle on it and two pin holes, and she has not been thanked and she is never going to be told why it was the right thing."

"Go on."

"A ward sister of sixty-one with twenty-six years came back onto a ward book on Monday and she has four photographs in a drawer in a building four miles away that she is frightened of, and she has been told by a man from Personnel that she did it properly, and a woman on a linen run with twenty-two years behind her has been asked by me in a corridor at seven in the morning whether I can see things and she has not been asked that in twenty-two years."

"That is three," said Otto Vahl. "Go on."

"A woman of thirty-eight stood in a room in Corrie Street at ten past four yesterday afternoon and said she had got a name and a date out of it and it was her aunt's and her aunt died in 2024, and she has been four times in two years and nobody has her name and nobody has written it down, and she came down a stair on Friday evening past a police inspector who did not follow her. A woman of thirty-one has four months of her own Sundays and is not getting them back. A girl of nine was the address of a working in November and nobody told her and she was not harmed and she has a pony book and she asked me on a doorstep on Wednesday evening whether the man who put the paper up was allowed to be somebody who was on the step."

"And you said."

"I said I am not allowed to be anything at all."

Otto Vahl drank about a third of his tea and then put it down on the table with both hands round it.

"And what did the doctor say about the girl."

"Nothing. She told me there was no question she could answer that would make that afternoon better, and she told me in advance so that I would not be able to think of one."

"That is a doctor," said Otto Vahl. "I have been going to that dispensary for nine years and that is the first thing one of them has ever said to me."

---

"There is a man of thirty-nine in a first-floor room in Corrie Street," said Elias Venn, "who has been doing it properly for six years and who charges eleven credits and who has about two hundred and forty sessions in a green book and about forty reels in a box, and who has used nine of them, and he has told nobody, and nobody has ever asked him anything, including the police, including a doctor, and he is not going to be charged with anything because there is nothing to charge him with until Monday morning, and on Monday morning there will be nothing at all."

Otto Vahl did not say anything for a bit.

"You put your hand in a working yesterday," he said.

"Yes."

"And it did not work and you got that on your own, in a corridor, with one man in a jumper and no coat, and you asked him to stand at the other end and he said no."

"He said no."

"Correct," said Otto Vahl. "I am sixty-eight and I have lived in this building since about 2019 and I have watched a lot of men come down those stairs wanting a second pair of hands and I have never once seen one of them ask a person in a jumper for a second pair of hands, and I have never once seen a person in a jumper say no to a man like you, and I would like you to sit with that for a minute."

---

They had the rest of the tea and he told her about the six inches by two, because Otto Vahl had asked what it was and because a man who has been awake since two is entitled to know what a man is doing on his floor at four in the morning, and Otto Vahl listened to about two thirds of it and then put his hand on the table, flat, the way he does when a job is on the table, and said, "That is six inches long."

"Yes."

"And it is a receipt."

"Yes."

"And they have taken it and they have used him for something with it and they have had a go at it for four years and there is not one thing in it that is not true." Otto Vahl took his hand off the table. "That is a rotten thing and I do not have a way of saying anything about it that is worth anything to you at four in the morning, so I am going to say the only thing I have got, and then I am going to make more tea, and then I am going to go back to bed and I am coming up at ten past six."

"What is the only thing you have got."

"That you cannot be the one who does it," said Otto Vahl. "You have known that since the twenty-first of December and it has cost a woman of sixty-one four days of a drug and a porter in that building his word, and on Friday you went and did it anyway with your hand on a door in a cold corridor and nobody behind you, and I would not have said one word about it on Friday if you had not come down here and been unable to get out of a chair without opening a drawer eleven times."

---

The man was asleep again by ten to six and the kitchen was cold and Elias Venn had not slept and he washed the two cups because a man does not leave two cups, and he put the tin of screws back in the drawer, once, and shut it, and his hand went flat on the front of the drawer and he took it away and put it in his pocket.

He was in the back room of the shop with the door shut and the bell off at about a quarter to nine, and there were five jobs on the bench, and he did four of them and could not have told anybody afterwards what he had done with any of the four.

A man of about sixty came in at nine with a clock under his arm and put it on the counter and said it had been in a drawer since his wife died in the autumn and he had got it out for the first time and did not want it to run, and would Elias Venn not let it run. It was a wall clock with a wooden case and a painted face and a keyhole with no key in it, and the movement was covered in about nine years of dust and had a hair in it, and the pivot was bent, and the case had been in a dry place.

"I can have it going again by Tuesday for eleven credits and I will not oil it and I will not clean the face, because you do not clean the face of a thing like that, and if somebody cleans the face you have thrown away the only part of it that was old."

"What I do not want," said the man, "is somebody to touch it at all. I want to know whether it is still able to go."

"It is able to go," said Elias Venn. "It is the movement that has had nine years of somebody's house dust in it, and the movement wants to go, and the rest of it does not mind."

He cleaned the movement out with a brush and a hoover on the reverse and did not touch the case, and he wound it twice, and he set it, and he put it face down on a folded cloth on the bench and told the man it would be ready on Tuesday and not to pay him now.

The man put a tenner on the counter anyway and did not want change, and said, "I have been carrying that round the district since about November and I have been in four shops, and one of them told me the price of a clean and one of them told me a price for a service, and I have been to two that would not touch it because it was old, and you have told me the price of a thing, and I am not going to argue with that."

Elias Venn put the note in the till, and the till had stood at forty since Christmas Eve and had not moved, and the last figure written down in it was on the twenty-fourth of December, and there is no figure for the twelfth of that month, and he never wrote one.

---

He telephoned Vine Street from the shop at twenty to twelve on the Saturday, from the wall telephone behind the counter, which he had used four times since September and which belonged to the shop and which he had never put a note on.

Nadia Kest took it herself.

"I am not telephoning you to tell you to stop," she said, before he had said anything. "I have nothing to telephone anybody about. I went back to a corridor on Vine Street on Friday afternoon and I asked one question of one person and I am not going to tell you what the answer was."

"What did you ask."

"I asked whether a person's consent can be produced by somebody who was not in the room. The answer is no, and the answer is no in about four lines, and the four lines have been there since before either of us." She let that sit. "That is a good answer for somebody who is not you, and I have not worked out who it is for, and I am not going to work it out this morning."

"I am not going to be in Corrie Street tomorrow."

"I did not think you would be, and if you were I would not have let you in, and I would have said so on the stair, and we would have wasted about four minutes." A pause. "Elias. What happened on Friday is going in a file in about three weeks with a number on it, and the number will not have your name on it, and in four years somebody in a room is going to read that a member of the public put an act into a working in a first-floor room in Corrie Street and that it failed, and that is what it is going to say, and there is not one word in it about a drawer in Kettle Rows at four o'clock this morning."

"I have not told you about a drawer."

"No," said Nadia Kest. "And I have not asked, and I want to say one thing about that, and then I am going to ring off because I have got a thing at one. **I am not asking you about the drawer because you will tell me about it in about four months when it is a fact instead of a thing, and if I ask you now then I get a version of it, and I have had a version of a thing off you once and I did not like it.**"

"That was December and it was fair."

"That was December and it was fair and it was also me standing in a corridor being told something by a man who needed to have said it, and I have been in this job twenty-two years and I can tell the difference and so can you." The line went quiet for a second. "Are you all right."

"No."

"Good," said Nadia Kest. "That is the first straight answer you have given me since November and I am not going to make a thing of it, and I am going to ring off now."


Otto Vahl came up half a flight at ten past six with a cup, in the dark, without putting the light on, which is the thing he has done since September.

"I am sixty-eight," he said, "and I am going to bed at nine from now on, and I am coming up at ten past six in the morning, and I am going to make the tea and I am going to drink it in the kitchen, and if you are in the kitchen I will say good morning to you and if you are not I will say good morning to the kettle."

"That is not necessary."

"No," said Otto Vahl, from the bottom of the stairs, in the dark, with a cup. "It is not necessary. It is nine o'clock at night and I am not coming up, and that has been an arrangement since September because an accident can be stopped by somebody being difficult, and as of about four o'clock this morning it is a job, and I have had two jobs in my life and one of them paid and this one will not and I would like that on a piece of paper, and it will not be on a piece of paper, and that is why I am telling you in a staircase at ten past six in the morning."

Elias Venn did not go to Saint Orra Reach and did not telephone anybody in it, and did not go back to Corrie Street, and did not go to the municipal building, and he sat at the front bench of his own shop at about a quarter to eleven on Saturday morning and put his hand flat on the counter, and then he picked up a piece of strip card, and then he put his hand flat on the counter again.

At ten past eleven the bell on the shop door went, and he got up and went and opened it, and shut it behind the woman, and stood there for a second with his hand still on it.
