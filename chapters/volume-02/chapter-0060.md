# Chapter 0060 — The Third Box

The notebook went from the back of the book forward twice between a quarter to eight and half past nine on Tuesday morning and it took him an hour and a half to do it, because a man who reads his own handwriting hunting for something is not looking, he is checking, and checking is a different thing and takes four times as long.

Four months. Four hundred and thirty pages, most of them four lines.

He went to the fifth of December, which was a Sunday, and the twelfth, and the nineteenth, and the twenty-sixth, and there was a car park in none of them and nothing about a living person in any of them, and he went to the second, the ninth, the sixteenth and the twenty-third as well, and the word *living* appeared in the whole of November once and it was in a line about a woman downstairs on Sallow Lane who had not written her own name on anything.

Then he went to September and October, every Sunday in both months, eight of them, and there were three car parks in the two months and every one of them was a Tuesday and none of them had anything in it about a living person, and the closest was the fifteenth of October outside Concord Hall at about eleven in the morning with a woman in a coat who had a folder under her arm and who had told him about a practice note with his own disagreement in it as a worked example, and that had not been on a Sunday and had not been a promise and had not been a car park.

He got to the third of November and stopped, because the third of November is a Saturday and the memory says a Sunday, and the whole of Saturday the third of November is a formal reprimand with a secretary in the room reading the difference between two things, and after that a shop at ten past one, and a laboratory at ten past four, and a flat at twenty past four, and Marshall Street in the rain at twenty to five.

He wrote it out as a heading and then could not think what to put under it, and put nothing, and turned the notebook round so it was facing away from him.

Then he got up and went upstairs and got his coat, and the note that had been in the inside pocket of it since the tenth of October was on the back of the chair where he had put it on the twentieth of November, and he had decided three weeks ago that he was not going to write to Marguerite Osei, and he did not write to her, and he took the coat off the chair and put it on.

---

The waiting room of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic had nineteen people in it at ten minutes past eleven on Tuesday morning and about eleven of them were standing up, because there had been no clinic this morning, for reasons nobody in the corridor had been told, and Sana Okafor had not said any of them out loud.

Behind the polythene on the board there were three kinds of paper. There were ninety-odd copied pages of the clinic's own maintenance book, going back forty-one months, in a fan so that each page showed an edge. There were four sheets of paper in a doctor's ink with four drawing pins in the corners and three lines at the top of the first one, and Dr Okafor's name under his, and it had been there since the eighth of October and had been photographed once by a sergeant who had asked for no form. And there was a registrar's transfer order in a clear sleeve that had been on that board since the twenty-first of September and was nobody's business and had never been taken down.

Elias Venn had brought the sheet in his coat, in a plastic sleeve he had bought for the purpose, and he had not discussed it with anybody except a doctor at nine o'clock that morning, and she had said one sentence, which was: *It goes up on my board. You do not do the three lines. I do the three lines, and they say what it is, and my name goes under it, and you do not get a say and I will not be argued with before eleven.*

At 11:00 he put up the fourth kind of paper with four drawing pins of his own out of his own pocket, and he did it himself, because it was his sheet to put up and not hers.

The sheet went up at the top of the fan, on its own, with air round it on all four sides.

The three lines Dr Okafor had written were underneath the heading, and there were three of them, and they said this.

> *A method is in circulation in this district. It is printed on one side of a sheet of A4. It cites a module and a page of the Witness Training Compendium. It is not in that printing, or in any earlier one.*
> *The sheet is not a person's property and it is not a document of this clinic. It is a copy of something that somebody has been handing to people who cannot afford a registered practice.*
> *If you have been given one, or if you have got one, the person who gave it to you is not a criminal for having it. The person who printed it is.*

Elias Venn read those three lines out loud in a waiting room with nineteen people in it, because somebody in the queue in October had asked him a question and had not been answered for a day and a half, and then he stepped aside.

Nobody said anything for about a minute and a half, which is a long time in a room like that.

---

A woman of about sixty came in from the corridor at twenty past eleven with her coat on and stood in front of the board with her hands behind her back, the way people stand in front of a thing they have already read, and read her own sheet from four inches away for a while, and then stepped back to read it at the right distance.

Rhoda Nesbit said, "There is a third box."

"There is a third box," said Elias Venn.

"It has been in that plate on that sideboard for four years and I have never used it and I did not know what it was." She said it to the polythene and not to the room. "I knew it was a release. I have known for four years and I have never said it out loud to a living soul, because I did not want to be a woman who was frightened of a piece of paper. **It is a release. A release in a working is not a fourth act. A fourth act is a release that is not the working's own.**"

Nobody in the room knew what she meant and one woman near the back said "what?"

"That is not a formula I have got," said Rhoda Nesbit. "That is the only thing I have and I am saying it in a room, and if there is a man from the Authority in this building I would like him to come and tell me I have said it wrong, and he will not come, and that is not a thing I am proud of, and I am saying it anyway."

Niko Senn had come up Marshall Street on a bus he had paid for and was standing at the end of the room with his hands in his coat, and he had not come to be spoken to, and he read the sheet the way he read everything anybody gave him, which is slowly and at the right distance, and then he read it a second time.

"That is an order," he said.

"It is a set of boxes," said Elias Venn.

"No, it is not." Niko Senn took his hands out of his coat. "I do not know what I know. I have said that in a police station and in this building and I am saying it again and I would like it written down that I said it. But I have held orders since August and I can feel when something is one, and that sheet is a description of an order, and the description is in the order, and it is on one side of a piece of paper, and if you gave that to nine hundred frightened people it would be nine hundred competent jobs and it would be correct every time."

He stopped there. He did not go on. He put his hands back in his coat.

Elias Venn did not ask him another question, and he said out loud, in the room, why.

"He is the only person in this building who has been inside a rehearsed order and come out of it, and I am not going to read him, and I have not read him since September, and I am not going to now, and I have said it in front of nineteen people so that if it turns out later that I did, there is a record that I said I would not."

"That is the first sensible thing you have said in this room," said Rhoda Nesbit, "and it is the second time a person in this city has said a sentence about orders in front of witnesses and it is going in a book somewhere and I am not going to be the one who writes it down."

---

The six lines went up on the same piece of polythene, below the sheet, in his own bad hand, because he had written them in a car park on the Verge Row side and had not changed a word of them since, and they said what they said, which was that somebody in an office in this city is putting a claim number on a procedure that cannot be claimed for, and that there is no other paper in the world, and that a number is not a person and a reference is not a document and a photo of a Tuesday is not evidence of anything.

There were four copies. One went behind the polythene on the board. One went to Sana Okafor's hand. One went into the notebook. And the fourth went to Corrie Street in the post with his own name on the outside, and he posted it at twenty-five past two on the Tuesday afternoon with four stamps on it, because the envelope was the wrong size and the post office on the corner of Corrie Street sold one kind.

She had read it on Thursday and had sent one line back by the same post, in her own hand, and the line said:

*Twenty second floor was three years old. Nobody has ever been there. I went and asked the man on the ground floor and he said there has not been a letting on the third floor since before the war and the fire escape does not go up that far. I am sorry. I am not sorry. I do not know what I am.*

---

He went to 22 Cutlass Row on the Tuesday afternoon at five minutes past three because she had written the words *I am not sorry* and he had spent a day and a half deciding that those four words were a door being held open, and because a man who has spent the batch not following people was going to have to spend some of the end of it following somebody, and he had chosen the moment and not been chosen.

It is a building with a shop on the ground floor and a yard gate at the back and four floors of flats over the shop, and the fire escape is on the outside and it stops at the second floor landing, and there is a second iron stair on the right-hand side of the yard that goes up to the third and the fourth and has been locked at the bottom for as long as anybody alive can remember, and there is a padlock on it and the padlock has been cut and put back about six times by somebody who then put it back so that it looked shut.

A woman of about sixty came to the door of number twenty-two at ten past three and had a cardigan on and a hand in a bowl, and she looked at him, and did not open the door any further, and did not ask him who he was.

"You are the fourth this month," she said, "and the other three were not police, and I would like you to say which you are before I put the bowl down, because I have got a plate in one hand and a wet cloth in the other and I am sixty-one years old."

"I am not police."

"You are the one who was in the shop on Marshall Street in September," said the woman at number twenty-two, "when the man in the rain was asking everybody about the windows, and I was on the top landing and I did not come down because I did not want to be in it. I have been sorry about that for two months and I have thought about it four times a week." She did not put the bowl down. "Are you here about the third floor?"

"Yes."

"Then I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to shut this door, and the first is that the third floor of this building has not been let since before the war, and the second is that there is a plate on that stair and the plate has a number on it and the number is not a flat number, and I have lived here eleven years and I have never once seen anybody come up those stairs, and the stair has a new padlock on it and the padlock is not my padlock, and I have never reported it to anybody, and that is the third thing and it is the worst one and I have got it in a drawer and I have been carrying it since the summer."

Elias Venn stood in the yard of a building in Kettle Rows at ten past three on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh of November 1929 with a plastic sleeve in his coat and a sheet of paper on a clinic wall behind him and a false memory of a Sunday in December in his own handwriting, and the woman at number twenty-two did not open the door, and the stair at the back of the yard had a new padlock on the bottom of it, and he did not have any right in the world to be standing in the yard of a building in Kettle Rows asking a woman of sixty-one what she has got in a drawer, and he was going to have to do it anyway, and the first thing he had to do was not to do it, because the last time he had gone straight in at a building on a Tuesday afternoon in September he had taken a sample off a bench that nobody had touched, and it had cost four examinations and a trade account and four first-scene samples and a certificate on a shelf six inches from a receipt, and he had said *don't let go of that* twice with his eyes shut to a mechanic holding his wrist.
