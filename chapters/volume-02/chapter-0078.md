# Chapter 0078 — Not Shown to Be the Same Hand

The corridor on Vine Street outside the documents room is eleven feet long and has a bench in it and a radiator that comes on at eleven and goes off at eleven-forty whether anybody is sitting on it or not, and Elias Venn was on the bench at five to eleven with his coat still on because he had come from a shop on Kettle Rows and was not going to be asked why.

Nadia Kest came out at eleven with her coat off and sat down on the other end of the bench and did not have a folder, and that was the first thing he noticed and the reason he stopped believing he was going to get anything at all.

The documents room door opened about six minutes after that and a woman of about forty-seven came out with her hair wet at the back and a case under her arm, stopped, looked at him on the bench, and came over.

"You are the man from the yard."

"I am."

"I am not permitted to give you anything and I am going to say one thing to you in a corridor and then I am going to go and be somewhere else." She said it fast and to the floor. "**You will hear four sentences and you will not get a page, and the page is better than the four sentences, and I am sorry about that, and it is not a way of being hard on you, it is that a page goes into a building and you go home.**"

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me, I have written a page and I cannot give it to anybody, which is a thing that happens to me about once a year and this is the year." The case went under her arm again. "And I will say one more thing, which is the only advice I have and I am a civilian so it does not count. **Do not go and look for the hand.** If somebody puts a hand in front of you and says *this is a person*, they have done it to get to the name, and the name is not behind the hand, the name is behind the name, and you will not get there by cutting things open."

Then she was gone, and Nadia Kest said, "That is the whole of what she has said to you in a year and I have watched her do it to two other people."

"I have not got anything from her."

"You have got advice from a person who is not allowed to give you anything," said Nadia Kest, "and in this city that is the most valuable currency there is, and I am aware of how that sounds, and it is true, and I have been on the other side of it for twenty-two years."

"I have four sentences."

"What you are getting is four sentences, and I want to know whether four is enough, and I am going to watch your face, and you are not going to be able to help it, and I am sorry about that."

"Four is enough if they are the right four."

"They are hers, word for word, and I have written them out twice, and the second time is in my own notebook and the first is in a file, and the file is not yours." Nadia Kest looked at the radiator. "One: the letters are cut and not written and the cut is with a tool about two and a half millimetres and one hand did it in one sitting. Two: the hand stopped in the middle of its own name and started again at a different angle and that is a fact and not a habit. Three: **the hand that cut the eleven letters is not shown to be the hand that wrote the memorandum of the fourth of November, and that is a finding and it is not a failure of method.**"

"And four?"

"Four is not what you think it is going to be." She turned and looked at him. "Four is that she cannot compare a knife hand to a pen hand, and never will be able to, and that the comparison she has done is a comparison of where a hand hesitates and nothing else, and that the memorandum has no hesitation in it anywhere because it was written by a professional writer on her own document in one pass, and because the two are not the same kind of act, and because **one of those two people was sure and one of them was not, and the comparison cannot tell you which is which, only that they are not the same.**

"And there is a fifth thing she said, which is not part of the four, and which I am giving you because it is the reason I asked you to stand in a yard on a Sunday."

---

"There is no other hand in this matter to compare it to," said Nadia Kest. "That is what she said, and she said it as though it were the ordinary state of the world and not as a thing that ought to frighten anybody. Every other hand in this case is a writing hand. The claim form, the countersignature, the memorandum, the reply, the two letters a woman wrote back by post, the green book in Corrie Street, the daybook that is ash, the register behind a counter on Cutter Street. **A writing hand leaves no mark like this. A writing hand cannot be compared to a cut and the only reason we know that the tin is a different kind of act is because somebody who has spent seventeen years looking at documents was asked to look at a tin.** So either the person who cut it does not write in this matter at all, or they do, and nobody has ever asked them for a specimen, and in this city there is no form on which to ask anybody for a specimen of anything, and nobody has been asked for one in about nine years, and I checked, and I checked because she said it and I have never once in nine years gone looking for a form that does not exist."

She stopped. The radiator came on and then went off.

"You are going to say something," she said, "and I would like it to be the right thing and I have got about four minutes before I have to go and be somewhere else."

---

Elias Venn took the notebook out of his coat and wrote the four sentences down in the words of a civilian examiner of documents and toolmarks, and wrote them slowly, and then he wrote under them a fifth line.

> *Twenty-fourth of December, Vine Street, from an officer, from a report I have not got. I have got four sentences and I am not going to get the page, and I am not going to ask for it, and the arrangement is correct. A man who asks for a document in a corridor is a man who thinks he has a right to one, and I have been a member of the public since the third of November, and a man who has no right to a document has to find out things like this in corridors from people who have decided to tell him, and that is the whole of what is left of this and it is not nothing, and on the sixth of December a clerk of nineteen years on the trays of a registry district office broke a rule for me in a corridor because I asked her nicely and did not shout, and she said if I came back and asked again she would refuse me again, and she was right twice.*

That was the fifth line and it was not about the case and he wrote it anyway, because a man who writes four lines of somebody else's finding should write one line of his own, and he had been told that in a laboratory in November by a woman who was right about it.

"You have gone quiet," said Nadia Kest.

"I have been thinking about whether the third sentence is worth anything," said Elias Venn, "and I have decided that it is worth a great deal, and that it is worth it in a way that is going to make somebody in about two years extremely uncomfortable, and I am not going to say who, and I would like you to notice that I have said *in about two years* and not *on Tuesday*."

"I have noticed."

"**A hand is not a name, and a name is not a hand, and the third sentence is the first document anybody has ever had in this case that says a thing is not what it looks like without saying what it is.** And the batch — " He stopped, because he had nearly said a word that has no business being in a conversation in a corridor on Vine Street, and he had been in this city long enough to know that you do not say *this is the fourth* or *this is the third* to a police officer, and he changed the sentence in the middle the way you change a step on a stair.

"And the whole of December has been the same shape," he said. "Four offices, four halves, none of them can telephone one another. A ward book with a fee in a margin. A form in a box in a cupboard. Eleven names with eleven addresses and nothing that joins them. And now a tin in a yard with a hand on it that is not shown to be the hand on my own memorandum, and it is the first thing since the seventh of December that is *about* what is happening rather than about what a building is shaped like, and it is worth precisely nothing, and I could not have got it in a month of my own, and I got it in four sentences on a bench from a woman who is not authorised to give me four sentences either."

"Is she?"

"No," said Nadia Kest. "I am. That is the difference between her and me and it is the whole of the difference, and it is why I am the one saying it to you and not her, and why she is in a room writing a page and you are on a bench, and neither of those facts is going to change in your favour in about four years."

---

"Sunday," said Nadia Kest.

Elias Venn did not look up from the notebook.

"Nothing," he said.

"That is what you said to me in a corridor on Bell Street a fortnight ago and it was not true then either, and I have not asked you about it since, and I have decided this morning that I am going to ask you about it exactly once more and then I am not going to ask you about it again in a way that is available to you to answer, and you may tell me to go to hell and I shall not repeat it, and that is a promise and I do not make many."

"There is nothing on Sunday."

"That is the third time you have said that about a day of the week to my face and I would like you to notice that all three of them have been in a corridor, and that a man who says a thing about a day of the week in a corridor is a man who has put it somewhere he cannot get it out of." She stood up and put her coat on. "You do not have to tell me. I am not going to make you and I have thought about it for a fortnight and I have decided that if there were something in it I would want to know, and that if there is nothing in it then you are a man with a bad fortnight, and I know which of those two I would rather be sitting next to, and it is not the one where I am right."

She got to the end of the corridor and turned round.

"For what it is worth, and I am not going to say this again either, I have not got a memory of anything about that Sunday either, and I have looked for one, and there is nothing there, and I do not know what to make of a man and an inspector who both cannot find a thing that is supposed to be in both of our heads." The door shut.

Elias Venn sat on the bench on Vine Street for another nine minutes with the radiator off and the notebook open on his knee and did not write a word about Sunday the sixteenth of December, because a man who writes it down has decided that it is a thing, and he had not decided that, and the sixteenth had been a Sunday and it was gone, and it had gone without anything in it, and there was nobody in this city he could say that to, including the woman who had been standing at the end of the corridor.

---

---

He got to 14 Kettle Rows at four o'clock on Christmas Eve and Otto Vahl was on the ground floor with a set of shop keys he was not supposed to have and had come up half a flight to put them on the counter and argue about, and there were two jobs on the bench and one of them was a kettle.

The first was a lamp base with a terminal block in it that had come loose where it had been screwed to a bracket, and the bracket had been painted over at some point in the last forty years, and the paint had gone into the thread of the screw, and so the screw had turned for about two turns and then stopped dead, and anybody who had tried to free it with a screwdriver had made it worse and there was a bright scar on the head of the screw to prove it.

He had it off the bracket in nine minutes with a piece of strip card between the paint and the head, and the strip card was bent and not broken, and the scar on the screw was not his and would be there when the lamp went back up, and he put the lamp back up and it worked and the customer said the money was in the tin.

"That is the fourth one this month that has been painted over by somebody who did not know what they were doing," he said, to nobody. "And I am the only one who tells them."

"You do not tell them anything," said Otto Vahl from the bottom of the stairs, from about half a flight below, with a set of keys in his hand. "You say *that is the fourth* and then you say *what is under the paint* and then they tell you, and then you say nothing, and that is how you tell them."

The till had been at thirty-one credits since the evening of the eleventh of December, and it had not moved since, because everything he had done in thirteen days had been somebody's second job and he had not charged for one of them, and the kettle went out on the Saturday and the man paid nine credits for it in coins without counting them out of a purse, which is what a man does when he is being decent about something.

It was forty credits in the till at twenty to five on Christmas Eve, and the till was the only thing in the shop that had improved in a fortnight, and he noted that in his own book, on the page where the numbers went, and did not write anything on the page where the case went.

---

The district office of the Proof Registry on Harrow Lane shuts at three and there were nine people in a queue outside it at four minutes to three on Christmas Eve, and one of them was a man of about sixty with a hat in his hands who had been told to come back on Thursday, and had come back on Thursday, and had been told to come back on Thursday again.

Elias Venn stood at the back of the queue for about two minutes and then asked Ruth Callow, at the tray counter, with nine people behind him, a general question.

"Does this office keep specimens."

"No," said Ruth Callow, nineteen years on the trays, without looking up.

"Does it ever have."

"It has got a number and a claimant and a premises and an issuing officer and a date and four schedule lines," said Ruth Callow, "and it has never once had a specimen of anything, and nobody has ever asked this office for one, and I would know, because I would have had to fetch it, and I have been fetching from the wrong side of that wall since the twelfth of October." She straightened the tray. "And I will tell you why, and then you can go and stand at the back of the queue with the rest of them. **Because a specimen is a thing a person brings in, and nobody in this city has ever thought there was a reason to bring one, and I have been on this tray nineteen years and it has never once occurred to me to wonder what an examiner is for, and if you had asked me that in November I would have told you it was a very good question and I would not have known the answer.** That is not a scandal. That is a district office that issues four thousand-odd certificates a year, holds no specimens, and has never been asked for one, and about forty people between this counter and the room at the end of the corridor who could put something in the middle in about an afternoon, and every one of those forty is doing a job and would do it correctly and would not be thanked and would not know what for."

The man with the hat in his hands was called at the counter and was told to come back on Thursday.
