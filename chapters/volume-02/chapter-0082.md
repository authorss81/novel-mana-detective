# Chapter 0082 — Six Inches by Two

The linen office at Saint Orra Reach has a corridor behind it with a fire door at one end and a window at the other that looks at a wall about four feet away, and past the fire door there are two rooms that are on no register in this city, and a sign on the fire door that says MAINTENANCE and has said MAINTENANCE since before the hospital was a hospital.

Elias Venn had a key. She had given it to him in November in a laboratory with four instruments in it and had taken it back in the same conversation, in words, and had then left it on the bench by the sink where he could pick it up, which is a thing that has been done to him exactly once in his life.

He let himself in at ten past eight on Saturday morning and she was already there and had been there since half past six, and the first thing she said was not good morning.

"You have come on a Saturday."

"You said the laboratory."

"I said the laboratory. I did not say a laboratory at eight o'clock on a Saturday morning in January, and if I had said it you would have been here on Friday, and I wanted the two days." She had a mug of tea going cold on the bench and a plate of toast on it that she had not eaten, and she was thirty-eight years old and had been running this room since she was twenty-two, and there were four instruments in it, and two of them were older than the department beside them. "Put the kettle on, you know where it is. The gas is on the meter and it is not on my account."

He put the kettle on.

The room was eleven feet by six and there was a bench down one side with a sink at the end of it, and a second bench along the other side with a stool under it, and a shelf above the second bench with a hard case on it with a padlock on the padlock, and on the same shelf, four inches from the case, a slot with a plate standing in it edge-on, and the plate was about the size of a hand and it was not in the case and it was not in anything.

He did not touch it. He had been lent that plate once, on the third of November, on a written loan with five conditions and a reason, and it had been signed back in at twenty to five that afternoon, and he had read the two overlapping orders on it in a laboratory in November while a woman counted past a number out loud in a doorway, and it had been the only time in four months that his faculty had produced anything that anybody else in the room could use.

The foil stencil was on a plate on the lower shelf and it had not moved since the ninth of October. Three maker's pairs had ever been cut in this city, and the sleeve that came out of a walking stick in October had carried none of them, and that had been a fact in three places in December and he was not going to be the man who broke it.

The log books were on the top shelf, four of them, spines going soft. A log entry and a plate take their numbers from one run, and a log number is not a plate number, and the plate standing in the slot had a number that was also an entry about a corridor bench behind a linen office at half past seven in the evening on the thirtieth of October, and a man who said *log 1201* when the plate was lying on the bench had swapped them, and she had told him that in November and had made him write it down.

---

She did not offer him anything. She made the tea, and she drank half of hers, and she stood at the bench with her back to the instruments and looked at him until he sat down.

"Six years and twelve weeks," she said.

"Saturday the fifth of January, and it is not a quarter past anything. It is Saturday morning and you have been here since half past six."

"It is six years and twelve weeks since the twelfth of October in the year before last, and I have been counting them every Thursday, and I have never once said that out loud to anybody, and I have just said it out loud to you, so you will want to know why."

"Yes."

"Because you would have got it wrong." She put her mug down. "You would have said six years and three months, and six years and three months is what a person says when they are rounding, and rounding is what a person does when they do not want to be asked about the twelve weeks. I have had the twelve weeks, Elias. I have had every day of them, and there is nobody in this city I am allowed to say that to, because the only other person who was in the world on the twelfth of October is not somebody I can put on a telephone."

The name she said after that was **Iven Quill**, and she said it once.

It is a rule of this household, unspoken and absolute, that a name is not said twice in the same room on the same afternoon, and so she said it once, and the room held it, and neither of them said it again.

"He was my younger brother," she said. "He was thirty-one in October of that year and he would have been thirty-seven last month. He is not a patient, he is not a victim, he has never in his life been charged with anything, he is a man who used to mend boilers in two streets and who was better at it than anybody my father ever hired, and he went into a first-floor room on a Thursday afternoon because somebody had told him it would help with a name, and there is no more of him than that and there has not been for six years and twelve weeks."

"I am not going to ask you where you think he is."

"I know you are not, and it is the reason you are in the room."

---

She took it out of the drawer of the bench on the second side, at about ten past nine, and the drawer is the drawer the log books are not in, and the thing came out of a plain brown envelope that had been folded over twice and had never been sealed, and she did not take the envelope to the bench. She took the paper out of the envelope and put the paper on the bench and put the envelope face down beside it, and she did not let go of it for about four seconds and then she did.

It is about six inches long and two inches high. It is the bottom of a form: the sheet is torn across a perforation that has gone soft, and the top edge of the piece is ragged in the way that a perforation goes when it has been torn and put back together in the same fold a hundred times, and the paper is cream and it is printed on one side and it is foxed at the corners.

None of the five boxes that were on the sheet is on it. Only their continuation is: the foot of the form carries a short rule under each box with what goes in it, and somebody has written into four of those five rules and left the fifth one empty, and the fifth is the one that says who told the patient. Above the five rules there is a name and an hours box, and both of those are filled in as well, which is six things written on a piece of paper six inches long and two inches high, and it is a receipt and not a record.

*Name:* **Iven Quill.**
*Date:* **12.10.23.**
*Procedure:* **D-10.**
*Hours:* **1.**
*Fee:* **11.**
*Where:* **4 Corrie St, 1st fl, back.**

And under all of that, in the same hand, across the full width of the thing, the printed line, and a signature on it from a man who had been standing up since six in the morning, in a hand that got slower towards the end of the name the way a hand does at the end of a long day:

*Patient's own consent. The patient confirms that they have been told, in words they could repeat, what this work is, and that they have had the opportunity to put questions about it.*

And written across the top of the strip, in a different hand, in a different pen, at a different time, leaning right and pressing hard: **RC-3904118/61.**

Elias Venn did not touch it. He put his hands flat on the bench on either side of it, well away, the way he had been taught to do in November by a woman in a smock and then by a woman in a laboratory, and he looked at it for a long time.

"D-10," he said. "What is D-10."

"Recovery of a name or date. One hour. Twenty-six credits on the tariff, unrestricted, and there is a note on it that it is not to be charged twice inside fourteen days." She said all of that the way you read a box out of a drawer. "Eleven credits is what she charged."

"She."

"Rhoda Nesbit. Four Corrie Street, first floor, above the dry cleaner, on a Friday, eleven credits, and she has been doing it for about six years, and I have never met her, and I have never written to her about anything." She turned her own envelope over with two fingers, face down, and did not open it. "He told me in November that he has posted her three times. I asked him no questions about it then and I have asked none since, and I do not know what she wrote back and he has not offered to tell me, and I have not asked him, and both of those are true and you may put them in whatever order you like."

"And you have not gone."

"I have not gone, and you are not going to either, and if you do go I shall find out, because a man of thirty-six who knocks on a door in Corrie Street is a thing that gets described afterwards by somebody." She looked at him. "There is a bell on that door. It is the dry cleaner's bell and it is on a string and it is the only way anybody who works in that building knows there is a stranger on the landing. I have worked that out from a photograph of the shop front on a public register and I have not told anybody that I have worked it out, and now you have it, and I have given you a thing that I should not have given you, and you will do what you are going to do."

---

"Can I photograph it where it is."

"No."

"It would not leave the room."

"No." She did not raise her voice. "Elias. A photograph of a signature is a copy of a signature. That is not a figure of speech, it is how it works — you copy it and then the copy is a thing, and the thing goes in a report, and the report goes to a lawyer, and the lawyer puts it in a bundle, and in about four years there is a bundle in a room with my brother's name at the top of it and a signature on the front that anybody in that room can look at, and he is not here to say *that is mine, that is the one time I did it*." She put her hand flat on the bench, about four inches from the paper. "I have had this in a drawer for six years and it has never been in a room with anybody in it. That is the entire protection this piece of paper has in the world and you are asking me to spend it on a Saturday."

He took his hand off the bench.

"Read me the number," he said.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she read it out twice, and he wrote it in his own book with the word *claim* in front of it, because it is a claim number and not a certificate number and a certificate number is in his head and in no book on purpose and there is a rule about it that he has never broken and was not going to start on a Saturday.

Then she said, "There. And now I am going to tell you the thing I have not told anybody, and then you are going to leave, because you have been in this room for two hours and there is a woman coming at eleven with a plate."

"Go on."

"I do not know what it is for," said Mara Quill.

He waited for the rest of it and there was not any more of it for about four seconds.

"I have had it for six years and twelve weeks," she said, "and I have taken it out about nine times, and I have put it in front of four instruments in that case, and I have had it out of the envelope and in the light and I have looked at it with a magnifier I use for burrs, and it is a receipt. It is a piece of paper about six inches by two with a man's name on it and a date and an hour and a fee and a code and a signature, and every part of it is genuine, and it is the patient's own copy, and there is nobody else's name anywhere on it including mine, and I cannot find one single thing on it that is untrue." She put both hands flat on the bench, one on each side of the strip, about nine inches apart. "I have a laboratory behind a linen office with four instruments in it and I did not go into that office at the age of thirty-one because a stranger put a plate under my hand. I have measured things that other people could not argue with. And I have had a piece of paper in my own drawer for six years and twelve weeks and I have never once been able to make it mean anything, and I am not going to sit here with a man in a shop and guess at it in a room, and if you ask me what I think it was for I will tell you that I do not know, and it will be the truth, and you can go and do what you are going to do with it."

She stopped.

"You may look at it as long as you like," she said. "You may not touch it. And it stays in that drawer, in that envelope, on that shelf, for the rest of the week and for the rest of the month, and if anybody comes to this room and asks me for it, including a policeman, including a doctor, including you in four years, then it is in a drawer, and that is the whole of what is between them and it."

"The drawer is unlocked."

"The drawer has been unlocked for six years," said Mara Quill, "and there is a plate worth more than that strip on a shelf six feet from it that four men in this city would like very much to have, and it is on a shelf, because I have decided that a locked room is a thing you can be argued out of in about eleven minutes by a woman with a form and a man with a hat, and a habit is harder."

She picked the strip up by the edge and put it back in the envelope and put the envelope in the drawer and shut the drawer, and he did not see her lock it, because there is nothing on it to lock.

The corridor outside had a linen cart in it and a porter on the cart and the porter was about twenty and had a radio going. He had been in the corridor with the fire door open behind him for about four minutes and had not come in and had not gone away, and he was doing the thing that porters do with a door that is open onto a room nobody has told them about, which is to be extremely busy nearby.

"That sign's wrong," he said.

"Which one."

"The one on your door. Says maintenance." He moved the cart a foot and moved it back. "There's no maintenance in there. I've been doing this run two years and there's a sink and a bench and a woman who says good morning and there's a case with a padlock on it and that's it."

"Does the sign bother you?"

"No. It's not my door." He looked at the cart. "Only my auntie says if you put a sign on a door for fifty years people stop asking what's behind it, and she'd have kept the sign off and had a sign on saying nothing at all, and then they'd ask about that."

He took the radio off his ear and put it back on and pushed the cart six feet down the corridor, and Elias Venn stood in a fire corridor of a hospital on Saint Orra Reach and thought about a form with five boxes and a line of type underneath them, and did not go back in to say so.

At eleven o'clock a woman came in with a plate in a sleeve and put it on the bench and said it had been in a cold room and that it was not right, and Mara Quill said *sit down there* and did not look at him, and Elias Venn took his notebook out of his coat and put it in his pocket and did not write anything on the page, because a man who writes a name down in a laboratory on his first visit has made a different decision from one who has not.

At the fire door he stopped with his hand on it, which is a thing he does, and which he had done four times in eleven years without knowing that the woman he was talking to could see it, and which she had watched him do in November and had not said anything about.

"Elias."

"I am not going to say it to you twice in one afternoon."

"I know. Go and have something to eat, you look like a man who has had a clock for breakfast." She had picked the plate up off the bench and had not turned round. "There is a woman at a desk in Tanners Row who has been there eleven years and who was there on the Wednesday night, and I have known that for four years and I have not gone, because I could not think of one single question that was mine to ask her. I have thought of about forty of them since Friday."

He did not turn round either.

"Forty is too many," said Mara Quill. "Take one."
