# Chapter 0077 — The Barrow in the Yard

The municipal crate store at the yard on Fenwick Street has a gate, a corrugated shed, a counter with a rate book on it, and eleven crates under a tarpaulin, and it is on no list of anything anybody in this city would visit on purpose.

Wendell Combe had been keeping it eleven years and had a kettle on a ring in the shed and a fireguard round it and a barrow with one flat tyre, and he came out into the yard at ten to nine on Sunday morning with his coat on over his shoulders and the gate open behind him.

"You're the one from the shop," he said.

"I am the one from the shop."

"Mind, I've had a policeman here twice in eleven years and both times it was about a bill." He put the barrow down against the wall. "She is out here in the yard and has been since quarter to eight, and I asked her twice why she was standing about in the cold, and she said because there is no light in here, and that is the whole difficulty and nobody has ever tried to solve it."

The shed had one bulb in it and it was not working, and there was no other light and no window, and the eleven crates were under a tarpaulin on a concrete floor.

Elias Venn stood in the yard and did not go in.

"Am I allowed in?"

"No," said Inspector Nadia Kest, from the middle of the shed, in a voice that was entirely ordinary. "You are not. Neither am I, in the way that matters. This is a recovery schedule signed at both ends and it is on my file and it is not on yours, and the person who opened this gate this morning did so because I asked her to, and she has a rate book and a kettle and eleven crates and no interest in any of it."

"Then why am I here."

"Because every line of that schedule ends *not identified to a person*," said Nadia Kest, "and I have read it about forty times this year and I have begun to think that is a sentence about the schedule and not about the objects, and I would like one person standing in this yard who is not in the service, has no standing in anything, and has no reason to want any of it. You are the only person I can think of. You are here because I asked you to come and not because you have any right to be, and if anybody asks you tomorrow, including a person you have been engaged to, you will say that."

"Who is the second witness?"

"The keeper of the shed, who is a witness to an inventory in the ordinary way and has done this twice before." Nadia Kest came out into the yard and pulled the tarpaulin back off two crates. "It is on the second one. The date is the first of November and it is a Thursday, and the fire was in the back room and the stock rack on the first, and the objects came out of a footlocker in a rented workshop and the footlocker is under the tarpaulin with the rest of them, and that is all I am going to say about the rest of them, because the rest of them are stolen things, and not one of the eleven is yours and not one of them is anybody's, and that is the state of the world on this."

---

The chain took nineteen minutes and every one of it was done by Wendell Combe with his own hands, and Nadia Kest read it aloud as he did it, and the schedule was on a board on the wall and it was not a new document and it had no number on it that anybody said out loud.

The schedule was read. The seals were cut and they were cut in front of three people. The tarpaulin came off. The crate went onto the barrow — which took three goes and a plank, because of the flat tyre — and out of the shed and into the yard, where the light was.

"Photographed where it is," said Nadia Kest.

Rosalind Teague had been crouched in the yard since half past eight with a plate camera on a tripod and had not said anything at all for forty minutes, and now she stood up and looked at the crate.

"How many objects in it," she said.

"Eleven."

"In one crate."

"In one crate, in a footlocker inside it, and the footlocker is underneath and the eleven are in the footlocker."

Rosalind Teague wrote a time on the back of her own hand and said, "Then I am photographing the crate and the footlocker, in place, and then you are opening the footlocker, in place, and I am photographing the contents, in place, and then — and I want this said while three people are listening — then they come out, one at a time, and each one goes on a blanket on this barrow, and I photograph it there, and it goes back in the footlocker, and the footlocker goes in the crate, and the crate goes under the tarpaulin, and **the whole thing takes eleven minutes and I am not going to do it faster for anybody.**"

"Take all the time in the world," said Nadia Kest. "I have got until ten and the district is shut."

The tin came out seventh.

---

It is about two inches long and it is japanned, black, with a chip out of one corner, and it has buttons in it. The lid turns over on a hinge that has been re-bent twice.

Rosalind Teague photographed it on the blanket before anything else was done to it, and then the keeper turned it over, and the inside of the lid came up into the light, and there were eleven letters in it, cut into the japanning with something that was not made for tin.

"There is the thing," said Wendell Combe. "That's the one with the marks on."

"I know that one," said Nadia Kest. "I have read about it for about four months and I have never seen it, and I am not going to tell anybody what is on it, and neither is he, and neither are you, and that includes the fact that I do not know and that I have had nine weeks to decide whether to find out and have not."

"I have never read them," said Elias Venn, from four feet away.

"I know you haven't, because you would have told me by now, and because you are a man who tells people things. It is the one thing in that crate I have never looked at properly and I have looked at all the other ten, and I have had nine weeks of looking at all the other ten, and I am telling you that I would give a considerable amount for it, and that is exactly the sentence a man says in a yard when he is about to do something he should not."

"I am not going to ask you to read it," said Elias Venn.

"I know you are not. That is why I said it out loud, so that if you did I would have it."

---

"I am not transcribing them," said Rosalind Teague.

The three of them looked at her.

"Not one of them, and I want it on the sheet that I was asked and declined, because in about two years somebody is going to bring a report of this examination to a room and there will be a question in it about why there is no transcription, and the answer is that a transcription puts eleven letters into four files, and a report is a copy, and a copy is a document, and eleven letters in four documents is a name anybody can carry out of this yard in a coat." She put the camera down. "And the test is not what the letters are. I could tell you what the letters are in about four seconds and it would tell you nothing at all, because the letters are not the question. **The question is whose hand, and a hand is not a name, and if I write the letters down then in about a year somebody will compare the writing in a memorandum to the writing in a note and they will find that they match, and they will be right, and it will have nothing to do with my work.**"

"Go on," said Elias Venn.

"Give me the light."

The morning was grey and dry and the sun came over the roof of a low building on the far side of the yard at about ten past nine, and it came onto the blanket and stayed there for about four minutes, and Rosalind Teague got down onto one knee and did not touch the tin and worked for a while without speaking.

"They are cut," she said. "That is the first thing and it is the whole of why I am here, and it is why you cannot compare this to a signature and why anybody who tells you they can is selling something. Somebody took something that was not made to scratch tin — not a nail, not a wire, not a blade, something with an edge about two and a half millimetres across — and they held it in a fist, not in a pen, because you can see where the heel of the hand rested, and the scoring stops dead every time it crosses the rim of the lid, and the rim is a flange and the flange is a quarter of an inch high and the wrist was on the flange while the hand worked in the middle.

"The burr, which is the ridge of metal that comes up on the far side of a cut, is on the exit side and not the entry side, and that tells me the tool went left to right, and that is the ordinary way round. On the overlap, where one stroke crosses another, the earlier stroke's burr is cut through and the later one's is not, and that gives me the order the letters were done in, and I have it, and I am not going to give it to you.

"One hand. One tool. One sitting. A right hand. And the depth falls off across about nine millimetres of travel, and I want to be careful here, because nine millimetres is where I would put a change of mind and it is not a change of mind. It is a hand going tired. It is about six degrees of angle across the eleven and it is not six degrees of decision, it is a wrist.

"And there is one thing that is a fact and not a habit, and I will say it once and I will not say it twice." She straightened up. "The tool stopped. At the sixth letter it stopped for about two seconds and came back at a different angle — about fifteen degrees, which is a hand changing its grip or a hand being told to change its grip. **A hand that stops in the middle of its own name and starts again at a different angle is a hand that is not sure of the name.** I cannot tell you what the name is and I am not going to try and I could not tell you if I tried, because I have not transcribed it and I do not intend to, and that is the correct professional decision and not a coy one."

---

"I am not going to touch it," said Elias Venn.

"You have not touched it."

"I would like that written down as a decision and not as a courtesy," he said, "and I would like it written down before I leave this yard, because I have been a member of the public since the third of November and I have got four samples and a trade and a case that I am not allowed to have, and the reason I am allowed to stand four feet off a blanket with a barrow camera pointed at it is that I do not put hands on things, and I would like a piece of paper that says so."

Rosalind Teague wrote something on a card and gave it to Nadia Kest, and Nadia Kest read it and put it in her own notebook, and neither of them said the words to him.

Then she said, "Now the other half, and it is the half that is going to be in the report. Are you the man who was in the corridor on Bell Street in December?"

"Once."

"Then you will know what a named certified record is and you will know what I am about to tell you and it is going to sound like nothing. A hand that cuts and a hand that writes are not the same act and they do not leave the same marks, and there is no examiner in this city who can take a name in a memorandum and a name in a tin lid and tell you whether they are the same person's. There is not one. It is not on, and it will not be on inside your lifetime.

"The only thing that can be compared across a knife and a pen is **where a hand hesitates**, and hesitation under pressure falls after the letters a person is least sure of, and it does not fall in the same place in two different acts by the same person, because the thing they are unsure of is different. So the comparison is a comparison of a pattern, not of a hand, and it is worth what a pattern is worth, and I will sign the pattern and I will not sign the hand."

Nadia Kest had the envelope in her hand. She had brought it out at ten past eight and had not opened it and had not mentioned it.

"There is a name on this," she said. "I want to say the shape of the thing and then I am going to stop talking, because the shape of the thing is your doing and the rest of it is mine.

"In September two people in an insurance building looked at a countersignature on a claim form and did not agree about it and have not spoken to each other since, and one of them thought it was a clerk's loop and one of them thought it was a machine. The memorandum that raised it is a four-line note and it is signed at the top with the name of the person who wrote it, and the handwriting in that memorandum is the handwriting of the person who raised it, and it is the only writing hand in this matter that anybody has ever asked for a specimen of.

"You have had that memorandum in a plastic wallet in your coat since the seventh of December," said Nadia Kest, "and you have not used it, and I have known that since the seventh of December, and you have not offered it to me once, and I have not asked you for it once, because I have no standing to ask and neither have you to give."

---

Elias Venn took the envelope out of his coat and put it face down on the barrow beside the blanket, and did not open it and did not say the name, and it was the only name in the world he had in a document and not in his head, and he had been carrying it for sixteen days and had not once written it in his own book.

"It is on there," he said. "It is a woman in an insurance company's claims department and she wrote a four-line note about something that was not right, and she has never been told that anybody outside that building has ever seen it, and nobody in that building is going to be told that by me, and I am not going to say her name in a yard."

"I will not ask you to."

"You do not have to. It is in your hand now and I have put it there in front of three people, and if it is ever challenged then there are three people in this yard who watched a man put a name on an envelope and decline to say it, and that is worth more than the name."

Rosalind Teague turned the envelope over and did not open it either, and put it inside her case, and wrote the time on the back of her hand again.

"Then I will do it on Monday," she said, "and I will put in a file, and you are not in the file."

"I understand that I am not in the file."

"No," said Rosalind Teague. "You are in a yard on a Sunday, on the word of an inspector, and the only thing that is going to exist at the end of this is a line on a board on a wall in here, with a date on it, and about four sentences, and none of them are yours."

---

The tin went back into the footlocker in the same order, and the footlocker went into the crate, and the crate went onto the barrow in one go because they had the plank, and the barrow came back into the shed and off again, twice, and the tarpaulin went over it, and the seals went on, and Wendell Combe wrote the date and the time and the word *nothing removed* on the board on the wall, and then took his coat off its shoulders and hung it on a nail.

"That's the eleventh time I've written on that board," he said, "and there were nine of them in a fortnight in 2027 and none of them was a Sunday, and I have never once minded any of it and I have never once understood a word of it, and I would like to say something and then I would like to go home."

"Go on."

"You are the first person ever come in here that I have had to explain the light to," said Wendell Combe. "That is all. Twenty-two years I have been in sheds and yards and cellars and I have never once had to explain the light to anybody, and I have explained it to you in one sentence without being asked, and I have been thinking about it since, and it is not a small thing and I do not know what it is. There. Now go on."
