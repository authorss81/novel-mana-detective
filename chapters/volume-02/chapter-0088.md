# Chapter 0088 — The Light Under the Door

He went up at ten to two on Friday the eleventh of January and knocked twice, and the door was opened before his knuckle was off the wood, which told him that the man on the other side of it had been listening for footsteps on the stair since about one o'clock.

"You did not telephone."

"No."

"That is twice in three days and I would like to know what the third one is going to look like, because if there is a third one I would like it to be on a Sunday, when I am not here, and I would like it to be somebody else." Eamon Brack was in a shirt and a jumper with no sleeves, because the room was cold, and the gas fire was off again. "The door is shut. I am not opening it and I want that said before anything else, and I want it said in the room, because you are the sort of man who goes home and thinks the door was not really shut."

"I am not going to ask you to open it."

"You are going to ask me to open it in about four minutes and I am telling you now that the answer is no, and I am telling you now rather than after, and a doctor and an inspector have both stood in that spot and been told the same word, so you may save yourself the question if you would rather."

Behind him, further down the landing, there was a light on under a door.

---

"A woman," said Eamon Brack, when asked, which he had not been. "She came at one. She is not on the book and she is never on the book. She has been four times in about two years and I have never had a name off her and she has never told me one, and she pays in cash, and I have never once had a conversation with her that was longer than the length of a receipt."

"How long has she been in there."

"Since one. It is a two-hour sitting when it is two hours. She asked for a name and a date." He stopped, and then he said, quite differently, "She asked for a name and a date. That is the whole of what she comes for, and I have never asked her whose, and I am telling you that I have never asked because I do not ask, and not because I thought about it."

"What is happening in that room."

"Nothing that I can tell you about, and nothing that is wrong, and I would like those two sentences to be heard together, because in six years I have never had anybody hear them together." He put his hands in his pockets. "She is having what she came for. It is going the way it goes. She will come out at about three and she will go home and she will think about it for a week and she will either come back in a month or she will not, and both of those are her business and both of them are how it has been with four people in two years and none of the four was ever a person to me who was more than a name on a strip I could not use."

Elias Venn stood in a cold room on the first floor of 4 Corrie Street and looked down the landing at a light under a door that had a brass bell on it.

"Brack. I am going to be honest with you and then I am going to do something and I would like you to hear the honest part first."

"The honest part."

"I have not touched the door. I have not been in that room. I have not been within six feet of her in the same material for one second in my life, and I am not going to be, and I am saying that out loud in this corridor on a Friday afternoon because there is a woman in that room and I have a faculty and I do not use it on people and I never have and I am not going to start in a room with the door shut."

Eamon Brack took his hands out of his pockets.

"Then what are you doing on this landing."

"I am going to find out," said Elias Venn, and went down the landing to the door the light was coming out from under, and put his left hand flat on the wood.

The door was painted over about nine times and the paint had gone into the wood at the bottom, and the door was warm, and the light under it was not a lamp under it, it was the light of a room with the blind up.

The working came through the wood the way a working comes through a jamb, and it came through the wrong way round, which is to say that it arrived already in the middle of something, and the room went up out of the floor of him the way it does, and he had a wall of nothing to hold and the left side of his head went and did what it does.

> **MARGIN — There is a working behind this door and it is not finished. There is one place in it where an act has not been put in and the place is not a pause. There is room for one. I can see the room. I cannot tell what the act is, and I cannot tell who is to do it, and there is nobody standing here to do it but me.**

He took his hand off the door and it took him about a second and a half to get it away, and he stood in the middle of a landing and found that his decision had been made by about four seconds of somebody else's room.

It is the smallest thing there is and it is the one that got him. A counterturn is an interruption put inside somebody else's working, in one of the places where the working has not yet decided what it is going to do, so that when it gets there it finds the place already filled by something that is not committed to anything. It needs three things and he had two of them.

It needs a sequence you understand. He had that; he had it better than anybody alive and it had cost him a trade, a case, four samples, a room in a dispensary, four days of a woman's drug, and his standing in a building on Marshall Street.

It needs a physical anchor in your hand. He had a door, which was a door and not an anchor, and he knew that, and the knowing did not stop his hand.

And it needs somebody ready to carry what comes back. There was nobody, and he had not asked. There was a man of thirty-nine in a sleeveless jumper six feet away who had not been asked for anything and did not know anybody was on this landing, and a police inspector twenty minutes off, and a woman behind a door who had asked for a name and a date, and a corridor, and a light.

He went back up the landing.

"I have had my hand on your door for about four seconds," said Elias Venn, "and there is a working in that room and there is one place in it where an act goes that has not been put in, and I have never done this in my life, and I am going to do it anyway, and there is one part of it that I cannot do on my own. It needs somebody at the other end of this corridor who is ready to take what comes back, and there is nobody else on this floor, and there is nobody behind me, and I have had a fortnight to arrange for a person to stand there and I have not done it, and I have a formal reprimand in a drawer from November for touching a thing I was not entitled to touch. Put on a coat and come and stand at the end of the corridor while I do it."

"No," said Eamon Brack.

"It is a two-hour sitting and she asked for it and she is in there on purpose."

"She is in there on purpose. That is why I will not open the door." He did not raise his voice. "I have had a police inspector in a corridor of this building in about 2028 and a doctor in 2027 and both of them stood where you are standing and asked me to open a door, and both of them were right to ask, and both of them were wrong, and I said no to both of them, and this is the third time and I have got better at it."

Elias Venn stood in a cold corridor and had been told the same thing twice inside four minutes, once by a faculty that could not tell him what the act was and once by a man who would not stand at the end of a corridor, and the two of them had not disagreed about anything.

---

He put his left hand flat on the door again at about ten past two, and the paint that had gone into the wood at the bottom had gone in a little further under his palm, and he did not take it off again.

He put the uncommitted act in.

---

Nothing happened for about two seconds, which was the two seconds he spent understanding that it was going to work.

Then the door was cold, and the light under it went out, and the whole of the landing went out, and Elias Venn was on his back on the floor of a corridor with his left hand still flat on a door and the room gone out of the side of his head, and he had put a thing into a working and the thing he had put in had not been a thing with an act in it, and a counterturn is a sentence with a verb in it and what he had put in was a blank, and a blank is not a refusal, and a blank is the one thing in a working that nothing can decide about, and it is the one thing that a person can be made to carry for a very long time.

Eamon Brack's voice came from somewhere above him.

"Get off my door."

He got off it. He got himself onto one elbow and then the other and then he sat with his back against the wall opposite, about five feet away, and put his hands on his knees, and they were shaking, and he looked at them.

"I have stopped it," he said.

"You have not stopped anything." Eamon Brack's face was about nine inches from the wall and he was looking at the door. "Look at the door. **Look at the door.**"

There was a light under it.

"It is going," said Elias Venn. "It is going to finish inside a minute and it is going to finish and she is going to come out of it with something that is not hers, and if I can put one act in there in the place where the act goes—"

"What have you done to it?"

"I have put a space in it."

"You have put a **space** in my working," said Eamon Brack, "just after ten past two on a Friday afternoon, in a room I have been in for six years, and you did it with your hand on my door, and there was nobody standing behind you but me, and I did not know, and I was on the stairs when you came in and I told you that."

"I know what I did."

"You do not know what you have done, because you are sitting on a floor telling me you have stopped something, and I have been listening to that room for two hours and that is not silence any more, that is noise, and it is a noise I have heard twice in six years and both of those times somebody was in that room and somebody else was on this floor and it is not a noise a room makes when it works." He put his hand flat on the door. "It is a noise a room makes when it works and there is nothing in the middle of it. And there is nothing in the middle of it, and I do not know what that is, and I do not know whether to open the door or not to open the door, and I have not known since about two minutes ago and I have known everything about this room for six years."

---

Inspector Nadia Kest came up the stairs at twenty past two with a coat on and a folded sheet in her hand and the wrong expression, which is the expression of a woman who has been called to a building by a member of the public and has brought a piece of paper because there is nothing else to bring.

"Which door."

"The one at the end."

"Is that a warrant."

"It is not a warrant," said Nadia Kest, "and I would like to say the next part out loud in a corridor where a man and a member of the public can both hear it, because I have said it twice in a file and I would like it said once to a person. I applied for a warrant in this district on the first of October. It was refused, two hundred words, four numbered paragraphs, and the refusal was on the ground that the application does not name the premises. **A further application in the same terms is barred.** I have not named the address. I have been told in a corridor that naming the address is a different instrument, and I have believed it, and if I produce a warrant out of that coat in this building I shall be putting a document in a room that carries no person's name, and I have put two of those in a room in Sallow Lane already this year."

She turned the sheet over once and put it inside her coat instead of using it.

"That is the order. It is the only instrument I have. It was served on the twenty-second of November and it runs to the fourteenth of January and it is the fourteenth of January on Monday, and in the twenty-second of November I put two officers in a building in Sallow Lane and in December I put two officers in a room in Sallow Lane for a different thing entirely, and I am telling you that the reason that piece of paper is in my coat and not in my hand is that it does not carry a person's name and it cannot, and a thing that cannot carry a person's name is a thing that gets me into a corridor and not into a room."

"You have three days on it," said Elias Venn from the floor.

"I have three days on it," said Nadia Kest, "and then it is a piece of paper in a drawer, and there is no version of this morning in which anybody in this building is arrested, and no version in which that box on that shelf is seized, and I have thought about the box for about eleven seconds on a stair and there is nothing in an order to allow it, and I am not going to put an officer's hand on a box in a first-floor room in Corrie Street on a Friday afternoon on the word of a man who is sitting on a floor."

"The woman."

"The woman is not a person to me, because she has not given me a name, and if she gives me a name then she is a person and then this is a matter and then I can do this properly." Nadia Kest turned round. "Mr Brack. I am going to put my shoulder to your door and I am going to open it, and I am going to do it now, and if there is something in that room that is dangerous I would like you to step away from the landing, and if there is not, then I have put my shoulder to a door for nothing and it will be in a report, and I would rather have the report than the other thing, and I have had about nine of those in twenty-two years and I would do nine more."

He stepped away from the landing.

She put her shoulder to it and it went in on the third push because the frame had been painted over nine times and the latch had been painted over with the frame, and the room at the end of the corridor had a blind up and a couch against the wall and a table with a glass of water on it that had not been touched, and there was no working in it, and there was no light in it except the one coming in off the landing.

There was a woman standing in the middle of it with her handbag in her hand.

She was about thirty-eight. She was not hurt. She was standing up and she was wide awake and she looked at the two of them in the doorway and then at the corridor and then at the floor, where Elias Venn was sitting with his back against a wall and his hands on his knees, and she said, "How long have you been sitting there?"

"Since about ten past two," said Elias Venn, from the floor. "I am sorry about the floor. I would have got up off it if I could have got up off it."

"You are the one," she said. "I heard you on the stairs. You knocked twice and I thought it was the other one knocking twice." She looked at him for a moment. "Was I all right."

"You were all right."

"I know I was all right. I want to know whether you know I was all right or whether you are being kind." She put the handbag down on the table next to the glass of water. "I have been in four times and I have not had a name and a date for a year and I came here for one and I have got one, and it is the wrong one, and it is my aunt's, and my aunt died in 2024, and I have known that for six years, and I have just found out what it feels like to find out."

Nobody said anything.

"I am not going to be asked anything," she said. "I know what that is. If anybody asks me a single question I shall go and it will be for ever." She picked the handbag up. "Somebody has said something to me on the other side of a door while I was in here, and it was not the man, because the man does not talk, and it was not loud, and I would like it recorded that nobody talked to me and I am not accusing anybody."

She came out into the corridor and went down the stairs at ten past four, and Nadia Kest stood at the top of them and did not go after her, and Elias Venn did not go after her, and nobody wrote her name down anywhere.

---

They were on the landing for about half an hour and then they were in the room with the couch, because Nadia Kest could not do anything else with the half hour.

"Nine used times," said Nadia Kest.

"He says nine. He says eleven he thinks."

"Then write both and let the man decide which one he meant when it is read out." She had a notebook out and she had not opened it. "I am not writing this down, and that is not a decision I am proud of, and I have made it eleven times in twenty-two years. What I am going to do is go and stand in a corridor of Vine Street on Monday morning and ask a question, and the question is whether a person's consent can be produced by somebody who is not present, and the answer is going to be no, and the answer being no is going to be extremely useful to somebody and I do not yet know who and I am not going to try to know this afternoon."

"You are not taking the box."

"I am not taking the box and I am not taking the book and I am not taking a strip out of his pocket, and if he has got a strip in that pocket with a name on it I shall not ask to see it, because in about four years somebody in a room is going to put a strip in front of a jury and say *he signed it*, and the fewer of us who have seen it the better that goes, and I have thought about that for about nine seconds and I am not going to think about it any longer this afternoon."

Eamon Brack stood with his back against his own table.

"You are going to come back on Tuesday," he said.

"On Tuesday there is nothing," said Nadia Kest. "I have told him what I told you and I have told you what I told him, and I am not writing it down, and on Monday the fourteenth there is a word in a box in a drawer in a municipal building and the woman who has to write it is not me and I cannot help her and there is nobody else."

"Then come back on Tuesday," said Eamon Brack, "and bring somebody who can carry it, because I have watched you put a space in a working with your hand on my door and I have watched you not be able to put anything in it, and I do not know which of those two things was the mistake and I have got six years of not knowing things in this room and I would like somebody in this city who knows which one that was."

"I cannot promise Tuesday."

"Then do not come on Tuesday," said Eamon Brack, "and do not send anybody, because anybody you send will be worse than a man who says the true thing to a door."

---

Elias Venn came down the stairs at about five and got his hand on the rail and then took it off it, and then put it back, which was nothing at all, and nobody saw it.

He went out into the street and it was dark and it was that cold hour in January when the buses are ten minutes apart and the shops have their lights on and nobody is in the street. The shop at the corner was shut and had a bell on the door, and he went towards it without deciding to, and he put his left hand flat on the door, at about the height of a handle, and pushed, and the bell went, and he stood in the doorway for a second and came out again and shut it.

His hand went flat on the door.

He pushed it again, and the bell went again, and he stood in the doorway and came out and shut it, and his hand went flat on the door, and he pushed it a third time, and the bell went a third time, and he stood in the doorway again and came out and shut it, and he was aware of all of that happening in a sequence with a first one in it that he could not find afterwards.

"You are going to break that glass," said a woman with a pushchair, going past on the pavement, not slowing down. "That is the third time. My husband does it at the fridge."
