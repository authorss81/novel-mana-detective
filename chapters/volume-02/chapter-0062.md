# Chapter 0062 — The Claimant

The man at the counter had been in the building since half past eight and had been told to come back at ten, and he was telling her so for the second time, and she was telling him the same thing with the same face, and neither of them was enjoying it, and Elias Venn stood four feet away with a piece of paper in his hand that was the wrong shape for anything he needed.

"Mr Anwar, it is not me. I have got a book here with your name in it and I have had it since the fifteenth of November, and it is on the machine, and the machine takes three days, and the machine will not be finished until Wednesday whatever anybody says to you on this side of this counter."

"I have been eleven days on this."

"Then it has been eleven days on the machine and not with me." She put her thumb on the book without opening it. "Will you sit down, because if you stand there I cannot get anybody else in before ten, and if I do not get anybody else in before ten then I am the one who is eleven days late and not you."

He sat down.

"Thank you," said the woman, and she was about forty-four and she had a badge on a lanyard and a card tray with four pencils in it, two of which worked. "Are you all right, Mr Anwar?"

"Am I allowed to say no?"

"You are allowed to say anything you like at me, and the only thing I cannot do is go faster than the machine, and I would like that written down somewhere because I say it every day." She turned round and pulled a chair out from under the desk and sat on it, which took her off her side of the counter, which is not something she was supposed to do. "Two years ago. A stairwell. Sixteen months of a course, and the last four of them were cut, and the four were cut because the assessor said the first four had already given her what she was going to get, and the first four had given her something, and it was not what she was going to get."

"What is the difference?"

"That is the entire question and there is no answer to it that I am allowed to give you, and if I gave you one it would be my opinion, and my opinion is that she could walk by then, and my opinion got her four months of nothing and a letter that says the remaining treatment was not necessary because it was not achieving." She put her hands on her knees. "The machine has had it since the fifteenth of November. On Wednesday somebody who is paid more than I am will look at the first four and either agree with herself or not, and if she agrees with herself you get nothing, and if she does not you get a letter you have to read in front of somebody you are ashamed of in front of."

"What would you like to happen?"

"I would like the machine to be switched off in the year it is switched off," said the woman at the counter, "and I have wanted that every day for two years, and it has not been switched off, so I will do what I can with the pencils that work." She stood up and went back behind the counter. "Go on, then. And if you put a number on my counter I cannot help you and I will have to stop you, and I would rather do that than do it badly."

---

The building was on the wrong side of the street from the office he had been in the previous month, which is what happened to all of them, and the office was a claims administration office, and it was called nothing at all above the door, and inside the door there was a runner on the floor and a row of fourteen chairs against a wall and a wall of frames.

The frames were the reason he came in at ten past nine instead of ten, because a public frame on a public wall is a public document, and the frame nearest the door said:

> **REHABILITATION ACCOUNTS — CLAIMANTS AND PREMISES — current to the first of the month**
> *A rehabilitation claim is made by the account. The claimant is the entity named in the second column. Patients are not claimants. The payer for each account is identified at the counter and is not published. Updated on the first of each month.*

Fourteen accounts and fourteen claimants and fourteen premises, in two columns, in a frame, with a plastic sheet over the glass and somebody's thumbprint on the plastic over the ninth one.

The ninth line said **HARLOWMERE REST AND REHABILITATION LTD — 2 WEXFORD ROW, WING — ACCTS 44-31 SERIES**.

Elias Venn stood and looked at it for about two minutes and did not write it down, because it was on a wall and would be there on Thursday.

"There is a card by the door," said the woman at the counter, "that says please do not touch the frames, and the reason that is on the card is a man in March."

"I have not touched it."

"You were reading it with your hand about a foot off the glass, and I am not going to be the one who told a man in March he had touched it." She put a slip away. "Are you here to make a claim?"

"No."

"Then are you here to ask a general question, because a general question I will answer and a question about an account I cannot answer, and if you have not told me which one you are I have to assume the second one on the grounds that nine times in ten it is."

Elias Venn thought about that for about three seconds, and it was a better rule than any he had heard in four months, and he said, "A general question."

"Go on, then. And if you put a number on my counter I cannot help you and I will have to stop you, and I would rather do that than do it badly."

---

"Who is the claimant on a rehabilitation claim."

She wrote nothing. That was the first thing he noticed and it was the thing that made him stay.

"Right," said Maureen Skelland. "A claim has a number and the number means nothing, and it has a claimant, and the claimant is a legal person, and a legal person is not a nurse and it is not a patient and it is not you and it is not me. It is a company or an account or a trust, and it has a premises and a registration and a set of books and an address, and it exists because somebody put it on a register, and the register is a real book in a real building and you can go and stand in front of it. That is the claimant. That is the whole of what a claimant is."

"And the patient."

"Is a participant in the claim and not a party to it." She said it evenly, the way a person says a thing they have said four hundred times. "The patient is in the claim. The patient is not the claim. The claim settles to the claimant, so the money goes to the claimant and the claimant pays for the treatment, and none of that has anything to do with the patient and it is not any of the patient's business, and the reason that is the law and not an opinion is that if it were the patient's business then a patient who was unhappy could stop it, and about a third of them would, and the whole machine would not run."

"A third of them."

"I made that up. I have made it up three times in two years and I would like you to notice that I have made it up and I have not stopped, because a woman who is tired says a number, and I do not want to talk about my job." She turned the card tray a quarter turn. "Next part. The claim is made by the account, from the account's own premises, on a form, and the form has a patient schedule on it, and the patient schedule is a **ward, a bed and a date**, and it is not a name, and it has not been a name since the nineteen-seventies and there was a fight about that and the ward won."

"And a procedure."

"Service code. Number, and the code is out of the published schedule, and it has a price on it, and the price is the tariff, and the tariff is the reason there is an argument at all, because the tariff is a public document and it is three credits and anybody can read it." She looked at him. "You know the schedule. You have read the schedule. I can tell because of how you said *schedule*, and if you have read it then you know that a claim cannot be made in respect of most of the memory procedures, and if you know that then you know that every one of those claims you are thinking about was either made before the prohibition or was not made at all."

"Or it was made anyway," said Elias Venn.

"Or it was made anyway. I have worked on eleven files where it was made anyway and I have never once seen a file where somebody was punished for it, and I have never once seen a file where it was noticed, and those two facts are the entire reason I am good at this job." She stopped. "Next part, and then you will go away and let me do the machine."

"The certificate."

"Certificate, where a certificate is required, and a certificate is required when a procedure is one that has to be certified rather than billed, and it is issued by a district office of the Proof Registry and it is issued **to the payer**, and it is issued to the payer and not to the patient, and the patient is told about it, and the patient is given a copy of the claim and not a copy of the certificate, and a copy of the claim tells them what was billed and not what was certified."

"What is the last one for?"

"The last one is for the patient, so that the patient is not frightened, and it works about four times in five, and the fifth is a patient who takes it to a solicitor." She put both hands flat on the counter. "And the thing you have actually come in here to get, so I am going to say it and then I am going to stop, because I can see you are the sort of man who will come back.

"**A patient who has not been given a copy has no document to dispute.** That is not a rule anybody made up. It is the whole of the position, and I have had it in front of me four times in six weeks, and it is not a scandal and it is not a cover-up, and there is nobody in this building who has done anything wrong. A patient with a copy in her hand can go to the assessment office and say *this is not what happened to me*. A patient without a copy is a person who is unhappy. In this city those are two different things and only one of them is a document."

---

He had the number in his head. He had had it since the nineteenth of November, and he had said it out loud twice in a first-floor room above a dry cleaner and written it down nowhere, because a woman of fifty-eight had stood over a table and made him say the sentence out loud before the lid came off and had then told him that if he wrote the numbers down he would have a document.

He had a pencil. There was a receipt counter in front of him with a printed slip on it and a pen on a chain.

He did not write it down.

"Ma'am," he said, "if I had a claim number, could you tell me anything?"

"I could tell you whether the machine had it, and if the machine had it I could tell you the claimant and the account and the premises, and I could not tell you the patient, and I could not tell you the procedure, and I could not tell you the funder, and if I could tell you all four of those things you would be the fourth person this month to be told them by a woman at a counter, and three of the four of you have been in this room and told me afterwards that you had worked it out yourselves." She turned the tray again. "And a person who works it out themselves is a person we can all live with."

---

She had a folder on her knees and she had been in the queue for most of an hour, and when he sat down beside her she did not close it, and it had a matron's frosted coat folded on top of it.

"Mind if I sit. Mine has not been called for an hour and a half and I am not going anywhere."

"You are welcome."

"I have been in this building eleven years and I have never seen anything come out of it that was anybody's fault." She laughed at it, and then she was quiet for a moment. "I do a day a week up on Wexford Row. That is the one with the sign, the rehabilitation one, the Harlowmere building. Six years. Twenty-eight beds and a day room with a jigsaw in it and a woman on the hatch who has been there since I was thirty-one."

"What do you do there?"

"Get people up, get them walking, and once a week stand in a corridor and be bored." She put her thumb on the folder. "And I can tell you every person on that wing. I could not tell you the name of the manager and I would not want to."

"You have been here with a folder."

"My mother," she said. "Nineteen months. She is eighty-two and she fell in her kitchen in March and she had a course, and she was given a copy of the claim, a proper one, four sheets, with the number and the account and the premises and a telephone number a person can ring." She shifted the folder on her knees. "And I have got it in a drawer in my own kitchen and I have taken it out about four times, and every time I have put it back, because every time I have read it I have been reading it as a daughter, and there is no line on it for a daughter. It says what was billed. It says what was not billed. It says a person was on a bed on a day and it does not say her name and it does not say the thing that was wrong with her, and I did not know that was possible, and I have been a physiotherapist for nineteen years and I did not know that was possible."

"Could you ring the number?"

"I have rung it. It is a ward, and the ward cannot discuss anything with anybody, because the ward did not do it, the account did it, and the account is a company. And I have spoken to a man from the account and he was polite and he read me the four sheets and he said *anything else and you will need the assessor*, and he was not being obstructive, he was being correct, and being correct is the entire difficulty, and I have started to think about it at night, which is a bad sign."

"If somebody came in with a number of theirs — a patient of yours — what would you be able to do with it?"

"Ask them for a copy." She said it as though there were nothing else. "That is the whole of what I have ever been able to do for anybody, and I have wanted to be able to do more, and there has never been more, and I have finally stopped minding, because a thing you cannot do does not stop you doing the thing you can do."

She was quiet for a while, in the way of a person deciding whether a stranger is worth eleven minutes.

"And that is what I would want," she said. "Not to do anything. Just to know whether she was on it. I would not do anything with it. I would put it in a drawer and I would know, and I would be a different sort of daughter about it." She closed the folder on her thumb. "There is no line on any of it for that either, and I have checked, and I have checked in the library on a Sunday with a pencil."

"You are not a claimant," she said, after a moment. "You have got the face of a man who has been told a thing is legal and is waiting to be told what is not."

"What is not."

"There is a schedule," said the physiotherapist. "I have asked for it three times in six years. Not a patient's schedule. **The** schedule. The one that says which of the people in that building are on an account and which of the people in that building are a patient's own business. And I have been told it exists, and I have been told I cannot have it, and every single person in that building has told me a different reason and every single reason was true."

Elias Venn sat in a chair in a building with a runner on the floor on the wrong side of the street from the office he had been in during September, and a woman of forty-four said *next* behind him, and he did not tell her his name, and he did not ask her what the schedule was called, and he got up and went out into Verge Row and stood in the rain for about a minute with his hands in his pockets looking at a building with no name over the door.

The certificate that carried the schedule was not in that building, and it was not in the one she had worked in for six years, and it was not in the flat above the dry cleaner, and it was in an issuing office on Harrow Lane that opened at nine in the morning and shut at three in the afternoon, and on Thursday he was going to walk into it as a member of the public and ask to see something he was not entitled to see.
