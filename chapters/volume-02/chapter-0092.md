# Chapter 0092 — Eleven Chairs

Eamon Brack made two cups of tea in a room with a gas fire that was off, in a first-floor kitchen the size of a press, and he gave one of them to Elias Venn and kept the other one and stood at the table with it rather than sitting down, which is a thing a man does when he has decided that a conversation has an end and he would like to be facing the door.

"The rule is from 2019 and it is one line," he said. "I do not ask anybody anything that is not about the sheet. A name is not about the sheet. Where you live is not about the sheet. What you are going to do with the hour is not about the sheet." He drank about a third of it. "And the first of the five boxes on that sheet says *premises*, and I have written *premises* in it two hundred and about forty times, and it has meant this room every one of those times, and I have never had a reason to ask why a person would write anything else in it."

"You did not ask because of the rule."

"I did not ask because of the rule and I have thought about it every Tuesday for two years, and there is no other reason, and I would like you to know that there is no other reason, because you are going to write something down."

The green hardback book was on the table with a rubber band round it and about two hundred and forty sessions in six years in it, every one with a date and a code and a fee and a number of hours, and no names in it anywhere, and Eamon Brack put his thumb on the corner and did not open it.

"It is in there four times," he said. "Same address, four lines, about two years apart. I am not going to say the address out loud in this room, because this room has a bell on the door and I have been in it six years and you have been in it four days, so you are going to read it and you are going to write it down and then you are going to tell me you did not, and both of those are fine and I do not care about the writing down. I care about you saying it in here."

Elias Venn wrote the address down in his own notebook in the corridor at half past eleven with the door shut behind him, and it was **nine Calder Place, first floor, the door at the end with a bell on it that is not a working bell.** A parade, four streets back from the shop on Kettle Rows, which the whole of that end of the city calls Kettle Rows parade. A shop at the end of it, eight rooms above the shop, and a bell on the door of the shop, which is a different bell and is not the one on the first floor.

Then he sat on the second step with the notebook shut on his knee and understood what he had been given, and it took him about a minute.

It was not her address. It was the address of a room. She had written the address of the room four times in a box headed *premises*, in a book with no names in it, on a first floor four streets back from Kettle Rows, and a man who had allowed himself one line of rule in 2019 had carried a woman's front door in his head for six years and had never once been able to do anything with it, because it was not a front door. It was a room where a man does a thing to eleven people on a Tuesday night for a fee, and the only person in this city who could have told him that was the woman whose name he did not have.

He had been given a building. He had not been given a woman, and he was not going to get one out of a book, and the man who had handed it over had known that on the Monday night and had given him the reason instead of the address, and had been right.

"That's a room above a shop," he said, through the door.

"It is a room above a shop and there is a man of twenty-five in the shop who has never been given a key to it and who has told me so twice this week because he thinks I am somebody who would want a key," said Eamon Brack. "Go on Tuesday. Not Wednesday. He is there on a Tuesday afternoon and he is not there on a Wednesday because the shop is shut on a Wednesday and he does the deliveries."

---

Nine Calder Place had a shop with a green sign that had faded to the colour of a two-week-old plaster and a bell on the door and a counter with a card holder on it, and there was a man of about twenty-five behind the counter in a fleece with the shop's name embroidered on it in a thread that had gone at the shoulders.

Elias Venn asked what the rooms above the shop were used for and the man said they were let, and asked by whom, and the man said by a man, and asked when, and the man said on a Tuesday, and asked whether it was every Tuesday, and the man said yes.

"That is my job to say yes to," he said. "I am not saying it to be difficult. I have been in this shop since I was twenty-one and the rooms went to a man in about 2026 and he pays the rent in cash on a Thursday and he has a set of keys and I have never been given one and I have never asked for one, because if you ask for a key to those rooms then you are a man who wants a key to those rooms."

"And this afternoon?"

"He is in the shop on a Tuesday afternoon because the stair door has to be unlocked and the rent gets counted on a Thursday and that is the only day I have ever seen him stand at that desk, and he is in the back room about an hour and I am not allowed in the back room." He said it without any weight at all, the way a man says the name of a supplier. "He is in now. He is in the back room with the door shut and a light on."

"Can I go up?"

"He says yes. He says yes to everybody on a Tuesday, and I have told him that is a bad way to be and he has told me it is the only way he has got left, and I have been arguing with a man about it for two years and I am twenty-five and I am losing."

---

The stair was at the back of the shop and it was a wooden stair with a handrail on one side only, and it went up past a first landing with a meter cupboard on it to a first floor with eight doors, and seven people were sitting on the top eleven steps in coats, and there was a shopping bag on the third step with a bunch of leeks in it, and there was a folded paper on the ninth, and a man of about seventy-four was sitting on the eleventh with his hands on his knees looking at the door.

Nobody was on the landing. Nobody was in the corridor. There was a light showing under the door at the end and a bell on that door and it was brass.

There were seven of them and they were not waiting the way people wait for a bus. They had brought things. The bag on the third step had leeks and a paper with a date on it. The folded paper on the ninth was a bill from a gas company and it had been folded and unfolded about forty times along one crease. A woman of about sixty-three had a zip purse on her knees with both hands round it. A woman of about fifty-two had come in a coat over a nightdress and had put a cardigan over the back of the rail, which meant she had been at something all day and had come here after it. A man of about thirty-six was sitting on the eighth step with his forearms on his knees and his head down and had not looked up once, and when the man of twenty-five came out of the back room and went past he said *she is late* to the floor. And on the sixth step, in a coat, with her handbag on her knees, there was a woman of about thirty-eight.

Elias Venn did not go up past the sixth step.

He stood on the fourth step, below all of them, and said, "I am not going anybody's room. I am going to say three things and one of them is that I am not a doctor and one of them is that I am not from the council and one of them is that I have got a notebook and I am not going to write any of your names in it unless one of you tells me your name, and if you tell me your name I will write it and I will not do anything else with it."

A woman on the third step said, "How much."

"I do not know."

"How much is it."

"I have got a genuine answer and it is that I do not know, and I am not going to make one up for you, because a person who makes a number up on a stair is worse than a person who gives you the wrong number." He put the notebook in his coat. "I am going to say the third thing. You are all sitting on a stair at four o'clock in the afternoon outside a door that a man locks again at about half past seven, and there is not one of you here who can say what the room is for, and I know that, because I have been in six rooms in this city in four months and there is not one of them that could say either."

Nobody said anything.

The woman on the sixth step looked at him for a moment and then she said her name, because he had asked her for it.

"Bernadette Lyle," she said. "That is my name and I have not given it to anybody on that stair in two years, and I have just given it to a man in a coat at ten past four on a Tuesday, and I would like it noticed that I did not think about it."

"That is the first name I have written down in four months," said Elias Venn, "and the first one that is not my own."

"You have a notebook."

"I have a notebook." He wrote it and shut the book. "Now ask me the thing you have come to ask, and I will tell you at the start that I have not got a good answer to it."

"Nobody has ever answered me," said Bernadette Lyle. "Not the man and not anybody. I have asked four times and I have stopped asking, and I want you to know that I have stopped because I did not want to be told off, and that is a thing I have done for twenty years with everybody."

"What would the answer be."

"I do not know. I have decided that I do not want it, and that is not the same as not having it, and I would like somebody to notice the difference." The woman on the third step, with the leeks, put her bag down. "But I will tell you the thing I actually want, since she has said it and you have been decent. I want to know whether I am going to be all right afterwards. Not what it is for. Whether I am going to be all right afterwards. That is a different question and I have never worked out how to ask it, and if you say yes I will go up those stairs tonight and if you say no I will go home and come back next Tuesday with the leeks."

There was a light on the landing above them that came from a bulb with a flex in it, and the flex moved when somebody walked past a door at the other end of the corridor, and nobody on the stair looked up at it.

---

He was let into the room at about ten past four and the man who let him in was fifty-two and about five foot nine and had a set of keys on a ring on his belt and did not offer to shake a hand.

The room was eleven feet by nine with a couch against one wall that had a blanket folded on the end of it, and a table with a glass of water standing on it that nobody had drunk from, and a man of about seventy-four already sitting in the first chair, and a woman of about fifty-one sitting in the fifth with her bag on her knees, and a woman of about thirty-eight standing just inside the door with her coat still on, and eleven chairs.

He counted them twice and it was eleven and he wrote it in his own book in the margin and then put a line through it, and then he wrote it again underneath, because he wanted it in his book in his own writing whatever happened to the page.

"You are early," said the man of fifty-two.

"I am on a stair with seven people on it and I have said three things to them and I am about to be late everywhere else."

"Then be late." He pulled a chair out from the table and did not sit in it. "That is the room. I let it on a Tuesday. I have let it since about 2026 and I have never once asked what it is for and I have told you that and I am not going to tell you the other thing, which is that I would like to be able to tell you that I have never once asked, in a way that you would believe, and I cannot, and I have stopped trying."

"I did not come to ask you what it is for."

"Then what did you come to ask me."

"How many."

The man of fifty-two looked at the eleven chairs for a while.

"Eleven is the most I have ever had in it," he said. "I have had four and I have had six and I have had eight and once, in the November, I had ten and one of the ten left at the top of the stair and did not come in, and I do not count him because he did not come in. **I have never had eleven. I have a room that takes eleven and I have never once filled it, and I want that said by me before you say it, because you have been writing things down since you came up that stair.**"

He said it in a completely ordinary voice, the way a man says the price of a thing.

The woman of about fifty-one said, from the fifth chair, "You have not answered her question."

"He is going to," said the man of fifty-two. "He has been in this city four months and he has not answered one question anybody has put to him and I have watched him work out on this stair that he does not have an answer, and he is going to come over here and tell her that in about nine seconds and I am going to hear it and I am going to be annoyed with him, and then you are both going to go up the stair and I am going to lock this door at about ten to eight, and I would like the whole of that to happen in that order and I would like it to happen now."

Elias Venn crossed the room and stood about four feet from her chair.

"I do not know," he said. "I have been looking for a man for six years and I have found a great many people who were careful, and I have found exactly nobody who is able to tell one of you whether she is going to be all right afterwards, and I have looked in five rooms this week and I have asked about four hundred things, and I would rather tell you that on a Tuesday afternoon than tell you yes, because if I say yes and you go up those stairs then in about six years there will be a man like me in a different city with a notebook who is looking for you because you told somebody on a stair that a man in a coat said you would be fine."

She looked at him for a moment and then she said, "That is the first true thing anybody has said to me about this since 2026," and she got up off the fifth chair with her bag and went out onto the landing and down four steps and sat down again on the third one with the leeks. Bernadette Lyle did not move. She was still standing just inside the door with her coat on, and she said, "I have been in this building four times in two years and I have never once been in this room before eight o'clock, and I have never come up a stair with anybody on it, and I am going to have to go back down that stair in about four hours in front of a man of twenty-five, and I would like somebody to have heard me say that I am going to do it."

"That is the true thing," said the man of fifty-two. "It is also not the answer to the question, and you are going to have to come back with the answer and I am not going to be here when you do, because on a Wednesday there is nothing in this room and I have said that already."

---

There was a cloth over one end of the table and under it a sheet of A4.

It was a plan of the room, drawn in ink, and it was a plan of the room as it was, including the door and the meter cupboard and the shape of the couch, and the eleven chairs were on it in eleven positions, and there was a scale in the bottom corner and a date in the top right in the form 04/06/26 and a code under it that was a claim number in a hand that leaned right and pressed hard.

There was a second plan on the reverse in a different pen and it had the chairs numbered.

Elias Venn did not touch it. He stood at the table with his hands behind his back and read it in daylight with two other people in the room, and one of those two people was a man of seventy-four who got up out of a chair when the plan came out of the cloth, and went to the far wall, and stood there with his arms folded looking at it for about twenty seconds, and then said, "That is us. That is where we sit. That is me in the second one on the left and I have never sat in the second one on the left."

"That is right," said the man of fifty-two. "You are in the second one on the left."

"How do you know that."

"Because I drew it." He did not look at Elias Venn. "I drew it in June 2026 and I have drawn it about four times since and I have put the chairs where I have watched people put the chairs, and there is one place on that plan that I have drawn twice and the second one is faint, and I have never told anybody that, and I am not going to tell you why, and I would like it noticed that I have said the word *faint* out loud in a room with a stranger in it at four o'clock on a Tuesday afternoon and that I do not know what I have done."

Cyril Danbury came back from the wall and sat down in the second chair on the left.

"You have drawn it twice," he said. "Why."

"Because in 2028 I drew it the way I thought it went and then I sat in this room for two hours and watched where I actually went, and it was not there." He put two fingers on the edge of the table about a foot from the paper, and did not go near it. "I draw a place when a person has been in it twice. I have four of those and I have never told anybody, and one of the four is a place I started and did not come back to, and that one is the fainter of the two lines, and I am not going to say any more than that."

---

Elias Venn came down at about ten past six and the light had gone and the shop had a strip light in it and the man of twenty-five was counting the day's takings into a metal box and did not look up.

"You went up."

"I went up."

"He was all right with you?"

"He told me a number and he told me a word and he told me that he drew a plan of his own room in June 2026 and that he has drawn it about four times since." Elias Venn put his hand flat on the counter and stopped it there, which is the thing his hand does now and which he had been told about in a kitchen and has not been able to stop. "I did not ask him a single question about what he does in that room and I want you to know that, because in about four hours somebody is going to tell you that a man upstairs has been asked and he has not been asked."

"Asked about what."

"About nothing. That is the point. In four months I have asked about five hundred things and I have never once asked the right one, and it has taken a woman in a laboratory and a man of sixty-eight and a woman at a forms counter to tell me that, and none of them were police." He took his hand off the counter. "Does he lock up."

"He does. He locks up and he has the only key and it is on his belt, and I have seen him do it about a hundred times, and I have never had a key and I have never asked." The man of twenty-five shut the metal box. "And he unlocks it at about half past seven on a Tuesday because I am in here and he does not want a key to turn in a lock with me sitting on the other side of the wall."

"Then at about half past seven tonight he is going to unlock a door with a man of about twenty-five in the room next to it."

"That is how it works."

"That," said Elias Venn, "is how a room above a shop gets a working in it on a Tuesday evening and there is nobody in the building at all except a man who has been told not to ask, and the man has been told not to ask by nobody, and he is twenty-five, and he has been buying a paper off me for six years and he is the only person in this district who has told me the truth twice for nothing."
