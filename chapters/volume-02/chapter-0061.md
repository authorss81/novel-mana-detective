# Chapter 0061 — Wexford Row

The door at the back of the Aldergate Wing was held open by a woman's hip and there were four hundred pounds of wet linen behind her, and Elias Venn took the other end of it at twenty to seven in the morning on Monday the third of December because she said *hold that* and he was the only person in a forty-foot corridor.

"Thank you," said Winnie Arkwright. "You are not on the books."

"No."

"You are not a doctor."

"No."

"You are a man who has come through a door I left open, which is the whole of what you are, and I have had this job for twenty-two years and I have had three men come through that door in that time and two of them wanted to see a patient." She pushed the trolley through. It had four shelves and a canvas top and it moved on its own weight, which is the one piece of engineering in the building he had not expected. "Third floor's locked. You can go up the first if you want the day room and the day room's not open till nine. Second lift's for the linen at ten."

"Right."

"Corridor's warm till about eleven because they shut the plant down for the night and start it at ten and there's a radiator in this hall that hasn't worked since I was a young woman." She looked at him. "You are going to stand there, aren't you."

"About a minute," said Elias Venn.

"You can stand there as long as you like. Half past eight, the day nurse comes through and she is not going to be as pleased with you as I am, and I have a round at eight and a round at four and I am not paid for a third one." She took hold of the trolley again. "The public parts are the hall, the day room, the canteen and the visitors' room. Everything past the double doors on this side is a ward, and the wards are not a shop."

He stood in the corridor for about four minutes and then let her go.

---

What he had come for was a name, and the name was in a book at 4 Corrie Street, and the book was a green appointment book with about four hundred lines in it in a woman's upright hand, and the address appeared on **one** of them.

Rhoda Nesbit had given it to him on Friday afternoon at the door of her first-floor room while he was standing in it with his notebook shut, which was a thing he had decided to do in October and had not decided against since.

"Saturday," she had said. "Not Friday, I do not work Fridays, and I have told you that three times and you keep asking me for Fridays."

"Wednesday," said Elias Venn.

"Well, that is new." She had gone to the dresser and had not taken the tin, and had taken the green book, and had opened it on the table with both hands and had run a finger down the address column without reading it, the way a person does who knows exactly which line they want and does not want to be seen choosing it. "One line. In six years. The eleventh of September, four o'clock, and it is the only Tuesday I have ever written and I can tell you why I wrote it, because you are going to make me say it, so I will say it now. **She could not travel.**"

"That is the wording in the manual."

"It is the wording in the manual and it is the wording in the tariff and it is in two buildings and I read it in a library in October because you sent me." She shut the book. "A woman who cannot travel is a woman in a bed, Mr Venn, and I have never once put those two things in the same sentence in six years and I have just done it, and I would like you to notice that I noticed."

"You wrote the building."

"Somebody told me the building. I did not ask what part of it, because I never ask, because I am fifty-eight years old and I have four hundred faces in this book and I have never lost one and I am not going to start asking questions in the middle of a Friday." She put the book in a drawer. "I have written that address once in six years and I have never been inside it and I have never been asked to, and you are not going to be able to get in either, and I would like to hear how."

---

So he had come on Monday morning at twenty to seven, and there had been a door propped for the linen run, and he had been let in to hold it, and the rest of the day was going to be about doors.

He went and had a cup of tea in the canteen at eight and it was eleven credits and it was the worst tea he had ever drunk and he drank it.

The canteen was a room with six tables, three of them with formica and a crack in one, and a hatch, and a woman of about seventy on the hatch who had been on it since half past six. There was a laminated card on the table by the hatch that said what everything cost, and the tea was on it at eleven credits, and beneath the price of the tea, in the same type, it said **residents and visitors alike, no exceptions, and if you cannot pay, say so and we will do something about it.**

That last clause had been typed in a different typeface from the rest of the card, and it was not new, and Elias Venn looked at it for a while and then asked the woman on the hatch about it.

"That was a matron," said the woman on the hatch. "About nine years ago. There were four of them eating here every day and not one of them could work out what the tea was for, and she got tired of it, and she came in on a Sunday and typed it and told the kitchen and never mentioned it again. It is still there because nobody has a reason to take it down, and if anybody ever takes it down I will know about it inside a week." She put a cup down in front of a woman in a hi-vis who had not ordered anything. "You are the second person this year who has asked me about that line."

"What did the other one ask?"

"She asked whether it was true, and I said the same thing I am about to say to you, which is that it is true, and that it is the only line on that card that is about anybody." She looked at him. "Are you here about somebody?"

"No."

"Good."

He walked out at half past eight into Wexford Row and stood on the pavement for twenty minutes in the rain with his hands in his pockets like a man waiting for a bus that was not coming, and then he went in again because the rain had started properly, and read the noticeboard under the ramp.

The building was four storeys of pale brick with a yard behind it and a ramp at the front and a strip of yellow paint down the edge of every step, and it had a plate by the door that said, in three lines and a crest, that the premises were registered and licensed and that the license was held by a company, and the company was **Harlowmere Rest and Rehabilitation Ltd** and the number on the plate was a number and not a name and meant nothing to him whatsoever on a Monday morning in the rain.

There was a noticeboard inside the entrance hall under the ramp, in a plastic frame, with eleven sheets on it and a gap where one had been taken down, and one of the eleven was a printed sheet headed **REHABILITATION FUNDING REVIEW — WEDNESDAY 5 DECEMBER — 10:00 — ALL WARDS — PUBLIC ATTENDED**, and underneath it, in a smaller box, it said that six beds on Brackwell were in scope and that a funder's continuation was an item.

He read it twice, standing up, in a public entrance hall, with his hat in his hand, and came out again, and went home, and wrote one line in the notebook at the back bench at ten past nine on the Monday morning with the paraffin heater on and the cat in the drawer.

> *3 December. A company with a registration and a building and four wards. One line in a green book in six years, and the woman on it could not travel, and the two documents that say so are three inches apart on a wire rack in Concord Hall and both of them are public. A person who cannot travel is a person in a bed. I have written that down once and I am not going to write it down again in this book, because a man who writes the same sentence twice has started believing it.*

He did not write a second line.

---

He went back at one o'clock because the day room opened at nine and he wanted to see it with people in it.

The reception desk was in the entrance hall and it was manned by a woman of about fifty called Petronella Osei in a cardigan with a lanyard and a telephone headpiece she was not wearing, and he went and stood in front of it because there was nowhere else to stand.

"I am not looking for anybody," said Elias Venn. "I would like to be in the day room."

"You are welcome in the day room. It is open till four and there is no charge and you do not have to be anybody's anybody." She said it the way a woman says a thing she has said a hundred times to people who were somebody's somebody and were ashamed of it. "Are you all right?"

"I am perfectly all right."

"You are wet."

"It is raining."

She gave him a card with the day room on it and no name on it, and he said thank you, and she said, and this is the part he wrote down afterwards because he did not think of it until the bus, "You did not ask me for a name, and about one in six who come through that door ask me for a name in the first minute, and if I cannot say it then I cannot help them, so I would rather they did not ask and I would rather they came back."

---

The day room had eleven people in it and a television on the wall with the sound off and a jigsaw on a table with about forty pieces in it and nine pieces missing, and it had the specific warm dry smell of a room where forty people have been sitting since breakfast.

There was a man of about sixty in an armchair by the window who was not doing anything at all, in the sense that a man doing nothing at all is a person who has stopped, and who said, without turning round, to nobody:

"It is the only warm building I have been inside for two years. I was in one in Saltmarket for eleven weeks and I had my coat on the whole time."

Nobody answered him and nobody needed to.

There was a woman of about seventy-nine on the second day back after a hip who was doing the crossword on a board with a hundred and twenty squares on it and had got to four across, and there was a man of about sixty being taught by a physiotherapist to hold a cup with two hands, and there was a man of about forty reading a magazine that had been in the building since August.

By about half past two he had stopped being a stranger in the room and had become a piece of furniture, which is the only thing a stranger can become in a day room, and which takes about ninety minutes, and which takes ninety minutes because somebody has to speak to you first and not ask you anything.

The physiotherapist was a woman of about forty called Marguerite and she came over with the cup in her hands and put it down and said, "You are not his."

"I am not anybody's."

"You are standing where you can see the door and the television is behind you, and that is not a thing a relative does." She took the cup away again. "Are you all right? You have got the face people get when they have come to ask whether we are pretending."

"Yes," said Elias Venn. "I have got that face."

"Then I will save you the trouble." She sat down on the edge of the table, which is not a thing anybody does in a day room and which she did because she had done it a thousand times. "We are not pretending. We are doing a hip and a shoulder and four other things on twenty-eight beds with twenty-seven of them staffed, and we shut a ward in October because we could not staff it, and we did not pretend about that either. There is a notice on a door on the third floor and it has been there since the seventh and it is the only notice on that door and it is not a notice about a scandal."

"Who put it up?"

"A ward sister, on the day, with a screw eye and a plastic frame, because that is what ward sisters do." She said it without heat, and then, after a moment, with some: "People come here and they see a warm building and a jigsaw and a woman in a cardigan, and they decide that a warm building is the same as nothing. And then they go and put a sheet of paper up on a wall in Kettle Rows about a woman who charges eleven credits, and she is fifty-eight and she is good at it and the sheet is right about the paper and the two of those things have been sitting on top of each other for four months and nobody has made them sit next to each other."

Elias Venn did not say anything for about four seconds.

"That is a very good sentence," he said, "and I am not going to tell you who said the first half of it, because you have worked here six years and I have been in the building one day and I would be taking it off you."

"I do not want it," said Marguerite. "I want to be able to be standing in this room in three years when somebody in an office changes their mind about all of it, and I cannot do that if I am the woman who said the thing."

Nobody was frightened. That was the thing Elias Venn had not been prepared for and could not put down afterwards, and it took him about half an hour to work out why it frightened him.

He had spent two months in a district where the most frightening document he had read was a price list. There was a man in a good coat who had been in the case once and charged on the second of November, and a card with four lines on it and a word in capitals at the foot, and a page of a public manual with a third box in it that the manual does not contain, and a shop on Cutter Street with a bell on the door, and a protection order in a municipal drawer with a date on the end of it, and a woman of fifty-eight doing published work for eleven credits in a room over a dry cleaner, and none of it was hidden either, and every bit of it was true.

And this was a licensed building with a hot press and a jigsaw and nine missing pieces and a woman in a cardigan who had said a true thing about names to a stranger, and **nothing in it was hidden, and everybody in it was telling the truth, and the whole of what he had come to look at was sitting in a drawer four streets away that he had not yet seen.**

---

At twenty to four a nurse came round the day room and turned the lights down one row at a time, and the man in the armchair said "that is the time, then," to nobody, and put his hands on the arms of the chair and got himself up in three stages with a hand on the wall, and went out through a door marked **ald**, and the physiotherapist said goodnight to a man of forty who said goodnight, and the woman with the crossword put the pen on the board and did not finish four across.

Elias Venn picked up his hat from the back of a chair where he had not put it down.

The jigsaw was on the table and nobody was going near it and the nine missing pieces were in a tin beside it, because a jigsaw in a building with eleven people in it is not finished and never is, and the tin was open, and one of the pieces was face down.

He walked out through the double doors past the desk, and Petronella Osei said "Goodnight" to him without looking up, and outside on Wexford Row the rain had stopped and there was a laundry trolley standing in the yard behind a wall with its canvas top off and six empty shelves on it, and nobody was with it, and the yard gate was open.

He got the bus at ten past four and wrote nothing on the bottom deck, and got in at Marshall Street at half past four, and Otto Vahl had a radio on the bench that had been finished on Friday and had not been collected, and the till had twenty-two credits in it, and the certificate was on the shelf above the back bench on its own with a receipt from the municipal building six inches from it, and there were forty-two days on the order in a drawer in a municipal building four miles away, and on Monday the third of December 2029 he had held a door for a woman he had never seen and been thanked for it and had been told the third floor was locked, and the whole of what he had learned was that he could not get in, and that a man of sixty in an armchair had told him the truth about the building without being asked.
