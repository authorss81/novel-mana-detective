# Chapter 0081 — Twenty-two Years

Otto Vahl had come up half a flight at ten past eight in the morning to say that the light had been on for eight days and that he could see it from the yard, and that he had not been able to work out what a man was doing with a light in January, and that he had decided he was not going to ask.

"You have not asked me once," said Elias Venn.

"You have not been here once," said Otto Vahl, "and I am sixty-eight, and there is a cup on the bench with tea in it that went cold about an hour ago, and I have come up to tell you about the light and I am going back down."

He went back down. The arrangement had been running since September — he did not come up, Elias did not come down, and neither of them treated it as an accident, because an accident can be stopped by somebody being difficult.

The shop had been open every morning since the twenty-seventh of December and it had been the ninth day of doing nothing that anybody could use. There were four jobs on the bench, all of them somebody's second job, and two of them were a lamp base with paint in the thread of a screw and a toaster that tripped a fuse, and both of those he could do with his eyes shut and had done twice. The till had forty credits in it on Christmas Eve and it had not moved since, and the only thing that had come in since was a wireless doorbell that a woman on Tanners Row had paid four credits over because he had not wanted the job and had not told her that, and the four credits were in a mug on the shelf behind the counter and not in the till, because he had not wanted the job and did not want the money for it either.

The woman from Tanners Row came in at a quarter to nine with the doorbell in a bag and a complaint about it, which was that it went off at about four in the morning, and he told her that it would go off at four in the morning because the transformer in it is the cheapest one they make and there is a lark in the design that sings, and she said that nobody had told her that, and he said that he was telling her, and she said that the last man who had anything to do with a thing of hers had told her nothing at all for eleven weeks and she had paid him anyway, and he did not ask who, and she did not say, and she left the doorbell on the counter and went out into the cold without her coat on.

Then the clock. It was a mantel clock, thirty-one years old, and it had come from a house on a street of terraced houses that he has repaired lamps on for nineteen years, and it was losing about four minutes a day, and the woman who brought it said it had been losing about four minutes a day since her mother died in the summer and she would like it to stop doing it for a fortnight, and he took the movement out, and there was dust in the case and a hair in the escape wheel and a bent pivot, and it took an hour and ten minutes and the woman waited on a stool by the counter and did not hurry him and did not talk, and at the end of it he wound it and set it and put it back on the shelf and told her it would be right for about four months and after that it would want doing again.

"I am not going to remember four months," she said.

"Then write it on the back of it in pencil."

"That is a good idea," said the woman, and wrote it on the back of it in pencil, and left.

He did two other things that morning that were not the case. A toaster with a short in the crumb tray, which took twenty minutes. And a lamp base with a terminal block in it, loose where it was screwed to a bracket, where the bracket had been painted over at some point in the last forty years and the paint had gone into the thread of the screw, so that anybody who had tried to free it with a screwdriver had made it worse, and there was a bright scar on the head of the screw to prove it. He had it off the bracket in nine minutes with a piece of strip card between the paint and the head, and the strip card was bent and not broken, and the scar on the screw was not his and would be there when the lamp went back up.

"That is the fourth one this month that has been painted over by somebody who did not know what they were doing," he said, to nobody.

"You do not tell them anything," said Otto Vahl, from the bottom of the stairs, from about half a flight below, with a set of shop keys in his hand. "You say *that is the fourth*, and then you say *what is under the paint*, and then they tell you, and then you say nothing, and that is how you tell them."

He had a shop on Kettle Rows with four jobs on the bench and forty-four credits in a till, and a piece of paper in a coat in a plastic wallet that he had not opened since the seventh of December, and a notebook with four hundred and thirty pages in it.

The false memory was a Sunday in the third week of December and it was gone. He had not looked for it since the twenty-sixth and he was not going to, and nobody in this city knew it existed, and the last time a person had asked him about it in a corridor on Vine Street he had said nothing and she had said she would not ask him again in a way available to him to answer.

The Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street are a stone building with a yard behind them, and there is a municipal crate store in that yard with no working light in it, and the offices have a public counter on the ground floor that opens at nine and shuts at three and sells nothing. It sells forms.

Elias Venn walked in at ten past eleven with nothing in his hand, which he had learned in a dispensary on Marshall Street in November from a woman who had learned it from somebody else: a man who arrives at a counter with a carrier bag has told you what he wants before he has opened his mouth.

"There is a person I am looking for," he said. "He has been missing six years. I am not family and I am not a police officer and I am not a clinician. I am a member of the public. I have got a document on a bench at home that says a man in this city did something to a person in a room, and I cannot get one single thing about that man out of any of the four offices that hold the other halves of it, and I am not going to start shouting about it. I want to ask you one general question, because a general question is free, and then I am going to go."

Nell Cadby was fifty-eight and had been at the forms and publications counter of the Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street for twenty-two years, and she had a cardigan on the back of her chair and a plastic tray of paper on the counter and a rubber band round the corner of it that had perished.

"Sit down, no, don't sit, that chair's for the trade," she said. "Go on."

"What is there about a person who has had work done in a room and cannot be found."

She wrote nothing down. She did not have a pad in front of her and she did not go and get one.

"Three things," she said. "One. The person keeps their own. There is a form, everybody in this city has had one of these, and it comes on one sheet and the sheet has five boxes on the top half and a tear-off line across the bottom, and the tear-off line is about six inches long and two inches high, and the patient takes the bottom of it home and the practitioner keeps the top, and the bottom of it is the only part anybody in law is required to keep, and it is about six inches by two."

"What is on it."

"A name, a date, a code, an hour, a fee, and a signature." She said it the way you say a thing you have said a hundred thousand times. "The signature is the patient's own. There is nobody else's on it. That is the point of it."

"And the other two."

"Two — the person is notified. If a claim is made for their work they get a letter telling them a claim exists, and they get a copy of the claim, and they do not get a copy of anything else, and there is a thing about the certificate that I am not going to say the whole of because it is in print and you can buy it, and the short of it is that the certificate is the payer's and the patient is not the payer."

"And three."

"Three is the one that will do you no good," said Nell Cadby. "There is no office. There is no register of these and there is no place they go. The strip lives in a house, or in a drawer, or in a coat, or in a handbag, or in a envelope with a bill in it, and the reason there is no register is not that anybody decided there should not be one. It is that nobody has ever needed one, because a person who has had a thing done does not come back to anybody to tell them about it. They just do not come back."

Elias Venn stood at a counter on Fenwick Street and found that four months of standing at counters had not got him to the thing he had come for, and that the thing he had come for was about six inches by two and was in a drawer in a woman's own hands, and that this woman had known that in 2024 and had told him nothing because he had not asked.

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it and I have not said anything I am not allowed to say, and I have said it in about a minute and a half, and you did not ask me anything." She put her hands on the tray. "You said one general question. I would have given you the same on Tuesday. What I am not going to do is be asked a second question by a man who has brought me nothing and a story."

"Then I am going."

"You are going to go somewhere with a great deal of nothing, and I am sorry for it, and it is not my job." She pulled the tray about two inches towards her. "But I will tell you one thing, because you have been polite and because you have not asked me twice, and it is the only thing I have got that is worth anything to anybody, and it is not advice."

"Go on."

"The woman who has the strip is not going to bring it here. She is not going to bring it anywhere, because there is nowhere to bring it, and because everybody who has one of these has found out within about a fortnight that there is no counter in this city where a strip means anything, and they stop asking inside a month." She put the tray back where it was. "So you can stand here for a year and there is nothing on this tray that changes it. I have watched men do it and I have watched one of them do it for a very long time."

He had been in a laboratory four streets from a hospital at eight o'clock on a Monday in November and a woman had put a plate under his hand on her own terms, and there were three instruments in that room older than the department beside them, and there was a log book with a number in it that he was not cleared for, and the woman who owned all of it had told him to put his hands where she could see them and had not been kind about it and had been right.

Her rooms are behind a hospital's linen office on Saint Orra Reach and there is no register anywhere in this city on which they appear, and a second room with a bench in it, and four instruments, and a hard case with a padlock on it, and a plate in a slot on a shelf that is called 1201 because a log entry and a plate take their numbers from the same run and a number is not a citation.

She works. She has a brother.

He stood at the counter on Fenwick Street and worked out that she was working, because it was Friday, and that Friday is a working day in a laboratory and not a day in a first-floor room, and that on Sunday she would not be there, and that he had known that she had told him not to come in a first-floor room in Corrie Street and had written it back to him by post in one line.

He had a shop on Kettle Rows with four jobs on the bench and forty-four credits in a till, and a piece of paper in a coat in a plastic wallet that he had not opened since the seventh of December, and a notebook with four hundred and thirty pages in it, and he went along Fenwick Street to the bus stop at ten past one and got on a bus with eleven other people and rode it four stops and got off, and he did not go to Saint Orra Reach, because it was ten past one on a Friday and she was at work and he had no reason to go and no right to go and, on the second of those two, had been told in writing.

The eleven other people on that bus at a quarter past one on a Friday afternoon were a woman with a shopping trolley she had brought on and was going to take off again, a man of about sixty asleep against the window with a newspaper folded back to the crossword, two girls of about seventeen sharing a set of headphones so that one of them had one earbud and was telling the other one something with her hand over the other's ear, a man in a high-visibility vest who got on with a flask and a newspaper and who was asleep at four stops, a woman with a cage on her lap that had a cloth over it and nothing moving under the cloth and who got off at the second stop and carried the cage down the steps very carefully, and six more who were not anybody.

The man asleep against the window had put a mark on a square with a biro and had put another mark on the one after it, and Elias Venn stood with his hand on the rail and looked at the two marks for a while, and then at the man's face, and then away, because a man asleep on a bus at a quarter past one on a Friday afternoon in January in a city like this has done something hard in the four months before this and is entitled to it.

He got off at the fourth stop and walked two streets and stood outside a shop that had a bell on the door and did not go in, and then walked another street and stood outside a building with four rooms above a dry cleaner and did not go up either, and it was twenty to three on a Friday and there was nothing on the page that he was entitled to do next.

---

The tray on the counter on Fenwick Street was four hundred sheets deep and there were four forms in it and the rubber band round the corner of it had perished and she had cut it off on the Tuesday and kept the end.

He had looked at the top of the tray on Friday morning at ten past ten and had not looked at it properly, because he had come in with a question about a man and had been answered in ninety seconds and had gone away holding the word *strip*.

The top sheet was cream and it was printed on one side and it had five boxes on it, and the boxes were in an order he knew, because the same five questions were on a continuity request in a clinic on Marshall Street and on a payer's claim form in an insurance building, and he had heard the order out loud in a room at the back of a tool registry in December.

The fifth box was headed *who told the patient*. He had seen that box on the twenty-second of December and had been told, by a woman of fifty-two at a counter, that it was the only box on either piece of paper that anybody in either building ever filled in.

And below the fifth box, with a half inch of white under it, there was a line of type. It was smaller than the boxes and it was not in a box and it ran the whole width of the sheet, and it was, as far as he could see from where he was standing, one sentence.

He did not read it. He noticed that there was a thing on the page that was not a box, and he stood there for about a minute and a half deciding not to read it, and then Nell Cadby said, "That's the standard footer," and he said, "I know," and she said, "You do not, but that is fine, and it will keep."

There were six people in the queue behind him at the forms counter. One of them was a man of about thirty with a set of car keys in his hand and a tradesman's card on the counter that had not been filled in. One of them was two women who had come together and were reading the four forms in the tray together without touching anything, and one of them said the word *consent* and stopped, and the other one said nothing, and they moved on.

The woman at the back of the queue was about sixty and she had a shopping bag on her feet and she had been coming to that counter for six years, and when the man in front of her had finished she said, not to anybody in particular and not to Elias Venn, that she had been coming since 2024 and that they had never once asked her that either.

She said it the way you say a thing you have said to yourself in a bus.

Elias Venn put his hand on the door of the Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street and stopped with it about an inch from the glass, took his hand away, and went out into the yard past the offices, where there were eleven crates under a tarpaulin and a corrugated shed with one bulb in it that was not working, and stood for a second in a place he had no business being, and then went and caught the bus back.
