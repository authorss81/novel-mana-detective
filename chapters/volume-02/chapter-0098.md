# Chapter 0098 — Two Instruments and One Refusal

Magistrate Dyment had a cardigan on under her gown on Monday the twenty-first of January at about ten past two in the afternoon and there was a fire bucket in the corner of the room with a sign on the wall about what to do with it, and Nadia Kest had a sworn statement on a page and a bag with three things in it and no coat on over her uniform, which she had not done in the four months he had known her.

"First instrument," she said. "A warrant to enter and search the first floor at nine Calder Place, that address being specific, that floor being specific, and the room being the one with a bell on the door, which the officer has described at the desk and which the officer will describe again to the officer executing it, and which the applicant has not been in and does not intend to be in." She looked at the magistrate. "I would like the address printed in full and not abbreviated, and I would like the words *first floor* printed twice, because I have been to a building where a warrant said *the premises* and it turned out to mean the shop."

"That is not usual."

"Then it will be unusual on my file," said Nadia Kest, "and in four years somebody will read it and think I was being difficult, and I will be sixty in four years and I will not be there to be told I was being difficult."

"Second instrument." The magistrate read down the page. "A summons requiring a woman to attend at these rooms at half past one on the morning of Wednesday the twenty-third of January."

"Two hundred and sixty pounds of costs against the authority, and I do not know whether she will come, and I have asked her to and she has said she will."

"Third."

"There is no third instrument," said Nadia Kest, "and I want it minuted that I did not apply for one, and I want to be asked why by somebody so that the answer is in a file and not only in my head."

Magistrate Dyment put her pen down.

"You are not applying for a warrant in respect of a licensed ward on the Wexford Row premises."

"No, my lady."

"That is a strange thing to say out loud on a Monday afternoon with a magistrate in the room."

"It is a strange thing to do on a Monday afternoon with a magistrate in the room, and I have thought about it since Friday and I am going to do it in front of you, and if I do it in a corridor on Tuesday night then it is a thing I did and not a thing that happened." Nadia Kest put her hands flat on the table, which was the first time Elias Venn had seen her do it. "**I know that there are people in a day room in that building tonight and I know that something is being done to them, and I have known it since Friday at about eleven o'clock at night and I have had four days to get an instrument for it and I have not applied for one, because there is no sworn statement about that building and the only person I could get one from is the same district nurse who is going up a stair in Calder Place, and she has been in that room for four minutes on a job and I will not put her on a second building.**"

"You want to go in there."

"I want to go in there," said Nadia Kest, "at about half past ten on Tuesday night, on my own, with no instrument at all, and to stand in a room with three people in it and take nothing and arrest nobody, and to be the only person in this city who has ever done that while being right, and to have it in a report in about three weeks with my name at the top of it. I would like the report to say that I did it knowingly and that I had four days to apply for a warrant and did not, and I would like the reason in the report, and the reason is in the second half of this."

She turned the page round.

"The reason is that a warrant is a search instrument and a search instrument does not name a corridor, or a stair, or a day room, or a person, or a fact. **It names a place. And there is not one thing in this city that stops a working except a person standing in a room, and a person standing in a room in a building with a warrant in their pocket is a person who is allowed to be there, and a person who is allowed to be there is a person who has been sent.**"

"Magistrate, may I say a thing."

"Any officer may say a thing."

"No." Nadia Kest did not look at him. "No, I cannot, because I am the officer and the thing I want to say is for the man who brought the district nurse to my desk, and if I say it he will be reported as having said it, and he has a finding against him in December and the last thing this city needs is another document with his name near the top of it." She turned back to the magistrate. "I have said my reason. I would like it minuted in my words and I will read it before it goes in."

---

The records counter on Fenwick Street shut at four and Nadia Kest wrote out one page in longhand from twenty past five until about ten to six with a pen off a string, and she wrote it standing up, and she read it back to him twice.

"It is one page and it is in the order the things come off," she said. "**One hardback rent book, four years of weekly entries, a signature and not a name, sixty pounds a week. One sheet of A4 with a drawing of a room on it and eleven chairs on it, which I have never seen and have never handled and have been told about by a member of the public, and I am putting it on a schedule because a police officer may not search for a thing she cannot name, and if I have the name and not the sheet then a man in a wig will say the sheet was never in the room. And that is all of it, and there is not a third line, and if there is a third line tomorrow then I have written this page wrong, and I have never in twenty-two years written a page wrong in a room where a man was waiting to see whether I had.**"

"You are writing it tonight so that you can read it tomorrow."

"I am writing it tonight because a schedule written in a room is a schedule made by the room." She put the pen back on the string. "**And I am going to stand in that room at half past five tomorrow with nothing in it, because it is not opened until eight, and there are going to be two hours and a quarter of me sitting on a chair in a first-floor room above a shop in Kettle Rows waiting for a man with a key, and I have never in twenty-two years had a warrant executed in an empty room and I have decided that it is not going to be as bad as the four alternative things and I would like that written down in my own words somewhere.**"

---

The Kettle Rows Community Clinic had nine people in the waiting room at about ten to seven on Monday evening and a board with three kinds of paper on it and a clean rectangle about nine inches by eleven and two pin holes, and Sana Okafor came round the counter with a sheet of paper folded in three.

"Eleven names," she said. "In my hand. Because a member of the public cannot hold a list of patients and you are not going to be the person holding this one."

"You have not asked any of them."

"I have not asked any of them, and I have thought about that for about a day and a half and I have decided I do not need to, and I want you to hear the reason, because I would like it written down somewhere and I am writing it on the back of this sheet." She turned it over. "I am not taking anybody's name off anybody. These are not my patients. They are eleven people who have been in my building at some point in nineteen years and who go somewhere on a Tuesday evening, and I have not asked one of them about it, and I wrote their names down on Saturday afternoon off eleven chairs in a drawing that a man read out of a coat pocket in a shop, and not one of the eleven names came from anybody who told me it. And that is the single most dangerous thing on this sheet, and I am telling you about it because you will find it out in about four years, and I would rather you heard it on a Monday evening from me than off a schedule in a room."

"And the reason I did not ask is not a proper reason for a doctor. It is that if I asked them I would be a doctor on a form in a way I have not been in nineteen years, and I would be frightened of the answer, and that is not the real one."

She put the sheet on the counter.

"Read it in here. Not at home, not on the stair, not in a car. Here, with the light on and the door open, and then it goes back in the drawer in the back office where the other one lives, and if you write anything down from it you write it in longhand and I read it, and on Wednesday morning it goes back in the drawer and nobody takes a photograph of it."

"What is it."

"Eleven names, a street, a floor, a door, and the words *Tuesday, eight o'clock* in a box at the bottom that I drew myself. Eleven names and eleven chairs, and the two lists are not the same length and the two lists do not have to be the same length, and that is not an error, that is a Wednesday." She did not unfold the sheet. "And underneath that, in my hand, there is a list of the eleven things I am going to do on Tuesday and it is longer than the list of names, and the first one is *I am going to be in that room as a doctor and not as the director of a clinic*, and if anybody asks me what I am doing in a room above a shop I am going to say that I am doing my job, and I will be able to say it, because I have never once in nineteen years done anything in this building that I could not put on a form, and I do not know what is in that room and I am not going to find out in a corridor."

"You will be in it."

"I will be in it from about half past seven and I will not leave it until somebody has been put on their side or told why they cannot be, and I am going to be the person who decides that, and I am telling you now so that you cannot make a speech about it at half past seven tonight in a room with eleven people in it." She straightened up. "And I am going to be there as a doctor and I have thought about that for two days and it is the only thing I am sure of, and I would like you to understand that the second I am the director of a clinic in that room, I am a woman with a building and a board and a telephone, and a woman like that in a room like that is not a doctor. I am not going to be her."

---

Mara Quill was in her own laboratory on Saint Orra Reach at about half past seven on Monday evening with the blind up and the fire off and four instruments on a bench, and she had the log book out, and she was not writing in it.

"Five things," she said. "And they are not mine, they are yours, and you will not change one of them, and if you change one of them I shall not come, and I have not said that to anybody in six years and I am saying it to a man who is going to be in a room tomorrow night and it is the only leverage I have ever had over anybody."

"Go on."

"One. You do not go in until I am in the corridor, and I do not go in the corridor until Sana Okafor is in the room, because I am not going up a stair in a building where I do not know who is in the room and neither are you." She held up a second finger. "Two. You do not read anybody. Not one person. Not the woman in the working, not a patient, not the man who is doing it. And I want it said in that room, out loud, in front of a police officer, because a police officer's hand goes towards a frightened woman and I have watched that happen to a colleague of mine in a room in 2024 and it took eleven months to get her back to work."

"Three?"

"Three. **The act is yours.** Not the panel. You. I am not the one who does it and I have been very clear about that since November and I am clearer now, because a thing that comes out of a woman in a laboratory is a warning and a thing that comes out of a man in a room is an act, and the working in that room can tell the difference, and so can the four of us." She put the hand down. "Four. You do not tell anybody what you are going to do, including Nadia Kest, including the nurse, including me, and that one is the hardest of the five for you and I know it is the hardest of the five for me, and I have put it in because on the eleventh of January a man told another man in a corridor exactly what he was going to do, and the other man said no, and he did it anyway."

"And five."

"Five is the anchor." She went round the bench. "Not the door. A door is a door and I have been in this building for six years and I have watched you learn that in a cold corridor at ten past two on a Friday, and I am not going to be unkind about it and I am not going to be careful either. **A thing you made. A thing you can name every part of out loud without hesitating.**"

He took it out of his coat. It was a brass ring, about three-quarters of an inch across the outside, and the shank had been closed and soldered by him in about 2016 with a jeweller's saw and a small torch, and the solder had gone dull, and there was a mark on the inside of the band that was not a maker's mark and that nobody could read.

"Brass," he said. "Shank closed and soldered by me at a bench on Ash Street in 2016, solder about two millimetres, and the joint is on the inside left so that it does not turn in a finger. Band about one point eight millimetres thick and it is not a round band, it is flat on the inside, and there are three shallow files across it that I did for a size and the size stopped being mine in about 2024 and I have not had it resized because I could tell you the thickness of it to a third of a millimetre and I could not tell you that if anybody had put a machine to it." He put it on the bench. "It is the only thing I own that I could put in a room and be believed about."

"That," said Mara Quill, "is an anchor, and it is a stupid one, and it will work about four times in a year and then it will not work at all, and I am not going to tell you the physics of that because I do not fully have it and because I have never once in nineteen years been able to write down the thing that makes a thing hold."

---

"Last thing," he said. "How long."

Mara Quill put her thumb on the log book and left a mark on it and looked at the mark.

"I have been counting since Friday and I am going to stop counting before I answer you, and I want you to hear me do it." She put her hands flat on the bench on either side of the log book, which is a thing she did, and which he had seen twice in six years. "The number is not the time. A number is what I have got instead of a time, because I am a woman who measures things and because in 2024 I held something for a doctor for two hours and nineteen minutes and it is the only number in my life that I am proud of and it did not save anybody."

"Then what is the time."

"The time is how long a doctor can keep eleven people flat. The time is how long a police inspector can stand in a doorway and be a doorway and not a raid. The time is how long a man of thirty-nine can stand at the end of a corridor with his hand on a door he still will not open, having said no once already, in a cold corridor, to a man in a good coat, and having been right." She turned the log book a quarter of turn. "**And the time is how long you can hold a thing in your hand that you made, and mean it, and there is nobody in that room who can do any of the four of those things except you.** So do not ask me for a number. Ask the four of them. And when you have asked them, you will have a length, and it will be about the length of a room, and you will not be able to say it afterwards, and neither will I."

He wrote in his own notebook, in his own hand, and he wrote one line and it said *the length of a room, and not a clock*, and he did not put a number next to it and he never put a number next to it afterwards.

She went round the bench and stood in front of him.

"And I am going to be the one who says the word," she said. "Not the act. The word. There is a difference between those two things and it is the only thing I have left to hand you, and I have been standing at this bench since Monday working out how to give it to you without giving you the rest of it with it. And I am going to be standing in a corridor at about half past eight on Tuesday night with a notebook I do not write in, and I am going to say one sentence to you through a door, and I have decided what it is, and I am not going to tell you what it is now, because if I tell you now you will rehearse it, and I have watched you rehearse things in your own head for four months and it is the only thing you are good at and it is the reason you put a blank in a working on a Friday afternoon."

"What if I get it wrong."

"Then I shall say it again," said Mara Quill, "and I shall say it as many times as it takes, and I have not got anywhere else to be on a Tuesday night, and I have not had anywhere else to be since about 2024, and that is not a romantic sentence and I am not going to soften it."
