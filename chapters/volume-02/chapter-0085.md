# Chapter 0085 — The Sixth Consideration

The forms and publications counter at the Municipal Offices on Fenwick Street is open from nine to three, and there is a trade chair behind it for people who are going to be at that counter longer than a general question takes, and nobody has ever sat in it who did not have to.

He had come in on Friday with a question and had not read the page, and it had taken him until Monday night to work out that the reason he had not read the page was that he had been in a hurry on a Friday, and that a man in a hurry at a tray of paper is a man who reads boxes and not lines, and that on Friday the only thing he wanted off that sheet was a word, and the word was *strip*, and it came off the top.

On Tuesday he came in at twenty to ten and put nothing on the counter and asked the same question he had asked on Friday, in different words, and the woman behind the counter recognised him before he had finished it.

"You have come back."

"You said I could come back on Tuesday."

"I said I would give you the same on Tuesday. I did not say I would give you more." Nell Cadby had the cardigan on the back of her chair and the tray in front of her and the perished end of the rubber band still in the cardigan pocket from Friday. "What is it now."

"What the line is under the boxes."

She did not answer that. She looked at him for about three seconds and then she said, "You are going to make me say a thing I have not said to anybody, aren't you."

"I am going to ask you one general question."

"You are going to ask me about the line under the boxes and it is not a general question, it is a specific one, and I want it on the record that I know the difference, because I have been on this counter for twenty-two years and I have watched about four hundred people not know the difference." She put the tray in front of her and squared it. "Go on, then. Ask it."

---

The tray is four hundred sheets deep and there are four forms in it and nobody has ever bought a form at that counter for the form's own sake. People buy them because they need a piece of paper with a particular head on it and they cannot get that piece of paper anywhere else in the district, and it costs nothing, and the clerk's whole morning is made of people saying *does she have to come in herself* and *is there a fee* and *do you have one of the older ones*.

The sheet at the top of the tray is cream and printed on one side and it has five boxes on the upper half of it, and a perforation across the width of it about a third of the way up from the bottom, and the line under the boxes.

The five boxes are the same five in the same order that are on the clinic's own request in a dispensary on Marshall Street and on the payer's claim form in an insurance building: **where, what, when, how much, who told the patient.** He has heard that order said out loud in a room at the back of a tool registry in December by a woman of sixty-three with a ledger, and he is not going to be told it again today, and he did not come for it.

The payer's form has no fifth box at all, so nowhere on a claim form is there any place to say who told a patient anything, and nobody anywhere certifies it, and that has been true for longer than anybody at that counter has been alive.

And below the fifth box, with about half an inch of white between it and the perforation, there was a line of type. It was smaller than the boxes. It was not in a box and it had no heading and it ran the width of the sheet and it was one sentence, and it was set in a heavier face than anything else on the page, and it was the only thing on the page that was written as a sentence.

> *The patient confirms that they have been told, in words they could repeat, what this work is, and that they have had the opportunity to put questions about it.*

"Read it," said Nell Cadby. "You have been standing there for a minute and a half and you have not read it, and I watched you decide not to read it on Friday, and I have thought about you not reading it for two days, which is more thought than most people get for free at a counter."

He read it.

"That is the line," she said. "That is the whole of the sixth thing, and it is on every sheet in that tray, and it has been on every sheet of that form since before the 2019 printing, and it is not printed by us, we get it from a printer in Fenner Street and it arrives with the batch. I have given out about nine hundred thousand of them."

"Five boxes and a sentence."

"Five boxes and a sentence, and the sentence is in a bigger face than any of the boxes, and I have never worked out whether that is because somebody wanted a person to read it or because a printer thought it looked better." She put two fingers on the tray. "Now you are going to ask me the question, and I am going to answer it, and then I am never going to answer it again, and I would like that understood as a favour I am doing you and not as a favour you are doing me."

He asked it.

"There is nothing in this tray that says what the work is for," said Nell Cadby. "There is a box for where it happens and a box for what it is and a box for when and a box for what it cost and a box for who told the patient, and then there is a sentence that says the person was told what it was, and the sentence does not say what it is for, and it is not a box, and it could not be a box, because a box is something you fill in and this is something you sign, and a thing you sign is a thing you are told is true, and a true thing is a thing that cannot be filled in afterwards by somebody else in a different building on a different day."

She stopped and looked at the middle distance for about two seconds, the way a person does who is deciding whether to say the second half.

"**There is no form anywhere in this city that has a box for what a procedure is going to be used for.**" She said it evenly, the way you read a price. "There has never been one. Not on this. Not on the claim. Not on anything the Registry issues, and I have been in that building, it is a two-minute walk, and I have asked. There is no box for what a person is going to be *used* for, and there is no line for it either, because a line of type is a thing you sign and this would have to be a thing you fill in, and nobody has ever printed one, and I have been giving these out for twenty-two years and I have never once been asked that question by anybody except a man in a shop, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to you that I know why there isn't one." She pushed the tray about an inch. "That is the whole of what I know and I have said it once and I am not saying it a second time to you or to anybody else, and if you write it in a book you write that a woman at a counter said it and not that a man found it out, because that is the order it happened in and the order is the only thing most of our problems are."

---

The queue at that counter on a Tuesday at ten in the morning is not a queue about anything. It is a woman with a tenancy form who has to sign it in front of a witness and does not have a witness. It is a man of about eighty who needs a school form for a grandson who lives in another district and has not been collected. It is a young man who wants a form for a tenancy that has already fallen through and who is angry about the eleven credits it cost him, and who is right about the eleven credits, and who is not going to get the eleven credits back.

Then a man of about fifty came and asked for the fee sheet for 2027.

"I have got a bill from 2027," he said, "and the figure on the bill is not the figure on the sheet I've got, and I am not saying anybody has cheated me, I am saying I would like to know which one is right for that year."

"Which schedule."

"The memory one. The D one. Grounding, and — no, I do not know the numbers. I have got a piece of paper with a number on it and a figure and I would like to know what the figure was in that year." He put a folded piece of paper on the counter. It had a name on it and a date and a number that was not a procedure number and a figure of sixty, and it had a stamp in a corner that is not a stamp anybody makes now. "They say the charge was sixty credits. The sheet I've got says twenty-six. I have had a good deal of use out of that sixty."

Nell Cadby did not pick the paper up. She read it where it lay.

"The twenty twenty-seven sheets went out of stock in the March of twenty twenty-two," she said. "There is a binder of them behind me on the shelf, second one down, and I will get it out, and I am going to tell you what you are about to find before I do, so that you cannot say afterwards that I sprang it on you."

"All right."

"When the current sheet is printed, the schedule is checked against what has actually been charged in the district, because the print run is paid out of a levy and the levy is a fraction of a percent of everything billed, and the people who print it will not print a sheet that is not what is being charged. And in twenty twenty-two nobody could agree what had been charged, and so they printed what was supposed to be charged, and there is a revision date in ink on the back of the contents page, and the ink is not a printer's mark, because a printer marks a revision before he breaks the page, and this one is written by somebody in a hand." She got the binder out and opened it at about two-thirds back. "So which is right for that year."

"Neither," said Nell Cadby. "The figure on your bill is what somebody charged you. The figure on the sheet is what somebody decided ought to have been charged, and those are not the same document, and neither of them is a lie. And there is a tariff note in the back of that binder which says a charge for a memory procedure is not a claim and is not to be settled by anybody but the person doing the work, and that note went into print in November, about three weeks before your bill." She closed the binder. "So you paid sixty credits for a thing the note says could not be charged for at all, and there is nobody in this building who can take that off your bill, and I have said that as plainly as I can, because you were decent enough not to shout at me."

The man stood there for a while.

"That is not an answer," he said.

"No," said Nell Cadby. "It is the shape of one. I have been on this counter twenty-two years and the shape is most of what there is."

The woman with the tenancy form is the one who held the place.

"He has to see it," she said. "The witness part. He has to see me sign it, and I have not got anybody, and he says he cannot witness it at the counter, he says it has to be at the property, and I have had it in my bag since the twenty-ninth of November."

"How long have you had it in your bag."

"Since the twenty-ninth of November." She said it without any particular feeling. "I have not thrown it away because I am going to live there."

Nell Cadby came out from behind the counter and read the form and read the second page and then said, "He is half right and he is being pedantic about the wrong half. He does not have to be at the property. He has to be in the room. Any two people in a room, with the addresses on it, is a room." And she told the woman, in eleven words, what to write at the top of the witness line so that the man could witness it in his own kitchen with a neighbour, and the woman wrote it down in her own hand, and said thank you, and went.

"It is not the form they are stuck on," said Nell Cadby, to Elias Venn, from behind the counter. "It is never the form. It is always one signature on a line in a room with nobody in it. I have been twenty-two years and I have never once had a person at this counter who could not do the thing in front of me, and about half of them could not do the thing at all, and nobody has ever been able to tell me which half I was talking about afterwards."

There is a pawnshop on the parade at the top of Kettle Rows with a grille and a counter at the left of it and a man behind it who has been doing it since about 1988, and Elias Venn went in at twenty past twelve on the Tuesday because his shop is nine doors from it and because he had been in three buildings and a hospital corridor since ten to eight and had eaten a bun standing up.

He was not going to pawn anything. He had come in because a man who has been told that the shape of a thing is the thing itself needs a room in which to be told it, and the pawnshop was the only one within nine doors that was open and had a person in it.

"There is nothing to do here," said the pawnbroker, without looking up. "I do not buy small electricals and I do not buy a ring without a woman with it."

"There is a woman with a ring."

"There is always a woman with a ring."

She was about thirty. She had a coat on that was too good for the parade and a bag that was not from a shop on this street, and she had the ring in her fist and had not opened her fist, and she put it on the counter and did not take her hand away, and the pawnbroker looked at it and did not look at her.

"Six carat," he said. "White, solitaire, about four grams. Hallmarked nineteen sixty-one." He turned it over with a pen. "I can do a hundred and forty and that is a good number and I will write it on a card and you can come back for it. Or two-forty in a loan that rolls over, and that is the same money out of your pocket either way except it goes round twice."

"I would like the hundred and forty."

"Most people want the two-forty." He wrote it on a card. "It is the same money, and I say that, and about half of them say *all right* straight away, and the other half go and think about it, and the ones who go and think about it are nearly always the ones who were going to take the two-forty."

She took the card. She had not opened her fist until the number was on it.

"Thank you," she said.

"You are welcome." He put the pen down. "You do not have to tell me and I am not going to ask, and I have been doing this for forty years and the ones who tell me are the ones who come back and do not buy it back, and the ones who do not tell me buy it back about a month later, and I have never worked out which is which and I have stopped."

Elias Venn stood four feet away with a bun in his hand and a woman's fist open on a counter and a hundred and forty credits written on a card, and he did not ask, because he had spent four months learning that a person who does not ask what is being done to them is the most useful shape there is, and that it is also the shape that a working gets done to somebody, and that the two facts are the same fact and it is the worst sentence in his own notebook and he wrote it down in November and he had not found out since whether it was worse than the one he was carrying on Tuesday.

He went out into the parade and the wind came off the ring road and there was a bus at the stop with the sign on it lit, and the shop had a bell on the door and nine doors down there was a repair shop with a till in it and a light on that had been on since the twenty-seventh of December, and a man in a shop in this city had been told at a counter that a sentence was missing from the only piece of paper in the world that said a man had consented, and had been told it by a woman who sells paper, and had been told it in about ninety words, and could not do one single thing with it.
