# Chapter 0095 — The Last Day She Works There

Sergeant Fenella Carr, 41, had the schedule on a clipboard and read it aloud on the landing of 4 Corrie Street from the top of the stairs so that everybody on the floor could hear it, which is not what the schedule is for, and which she did anyway, and said afterwards that it was the only thing in twenty-two years she had ever added to one.

The instrument was a warrant granted at Vine Street on Thursday the seventeenth of January at about four o'clock in the afternoon, on an officer's own grounds sworn, and the grounds were a complaint from a letting agent about a smell at nine in the evening, and an officer's own observation of a man carrying a cardboard box up an external stair at ten past two in the morning on nine consecutive nights, and neither of those things was a working, and together they were enough for a magistrate, and the order named one room.

She read it twice. The first time was for the record and the second time was for the corridor. It named one room. It did not name the landing, and it did not name the corridor, and it did not name the shop below, and it did not name the room four doors away with the bell on the door, and Nadia Kest said all of that out loud before the second reading so that nobody in the building could later say they had not heard it.

"Mr Venn." She did not turn round. "You are going to be asked to go down to the bottom of the stairs."

"I am not going anywhere."

"You are going to the bottom of the stairs," said Nadia Kest, "because you are a member of the public with a finding against you in December, and because a lawful search of one room is a thing a member of the public may stand at the bottom of the stairs during, and not one inch nearer than the bottom of the stairs. I have thought about it for four days and I have got a piece of paper coming on Monday that says it, and until that piece of paper exists I am doing it without one, and I would like that noted, because it is the third time in nine months I have done something without a piece of paper and I have a very good reason for two of them."

"Go."

He went.

---

The search of one room on the first floor of 4 Corrie Street took two hours and eleven minutes and Elias Venn spent all of it sitting on a chair against the wall at the bottom of the stairs with a fire bucket beside him and a stairwell door that would not shut properly, and he could hear the sound of it the whole time and he could not make out one word of it, and that was the arrangement, and it had been negotiated in eleven seconds on the landing without a single word being said about it in front of the man whose room it was.

What he could hear was: a schedule read twice. A door off its latch. A camera shutter, a great many times, and a voice saying *leave that one, it is in place, move yourself and leave that one* about forty times in two hours, which is the sentence that four people in this city say most often to each other and which nobody outside this building has ever heard anybody say. A bag being opened and closed. A small number of weights being set down on a wooden floor. A pen.

Twice he heard a man of thirty-nine say something short and nobody reply to it.

At about twenty past twelve Fenella Carr came down half a flight of stairs and read the schedule out loud to him, in the stairwell, in the voice of a woman reading a bus timetable, because a member of the public is entitled to know what has been taken out of a room he is in a building he is standing in.

"A green hardback book with a rubber band round it, about two hundred and forty entries, no names. A cardboard box of about forty small reels, each with a paper band, not played, not counted, weighed at eleven ounces with the box. Nine single sheets of a printed method, one page each, headed with a module and a page, and on each of them a third box completed in ink, and every one of the nine completed in a different session. And nothing else."

"Nothing else?"

"There is a box of about forty reels and there are four paper bands on the four reels nearest the front of it that the officer has described as *bearing a date on one side and a fee on the other, written by an unidentified hand*." She looked at him. "I asked her to write *an unidentified hand* because I do not know whose hand it is and neither does anybody in this building, and I am not going to have a piece of paper in a file that says a hand was identified, because nobody has asked a single person for a specimen and nobody is going to."

"Nobody has asked."

"Nobody has asked, and nobody is going to ask on Tuesday either, and I have written that down as a decision and not as an oversight." She put the clipboard against her chest. "The schedule is one page and the return is one page and the seals are numbered and there are three of us and we cut them in front of three people, one of whom is the man whose room it is, because that is what happened in October and it is what happens now."

---

At about half past two Eamon Brack came down the stairs and sat on the step above Elias Venn with a cup of tea he had made in the kitchen at the end of the landing and had not offered to anybody.

"They have taken the book," he said.

"Yes."

"And the box."

"Yes. Eleven ounces."

"That is heavier than it looks and I have carried it up and down those stairs about four hundred times, and I have always thought it was lighter than it looks, and it turns out that is because I have never weighed anything in my life." He drank some of the tea. "They have read the schedule out twice on a landing and once in a stairwell to you, and I have been in this building for six years and I have never once heard a schedule read, and I have heard about them, and I did not know it was done out loud."

"It is done out loud."

"I am not complaining," said Eamon Brack. "I want to be clear that I am not complaining, because I know exactly what that sounds like coming from a man with two hundred and forty sessions in a book and nine of them used. I am telling you that in six years nobody has ever come up those stairs and said *these are the things I am taking* and *these are the things I am leaving*, and there is a door on this floor that nobody has knocked on in about four months, and I have not said a word to whoever is behind it, and this morning there were two officers in a corridor eight feet from it."

He put the cup on the step.

"I am not sorry," he said. "I have said that twice, to two people, and I am going to keep saying it, and you can put whichever of them you like in whatever you write."

"I know."

"Do you." He looked at the stairwell door. "Then say it back, because I would like to know whether the man who has a notebook is writing down that I am not sorry, or whether he is writing down something else and I am not going to be able to find out for about four years."

"I am writing down that you signed the schedule without reading it twice."

Eamon Brack looked at him for a while.

"I did not read it twice," he said, "because if I had read it twice I would have asked a question, and if I had asked a question they would have had to answer it, and then it would have been a conversation instead of a search, and I have wanted this to be a search since the eleventh of January about two o'clock in the afternoon, and you put a space in my working and I have not forgotten a minute of it."

---

There was a woman of fifty-eight standing in the doorway of the dry cleaner's at the bottom of the stairs with her coat on and a handbag on her arm, and she had come down when she heard the seals cut, and she had not come down at the reading, and nobody had sent for her.

Elias Venn had not seen her since the twelfth of January and had not knocked on her door since the fourth, and the bell on the corridor wall was a hand on a string and belonged to the dry cleaner's and he had not gone near it.

"You have got the bell," she said to the stairs, not to him.

"The bell is on a string and it has been on a string since before either of us."

"It is mine. I paid nine pounds for it in 2019 and it is on a string because a bell on a door downstairs goes off in the middle of the night and I have never once asked for it to be taken down and neither has anybody else in this building, and that is the most deliberate thing I have ever done." She put her handbag on the shelf beside the till. "I heard the seals. I have heard a door go before, in 2027, and I did not come down then, and I have thought about why I did not come down then, and it is that in 2027 the thing that was happening upstairs was not a search and it was not a room, and if it had been a room I would have come down."

"You have come down now."

"I have come down now because it was a room and it was a search and it was his room and not mine, and I have had nineteen years to work out that the difference between his door and mine is about eleven feet of corridor and a wall, and I would like somebody to write that down as the whole of what has happened in this building in six years, and I do not mean it unkindly. I mean it as a man who has been up a great many stairs."

Nadia Kest came down. She had her hat in her hand.

"Mrs Nesbit. I am not going to say anything about your room."

"I know you are not. I have known that since you stood at the top of those stairs on the eleventh of January and put your shoulder to a door instead of using the paper in your coat, and it is a week since somebody stood at the top of those stairs and nobody has come up them since." She did not raise her voice and she did not move away from the till. "**You have named his room and not mine. I know what that means. I have been in this building nineteen years and I know what an order that names one room means and I know what it does not reach, and I am not going to help you and I am not going to stand in your way, and I want both of those said in the same breath because if only one of them is said then one of us is a liar.**"

"Mrs Nesbit—"

"**There is a thing in a tin in my room and there are three of them and they are the only three that exist in the world, and I am going to tell you that it exists, and I am not going to tell you what it is, and I am not going to tell you on a Friday.**" She said it to Nadia Kest and not to Elias Venn. "If I tell you what it is, you will take it off me on a Friday, and you will put it in an envelope with a seal on it and a number on it, and it will be in a building by six o'clock and I will not see it again, and then on Tuesday night there will be a room with people in it and there will not be one piece of paper in the world that anybody can put in front of anybody else, and it will be gone, and I will have been the one who lost it."

"And on Wednesday?"

"On Wednesday you will need me to hand it over, and on Wednesday I will hand it over, and I would like you to hear me say the reason, because the reason is not that I trust you." She picked her handbag up. "The reason is that if it is taken off me then it is taken off me **at a time when I am the only person in this city who can put it in front of anybody.** And I am fifty-eight years old and I have a green book with about four hundred sessions in it and no names in it at all, and a thing in a tin, and I have had both of those for a very long time, and there is exactly one of me, and there is not going to be a second one on Tuesday night, and I have thought about that in a kitchen and I have come down here and said it out loud so that you cannot pretend later that a woman of fifty-eight did not tell a police inspector exactly what her tin is worth."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, and Nadia Kest said, "I will put that in a note in my own words and not in yours, and I will read it to you before I put it anywhere, and I will ring you on Tuesday at about four o'clock."

"You will ring me at four o'clock and I will be here, because it is a Tuesday, and if you do not ring me at four o'clock then I will be here anyway, because it is a Tuesday." She turned round to the till and then turned back. "And you will bring somebody who can carry it, because the last time somebody came up these stairs they brought nobody and I heard about it from a man in a jumper and it is the only thing anybody in this building has ever said to anybody about it."

---

Elias Venn was on a chair by his own counter at about ten to seven on Friday evening with the till behind him and a strip of job card in his hand and a mug of tea going cold, and he had a question he had been carrying since about half past two on Wednesday afternoon and it had nothing to do with the room and everything to do with a waiting room two streets away.

"Can I ask you something that isn't about today."

"You have just asked me something that isn't about today," said Nadia Kest, from the telephone, "and I have been waiting four days for it, because you have been extremely well behaved and it has been making me nervous."

"There is a sheet of paper on a board in a waiting room in Marshall Street that has been on that board since the twenty-seventh of November and it cannot be un-published, and there are four sheets under it in a doctor's ink, and there is a clean rectangle about nine inches by eleven where about ninety pages used to be." He put the job card down. "If somebody came into that waiting room tomorrow with a camera and stood in front of that wall and photographed it, in daylight, with nine people in the room and a doctor at the counter — is that a photograph?"

"That is a photograph of a wall."

"Is it a copy."

"No," said Nadia Kest. "It is not a copy of anything, because there is no original of a wall. It is the only instance of that wall in the world and anybody in a public waiting room may stand in front of it and look at it, and the only person in this city who has ever been told they may not photograph it is a doctor who has told herself that, and she was wrong, and I have not told her so because it is her wall and I am not going up there to be right about somebody else's furniture."

"That is not what I asked."

"Then ask me the thing you asked, because I have been on a telephone since Friday morning and I am about to go and eat something that is not in a bag."

"I asked whether the woman in the waiting room knows that anybody may photograph that board."

"She knows it because she is a doctor and she has worked in that building nineteen years and she has never once been asked to prove a thing on her own wall," said Nadia Kest. "That is the whole of it. It is not privileged and it is not protected and it has never needed to be, and in four months you have spent most of your time trying to get into rooms and the best thing anybody has offered you in this city is *that is a wall and anybody may look at it*."

She rang off. Elias Venn put the job card in the tray, and his hand went flat on the drawer under the counter, and he took it away and put it in his pocket and did not open the drawer again that night.

---

Kieran Slack was counting keys into a tin at about half past eight and did not look up when the bell on the shop door went.

"You have not been in today."

"No. I have been on a chair at the bottom of a set of stairs for two hours and eleven minutes and I could hear all of it and could not make out one word of it, and that is how it is supposed to be done, and I have been told so by two people who both know."

"I heard the seals," said Kieran Slack. "I have never heard a seal before. It sounds like somebody cutting a boot." He counted six keys onto his fingers and then put them down. "Is the room empty now."

"No. It is exactly as it was except for a book and a box and nine sheets of paper, and one of those nine is a page out of a method that is printed on a wall two streets from here and cannot be un-published, so nobody in this city has done anything to that sheet except carry it about."

"Right," said Kieran Slack, and put the tin away, and shut it, and then did not put his hand flat on it, which he noticed himself doing and mentioned. "Then it is not empty, is it. It is a room with nothing in it that anybody can be arrested for, and there is a difference and I have had four years of learning it."

He looked at the back room door, and at the stairs behind it, and then at the kettle on the counter behind the till.

"I am going to make them a cup of tea," he said. "There is a sergeant and a woman inspector and two constables and they have been in this building since ten o'clock and nobody has offered them anything, and there is a kettle in the back and there are mugs on the top shelf, and the last time anybody in this building was made a cup of tea in that room was about 2026 and it was me, and I did not go up, I took it to the door and put it on the floor and knocked and went down again."

He got the kettle.

"I have never been given a key," he said, "and I have never asked, and I have told two people that this month and both of you took notes, and I have thought about it for about a week and I have worked out that I am not frightened of him. I am frightened of being the fourth voice. I have been in this shop four years and I have heard four years of that room and I have never once said one word about it to anybody, and if I am up there with a tray of mugs then I am a man in the room, and there is no version of this where a man in the room is a man on the stairs."

He filled the kettle at the sink behind the till, and then he stopped with it in his hand and said, to the kettle, "It is only tea. Go on, then," and went up the back stairs of his own shop with four mugs on a tray, and Elias Venn stood in the middle of a parade and let him.
