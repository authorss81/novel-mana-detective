# Chapter 0051 — A Small Repair

There was a man standing in the lane outside number three in Tanners Row at twenty past nine on Saturday morning with his hand on the gate, and he was not a man in a very good coat, and he had been standing there since a quarter past nine because he did not want to knock while the kettle was boiling.

Elias Venn saw him from the corner and stopped, and worked out in about four seconds that he was looking at a seventy-eight-year-old man in a cardigan with a walking stick that was not the one he had lost, and that the stick was a shop stick with a rubber ferrule and a brass ferrule cap over the rubber, and that it had cost about six credits.

"Mr Venn," said Alfred Neve, before he was asked. "I want to tell you before I tell anybody else, because I have been thinking about it since Friday and I would rather you heard it from me than from the woman on the top floor."

"Go on."

"I have had a woman round. Not a nurse. A woman who does small repairs." He said the words the way a man says a thing he has checked twice. "I do not know what you call it. She came to the door on Friday afternoon in the rain and I said let her in, and she said she was not doing memory, she was doing small repairs, and I said go on then, because it was raining."

Elias Venn put his bag down on the wet brick by the gate.

"What did she do, Mr Neve."

"She asked me for a name." Alfred Neve looked at the rubber ferrule on the end of the shop stick. "I have known my sister since 1951 and I could not have told you her name on Friday morning. Not her first name. I could see her face, I could hear her voice, and the name would come up to about the front of my teeth and then go. And I have been asking people at the clinic for four years and everybody said the same thing, which is that it is not a thing anybody can do."

"And she did it in an afternoon."

"She did it in about twenty minutes and she charged me eleven credits and I paid her in coins out of the tin on the dresser, and it worked, and I want you to understand that it worked." He shifted the stick. "I could say her name. I can say it now, this minute, and I have said it about forty times since Friday because I keep wanting to check."

"That is not nothing."

"It is not nothing and it does not stay," said Alfred Neve. "It came back Friday evening and it was there Saturday morning and I have not tried it since, because I have a fair idea what happens if I do, and I would rather have it on a Tuesday and lose it by Thursday than have it all the time and lose it in a month. I am seventy-eight. I have already lost the only stick anybody ever made me."

Elias Venn stood in the lane in the rain and found that the sentence he had prepared for four days had nothing to do with this.

"Who sent you to her?"

"Nobody sent me. She came to the door." He said it, and then he stopped, and he had the look of a man who has arrived at a part of a thing he had not looked at. "Well. I gave her a cup of tea and I asked her where she had come from, and she said Concord Hall, of all places, and I said I did not know anybody at Concord Hall, and she said she did not either, she said she had been there when it was still called something else and had stopped going."

"And you told her about Mrs Achterberg."

"I told her about Hilda," said Alfred Neve. "Because Hilda has had a bad month with her sister's name and I have known that for two years and I have never once been able to do a single thing about it, and I am a man who gives things away, Mr Venn, that is my whole character, my wife used to say it and she was not wrong, and on Friday afternoon a woman who fixes names was standing in my kitchen and I could not think of a single reason not to say her name out loud."

"You are not the first man this month who has said a name out loud and found out what it cost."

"I am aware," said Alfred Neve, "and I want to say one thing about it before you say anything at all, because I have had all night to think about how this looks." He straightened up, and it took him a moment, and the moment was the bad knee on Thursdays and it was not Thursday. "That woman did not come to my door because of anything I did. I did not know her and she did not know me and she was not after my money, and if I had been twenty years younger and a harder man I would have shut the door on her and gone to the clinic on Friday and been told again that it is not a thing anybody can do. I am not a foolish man. I am an old man who is given things, and this is the first thing anybody has been able to give me since they took my stick."

"I believe you."

"Then do not write it down as a fault, because it is not one."

---

He made Elias Venn a cup of tea and then told him, with the air of a man reporting on a colleague, that the stick was a shop stick and had cost six credits and was a curse on sticks and would last fifteen years, and that he was going to get the shop to cut a new socket in it in the spring.

"What for?"

"To put something in," said Alfred Neve. "I do not know what yet. I liked having a place to put something. It is the only thing about the old one I liked that I could not tell you the use of."

Elias Venn drank the tea and did not say any of it, and at a quarter to eleven he went up Marshall Street and then up the stairs of 14 Kettle Rows to the second-floor landing, where he had to wait, because somebody was coming down.

She was small and she had her coat on over her cardigan and a cloth in her hand, and she took the second-floor landing at the speed of somebody who has done it for nine years and stopped apologising for it.

"Mrs Achterberg."

"You were the one who put the paper up," said Hilda Achterberg. "On the board. Behind the plastic. With her name under it."

"Yes."

"I read it four times. I have a niece who would have printed it and laminated it and I am seventy-four and I am not a fool." She did not stop on the landing. "You want to know about the woman."

"I want to know about the woman."

"Then come up, because it is not a thing to say on a stair."

---

The flat on the fourth floor had a chair by the window with a blanket over the back of it, a radio, a table with a cloth on it, and eleven cards in a box on the dresser with a rubber band round them that had been there since a wedding. The kitchen at the end of the hall had a gas meter with a tape round it that had a date on the tape.

Hilda Achterberg put the cloth down, which turned out to be the thing she did instead of sitting down.

"Nineteenth of October," she said. "That was the first one. I had a fortnight where I could not put a name to my sister's face and I had been to two doctors about it, and one of them said it was grief and one of them said it was the ward grid, and neither of them said anything that helped, and a woman came to the door on the Friday with a card in her hand and asked me what a street was for."

Elias Venn sat down on the chair with the blanket on it, because he was asked to.

"What did you tell her."

"I told her I could not put a name to Ada and I knew the room." Hilda Achterberg took her hands off the cloth. "And she asked me which room, and I said the front room at my mother's with the piano against the wall that was never tuned, and she said right, and then she was about forty minutes and she charged me eleven credits."

"And the name came back."

"The name came back and I could have told you the room too, and I could have told you what was on the piano, and I have had both of those since then and there is nothing wrong with either of them." She sat down at last, on the edge of a chair. "It lasts until the Wednesday. Then it is gone until the Friday and I go back down, and she says that is how it goes and that I should stop making a ceremony of it, and she is right, and I have been making a ceremony of it because it is the only thing that has worked on me in two years."

"How many times."

"Four. Nineteenth of October, the twenty-sixth, the second of November, and yesterday." She said the dates without hesitating, the way people say the dates of things that are the structure of their week. "I have told my son I had remembered it permanently, because what is the alternative, and he is in Cork and he is not going to come and hold my hand for four hours on a Friday."

Elias Venn looked at the eleven cards in the box on the dresser with the rubber band round them.

"You keep a note of what you have asked for."

"I keep a note of what I have asked for and what it cost and what I had back by the Wednesday," said Hilda Achterberg. "Because in six months somebody is going to tell me I have had my memory taken off me by a stranger and I am going to want to be able to say what, and for how much, and who was standing in the room. That is not being suspicious. That is being seventy-four."

"That is completely correct."

"It is not, actually, because it has never occurred to me until this minute and it has taken me about nine seconds, so do not be nice to me about it." She got up and went to the dresser and opened a tin, and the tin was a tin that had held a particular kind of biscuit for about thirty years, and inside it were four folded slips of paper. "That is what she gives me at the end. One slip, the date, the time, the number of the thing in her list, what I came for, and the fee. Four of them in a tin, and I have brought them to the front, and I have been keeping them since the first one."

"I would like to see them."

"I know you would, and that is why they are in the tin and not on the table." She put the lid back on it. "The last man who looked at my papers was a man from the council in March and he was very nice and he copied three things out of the little book I keep by the telephone and I never saw that book again and I did not get a copy and I did not ask, because I was afraid of being difficult."

"That is not being difficult. That is the only part of this conversation where anybody is in the wrong."

"I have worked that out. I have not worked out who to give them to." She put the tin down on the dresser and turned round. "You are not a policeman."

"I was one in September and I am not one now. There is a formal reprimand on me from Saturday week and I have not got a piece of paper that would let me take any of yours, and there is nobody in this building I could ring who would do it for me."

"Then why are you in my flat."

"Because a man in Tanners Row asked me to pass on a message this morning, and I came up four flights to do it, and then you were coming down the stairs."

Hilda Achterberg stood quite still for a moment, in a flat on the fourth floor of a building in Kettle Rows with a biscuit tin in her hand.

"What message."

"He said to tell you that there is a woman who comes on Fridays who fixes names, and that she is very good, and that the price is eleven credits, and that he is sorry to hear about the Wednesday."

"That is a message from a man of seventy-eight with a shop stick and a bad knee," said Hilda Achterberg, "and it is the most useful thing anybody has said to me since March, and you will go down and tell him I have been and I have been again, and I do not need the name because she gives it me every time and I write it down."

Then she sat down properly in the chair opposite, which took some doing, and looked at the tin on the dresser for a while.

"I am going to do something and I want you to be here for it, and you are not here for it, and that is exactly why I am going to do it in front of you." She got the tin again and took the lid off and put four folded slips, one at a time, into a second envelope, the kind that came with a shop's parcel, and wrote nothing on it. "You put four sheets of paper on a wall in that waiting room and you did not write one name on them, and I have known about that for two months, and my sister's name is on one of these slips and my own name is on two more, and I have read that paper often enough to know exactly what you did and why you did it and it cost you something, because I heard at the clinic on Monday that the officer who did it has been put on a form."

"I have."

"Then I am going to give you this envelope, and I am not going to ask you to promise me anything with it, because I have found out in seventy-four years that a man who promises is a man who is about to be asked. You may show it to somebody. You may not show it to anybody in this building who comes with a form. And if you keep it, then it is in a second place, and that is all I want, and it is all I have ever wanted, and I would like that on your notebook in your own handwriting and not in a way that says a frightened old lady sent it."

Elias Venn stood up and took the envelope, and it was lighter than eleven folded pieces of paper ought to be.

"I will write it in your words and not mine."

"Write it in mine," said Hilda Achterberg. "Mine are better."

He went down four flights at a quarter past twelve and out into Marshall Street with an envelope in his inside pocket that had no name on it and no address on it and no mark of any kind on it except a shop's parcel stamp with a price on it.

---

Otto Vahl had been at the front window of the shop with the wireless apart and a piece of solder in his hand for three weeks, waiting to be told something, and Elias Venn had not known that until he came down, and he had known since the twenty-seventh of October and had said nothing about it, and on Saturday afternoon in November he told him.

"There is no man in a good coat going round Kettle Rows fitting things to people's walking sticks."

Otto put the iron down.

"There is one," he said. "There was one. He came to the top flat's door on the Monday and he asked her whether the man with the walking sticks had been to her and she said she had not opened to anybody, and he asked her whether she had a friend on the same landing who had, and she said no, and he said that was all right and went away." Otto Vahl put the solder down next to the iron. "So I have been telling people, at the counter, since the twenty-eighth of October, that there is no man in a good coat and that if anybody comes to the door wanting to know about a man with a walking stick, the answer is no, and I will say it to their face, and I have said it to the face of one, and he was about thirty-five and he had a holdall."

"What did you say to him."

"I said the man with the walking sticks is a shopkeeper at number fourteen who mends kettles, and I said it out loud, and I said it to a man in a good coat who wanted a direction." Otto Vahl took his glasses off. "That is the reason I have not said anything to you, because it is the only thing I have done since the end of October and it is not a fact, it is a piece of work I have done, and I did not want to hand you a piece of work in place of a fact and have it in your notebook as though it were the second."

"You kept it from me for a fortnight."

"I kept it from you for a fortnight because you have not been in this shop on a weekday afternoon since the eighth of October," said Otto Vahl, "and because the man asked me a question and I gave him the wrong answer on purpose, and a man who has done a thing like that does not go and explain himself to a member of the public about it."

Elias Venn stood at the counter of his own repair shop in the middle of a Saturday afternoon and looked at a man of about sixty with a soldering iron and a cat asleep on the bench behind him.

"That was the right thing to do and you did it for the correct reason and you did it without a single person to tell you that you had," he said. "I want it in the book in those words, and I am writing it now while you are standing there, so do not start tidying up."

"You are not writing anything in that book about me."

"I am writing: *Otto Vahl gave a man in a good coat the wrong address on purpose on Monday 5 November, at the counter, having been asked it once, and told nobody for five days because he did not want it in a notebook as a fact when it was a thing he had done.* That is a sentence and it is true and it is about you and it is going in."

Otto Vahl picked the iron up and looked at it and put it down again.

"Then do it before the queue comes in at two o'clock," he said, "because there are two radios booked and one of them is a man who wants it done by Monday and has not brought it."

---

Elias Venn wrote it at the back bench with the door up and the rain coming under the yard side, and he wrote four lines and then stopped and added a fifth, and then he walked to the corner of Marshall Street with an envelope in his inside pocket and stopped there, because there was nothing in the whole of the previous sixty-five days that told him what a piece of paper about eleven credits a session was worth, and there was a man in a lane in Tanners Row who had started the whole thing on Friday afternoon by asking a stranger for a cup of tea, and the certificate on the shelf in the shop said that a man of seventy-eight had performed a complete dangerous working, and neither of those two facts had anything to do with each other at all, and both of them were true, and the week had not started.
