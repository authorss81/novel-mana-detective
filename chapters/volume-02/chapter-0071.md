# Chapter 0071 — The Address Column

Ahmed had a mop in one hand and his coat over his arm and he was standing in the corridor at the end of the waiting room with his back against the wall when Elias Venn came in, and he said, before Elias had got the door properly behind him, "You are the one who put the sheet up."

"That is a fair way to start a conversation."

"It is not a conversation, I have to be at the dispensary in four minutes." Ahmed did not move off the wall. "You are also the marshal, and I have not forgotten it, and I timed nine minutes on my watch the first time and I have thought about it more than I have thought about most things. So which one are you this morning, and I would like the answer before I put the bucket down."

Elias Venn put his hands where the woman on Marshall Street had taught him in November to put them, which was nowhere in particular and visible.

"I am a member of the public," he said. "I am not a clinician and I am not a police officer and I have been formally reprimanded for touching a thing I was not entitled to touch, in a treatment room, in October, and the finding was upheld a week ago and I lost the appeal. I am here because there is a piece of A4 on the board behind the polythene with your clinic's name on it and my name under yours, and because I have got nothing else to do this winter."

"Then you can go in the waiting room and you can stand at the board and you can look at it," said Ahmed, "and if anybody asks you what you are you tell them the same thing you have just told me, and do not say the word practice, because the lady will not thank you for it."

"I would not have used the word."

"You would have used it by ten past eleven." He put the bucket down. "I am covering until six. The day man has not come in and I have told Enid twice."

---

Doreen Tillery had been in the dispensary fourteen years and she had a way of standing that made the counter about a foot shorter than it was, and she listened to the whole of the first question before she decided how much of it she was going to answer.

Elias Venn put nothing on the counter. He had learned that in November, in a claims office on the Verge Row side, from a woman who had told him that a general question is free.

"What happens," he said, "when a patient's repeats come in and they are not collected?"

"They go on the shelf." She nodded at a wire rack behind her with about forty envelopes on it, and eleven of them had a paper clip bent double. "And after a certain time we send them back to the prescriber with a note, and after a certainer time than that the patient comes off the list and the prescriber writes to us and asks why."

"How long is a certain time?"

"Three weeks is the first one. Eleven weeks is the second one." She said both figures without hesitating, which told him she had been asked before. "And the note goes on the letter, and it says uncollected, and it does not say anything else, because anything else is a conversation about somebody's life and there is nowhere in this building to have that conversation."

"And if there is nobody at the address to take them?"

"We send them to the address."

"And if nobody takes them."

"They come back." She looked at him properly for the first time. "Are you writing this down?"

"No."

"Then why are you asking?"

"Because I am asking a general question and I have nothing in my hand," said Elias Venn, "and I would like to ask three of them, and you may refuse the fourth."

---

The third question was the one that took her nine seconds, and she spent them looking at the rack, and when she came back to him she had both hands on the counter.

"You are asking me about the people on the sheet," she said.

"I am asking you what you do about a patient who is not at an address at all."

"That is the same question with a hat on." She did not soften it and she did not harden it either. "There is a sheet. It is not a register and it is not in the book and it is not a list of anybody's business, it is a piece of paper in a drawer in the back office and it has got a list of names on it and a list of addresses, and the addresses are not the patients' addresses. That is all I am going to say about it and I have said it to a man I have known for four minutes because you are the one who put the sheet up and because the other half of this building has been talking about you since November and I would rather you heard the version from me."

"That is more than I expected."

"You are not getting the sheet." She said it flatly and without any pleasure in it. "And you are not getting the photographs and there is no camera in this room that anybody would let you near. This is a clinic and that is a clinic's cupboard and you are a member of the public, and a member of the public does not get a clinic's paperwork, and I have watched a man in a good coat be very good at a counter in this city and I am not doing it for him."

"I have not asked for the sheet."

"You asked for the sheet the moment you said *what happens to a patient who is not at an address*, and I would have said yes to the first three if you had not asked that one." She picked up a stack of boxes and put them down again. "Ask me a fourth one and I will answer it and it will be the last thing in this room today."

---

He asked the fourth one at twenty past twelve in a corridor at the end of the session list, and there were a nurse, two patients and a porter in it, and he asked it out loud on purpose.

It was not a question about a document.

"I want to say something in this corridor and I want it said where the four of you can hear it and where it is written down afterwards that I said it," said Elias Venn. "I do not read people. I have never read a person in my life and I am not going to start this afternoon. There is a thing I do that works on objects and paper and it has cost me a trade, a case and four samples since September, and if anybody in this room is worried that I have put a hand on them or on their prescription or on their name, they should know that I have not, and I am not going to, and there is a woman in this building who has known that since September and who told a sergeant the same thing in a corridor in November before I had thought of saying it myself."

The nurse looked at him for a moment and then said, "I know. She told us in the clinic meeting and she was quite emphatic."

"Then you have heard it twice and I would rather you had heard it from me," he said, "and that is the whole of the reason I am allowed to stand in this corridor."

Ahmed, at the end of the corridor with the mop, said nothing at all, and Elias Venn noticed that he had moved about nine feet further away, and did not say that either, because a man who narrates his own restraint is a man performing it.

---

The specimen cupboard is behind the dispensary, between the storeroom and the yard door, and it is a shallow press with two shelves and a hasp, and the key lives on a hook inside the dispensary on a loop of string, and Doreen Tillery opened it herself at twenty-five past one because he asked her a general question about what the clinic keeps.

"That is not a general question, that is a cupboard."

"It is a cupboard with a lock on it, and you have not said what is in it."

"I have said what is in it, which is nothing." She turned the key. "We keep the things people bring in. Nobody brings a specimen to a dispensary, that is the thing you have to understand about this building. What they bring in is paper, and paper is what we keep, and after about two years somebody decides it is not paper any more and it goes out to a skip, and the skip is behind the yard, and the yard is locked at six."

There were two shelves. The top one had a hundred and forty plastic boxes of the kind that come out of a printers' office. The bottom one had a cardboard box, and the box was about the size of a shoebox, and it had been folded down at the corners and it had four elastic bands round it, and the bands were new.

"What is that?"

"That is a box of forms that people have not come back for." She said it and then stopped, and Elias Venn heard her hear it. "It is not specimens, obviously. It is a bad name for it. It is the repeats-and-requests box."

"What is in it?"

"Eleven." She did not open it. "It has been eleven for about a fortnight. I know it is eleven because I count it on a Friday, because if it gets to twenty I have to say something to Dr Okafor and Dr Okafor has to say something to somebody, and eleven is not twenty."

"Are they the same as the forms you fill in?"

"No." She put her hand flat on the lid of the box. "The ones in the box are the ones nobody ever filled in. That is the whole of the difference. The ones I fill in are completed and go on the file. The ones in the box are half-started or not started at all and they are in there because the person said they would come back and did not come back, and in about four years somebody will say what do we do with them and somebody else will say throw them out, and I am the one who has to carry them to the yard, and I do not want to be the one who does that."

Elias Venn looked at the box for a while. He had a fair copy of a claim form in the back of his notebook in his own handwriting, written out by hand in a room in eleven minutes on the sixth of December with a gap where the claim number was and a gap where the certificate number was and no countersignature at the foot, and he had been carrying it about for a fortnight and had not had occasion to put it beside anything.

He did not say so. He said, "How long is the paper on a request?"

"About a minute to fill in if somebody is standing there and knows what is on it. Four or five minutes if they have to look for it." She was watching him now and she had decided something about him and it was not unfriendliness. "You are going to ask me whether the form in that box is the same form as the form on the payer's claim. And I am going to tell you that I have never seen a payer's claim and I have no opinion, and you can go and ask somebody who has."

"That was not the question I was going to ask."

"No," said Doreen Tillery. "It was not."

"Then I will tell you what I was going to ask and you can decide what to do with it," said Elias Venn. "I have got the other half of it. Not the paper — a piece of paper in my own handwriting that I wrote out in a room in eleven minutes in December with two gaps in it where two numbers go, and the numbers are not in my notebook, and there is no signature at the foot of it, and I wrote it because I was not allowed to have the sheet. And on my piece of paper there is a box for a place and a box for a number and a box for a date and a box for a code and a box for a fee, and the boxes are the same boxes, and the widths are the same, and there is a space above the fee box for a stamp, and there is nothing on either of them for a person."

He put his hand flat on the counter about a foot from hers and did not put anything on it.

"So I am not asking you whether it is the same form," he said. "I know whether it is the same form. I am telling you that I know, in a room with the key in it, and that I am not going to say it anywhere else, and that the reason I am telling you at all is that you have a box of eleven of them and no idea what they are."

Doreen Tillery did not move for about four seconds. Then she put the key back on its loop of string and hung it on the hook, and did it twice, because the first time her hands did not do it properly.

"You will not say it anywhere else," she said.

"I will not say it anywhere else."

"Then I will not ask you where you saw the other one, and I will not ask you whose it is, and you will not tell me." She took her hands off the counter. "But I am going to do a thing at half past three that I have never done in fourteen years, and I am doing it because a man has just told me the truth about a cupboard in my own building for free, and if I am wrong about it then I have lost a day and a half and if I am right about it then somebody in this building has been keeping half of something since before I came here."

---

The back office is a room with two desks and a wall of shelves and a window that looks at the wall of the next building, and Enid Marlow had been the clinic's ledger for nineteen years and had a thing on her desk that she did not offer.

"You will not see the sheet," she said.

"I have been told four times this morning and I have not asked for it once."

"You will see it," said Enid Marlow, "because I have read it out loud twice this morning to a man at the counter and a nurse and I am tired of saying it behind my hand, and because a thing that is said four times in a corridor by four people stops being a secret in about a fortnight and it is a shame to waste it. It is a sheet of paper. It is not the book. It does not go in the book, because the book is the book of the clinic and the clinic has patients in it, and this is not a patient thing, this is a *where do we send it* thing, and I have kept it in a drawer on its own for eleven years because that is where a *where do we send it* thing goes."

She took it out of the drawer. It was one side of A4, unruled, with a line down the left-hand side about a third of the way in, and it had been folded and unfolded so many times that it had gone soft along both of those folds, and it was written on one side only.

There were eleven names. He read them and did not write them down and did not say any of them aloud.

The column on the left was headed **name** and the column on the right was headed **send to**, and Enid Marlow was right that it was not a register and it was not a list of anybody's business, and it was also the only document he had seen since the seventeenth of September that told him where eleven people actually were.

"Send to," he said.

"Send to." She put her finger on the head of the column and did not go further down it. "That is all that column is. A house, a hostel, a sister, a sister-in-law, care of, a boarding house and a bed number. There is one name on here with a bed number in it and if I say it out loud in a room then the woman who owns that bed does not know that she is on a piece of paper in a clinic in Kettle Rows."

"How many of the eleven are at an address of their own?"

"None of them." She said it the way you say a thing you have said before and been surprised each time by how short it is. "Not one. In eleven years. I have put a patient's own address in that column about four times and three of those were a mistake and one of them was a woman who stayed in one place for six weeks and then left the city."

"Then how do they get their medication?"

"Somebody brings it. Or somebody collects it for them. Or it does not get collected and after eleven weeks it goes back to the doctor." She shut the drawer. "And I will tell you the part that is not in the drawer, because you have not asked for it and I am going to say it anyway. **Every one of those eleven has somebody who has been doing this for them for free, for months, and not one of them is paid, and not one of them is a relative, and not one of them has any idea that the arrangement has a name on it.** The minute the one who brings the medicine stops bringing it, that person stops having her medication. That is not a system anybody designed. That is what happens when eleven people have no address and there are eleven other people who are not unkind."

---

Then he was in the corridor at half past three, and Doreen Tillery came past him with the cardboard box under her arm and the elastic bands still round it, and Enid Marlow behind her with a ledger under her arm, and the two of them stopped at the yard door and counted it out loud together while he stood eleven feet away and was not invited closer.

"Eleven," said Doreen Tillery. "Nineteen to the third of December and eleven to the third quarter of December and it is eleven again, and I have written it on the lid in front of two people, and that is the first time in fourteen years that anything has ever been written on that box."

"Whose pen," said Enid Marlow.

"Mine." She put the box down on the yard step and wrote it. "And the count is a count of forms, and a count of forms is not a count of people, and I want that written down as well, because in about three weeks somebody is going to come in here and ask how many people this building is treating out of that box, and the answer is not eleven and the answer is not one either."

---

He was in the corridor again at four in the afternoon with his coat on and the waiting room emptying behind him, and Ahmed was at the dispensary counter doing a day's work with somebody else's till because the day man had still not come in, and the wire rack behind him had eleven envelopes with the clips bent double.

"You have been on your feet since ten past ten," Elias Venn said.

"I have been on my feet since six yesterday." Ahmed did not look up from the counting. "You have asked me a question a day for a fortnight and you have not asked me a single one about the people on that rack, and I have been waiting for you to, so I can tell you not to."

"You could have said that on Monday."

"I have had a bad weekend." He put a clip on the twelfth envelope and bent it double. "If you want to know one thing about that rack, I will give you one thing and it is the only one, and then you can go and open your shop."

"Go on."

"There is a name on the top one that is not the woman's." He pushed the rack back where it was. "It is a house. It has been a house for four months and the woman has been at that house four months and she is sixty-one and she is on that list under a name that is not hers because when the doctor asked her who she was staying with she said *I am staying nowhere* and the doctor wrote down the address of the friend who had put her bag down, and the friend never knew."

"And you will not say the name out loud."

"I will not say the name out loud in this building," said Ahmed, "because the woman who lives at that address does not know she is on it, and because you would write it down."
