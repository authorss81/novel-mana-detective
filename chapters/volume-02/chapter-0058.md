# Chapter 0058 — A Person, Not a Route

Wen Senn's launderette had a counter eleven feet long and a queue of two and a radio going and a smell of hot fabric, and she was sixty-six and had worked the counter for nineteen years and had never been in a police station, and when a police officer came into her shop to talk to her nephew she put a card on the counter, said *one moment*, and took a coat out of a machine, and did not look at either of them for a hundred and ten seconds, and when she looked up she said, "You have got until the queue goes and then I'm going to the back and you can talk to him in the back, and I am not being unkind, I have four machines going and I cannot leave them."

Then she went to the back, and shut the door to the shop, and left it open an inch.

---

Niko Senn had asked for it. That was the first thing he said and he said it in the doorway with his coat on.

"I wrote a letter to the station and I asked for you, and I asked for a woman, and I got you, and I want that said first because you are going to think I wanted a favour and I did not want a favour, I wanted a woman, and there is only going to be one of you in this building today."

"Say it."

He took a folded piece of paper out of his coat and put it on the counter and did not open it, and then he opened it.

It was printed on one side, and it was not a form, and it was not a letter, and Elias Venn read it standing up in about eleven seconds and then read it again.

> **PRIVATE PRACTICE — SESSIONS BY ARRANGEMENT**
> *Grounding and anchor. Two hours. Sixty credits, in advance.*
> *Sessions in the practitioner's room, or in the client's own premises where the client cannot travel.*
> *Please bring: something you have had for a long time and would not want to lose.*

Underneath, in ballpoint, in a hand that leaned to the right and pressed hard, somebody had written four lines, and there was a space at the foot of the fourth one with a word in capitals in it.

> *What is wrong / What is to be done / What it will cost / Where to leave the money*

And a time and a street: **Thursday, seven, the room above the shop.**

"He gave me that outside the free service on Tuesday night," said Niko Senn. "Not inside. Outside, on the step, where the leaflets are. And he knew my name. I have never told a man on a step my name. He said *you're the man from the sixteenth of September* and I said I don't know what that means and he said *I think you do*, and then he said the money, and then he said there was four people booked and one of them was going not turn up, and that the man who did not turn up on Thursday was going to owe it in December, and that I would be the man who did not turn up, and that it would be settled between us and not through a counter."

Elias Venn looked at the sheet.

"What did you say."

"I said no." Niko Senn put his hand flat on the counter beside the paper. "I said no and I said it out loud, on a step, at about twenty past eight in the evening, and I said it in a voice I have been practising since Wednesday, and he did not argue with me. He put the sheet in his hand and he said *sensible* and he went off up the street and I stood on the step for about four minutes with my heart going."

"And then you wrote a letter."

"And then I wrote a letter and I asked for a woman, and I have not told my aunt any of it and she is not going to be told any of it, and I want to say that to you and to nobody else: **she is not part of this.** You have got a name in there, Wen Senn, and you have not written it, and I have not written it, and if you write it down tonight I will read it, and I will not be angry, I will just know what you are, and that is all."

"It does not go in."

"Then say why, and say it in a way I can repeat to my aunt if I ever have to."

"Because you are not a route," said Elias Venn, "and you have said that to me in a shop in October and in a corridor at a police station in October and you were right both times, and I am not going to do it again in November because the case has got more interesting. **Wen Senn is a cost. She is not a door.**"

Niko Senn nodded at that and picked the sheet up off the counter and did not fold it and did not put it back in his coat.

"Now I am going to tell you the part I have not told anybody, including my aunt, including whoever comes on Thursday, and then you are going to do whatever you are going to do and I am going to go to work on Wednesday nights as usual."

---

"When he said *you're the man from the sixteenth of September* he was telling me he knows what I am," said Niko Senn. "I could tell that, because everybody's face does the thing when they get a person. But he did not say it to use it. He said it to **book me**. He said there was four people booked and one of them was going not turn up and the man who did not turn up on Thursday was going to owe it in December, and I have been carrying that sentence round since Tuesday like a stone in a shoe.

"And then on Wednesday night I got on the bus and I went to Halloway Street to a place that is a shop with a room over it, because I did not have to be told where the street was. I got the number off a card in my own coat pocket that I have carried since September and that nobody has ever asked me for, not you and not the woman at the free service and not anybody at the station. Six times I have been asked for that card by somebody. Six. And I have said no six times, and I have said no to you in this shop and you did not once make me feel like a thief about it, and I have thought about that every day."

He put his hand in his coat pocket and did not take anything out of it.

"So on Wednesday I stood outside that shop on Halloway Street and I did not go in, and I want you to understand that I did not go in **because I did not want to be the man who goes in.** Not because I was frightened. Because I have been the man who goes in since September and I have been on a card and in a list and in a certificate and in a room and every single time somebody went in and gave something up, the person who went in was me. And I am twenty-four in January and I have decided I would rather be a name in a document than a route, and I have said that to an inspector in a room with a witness and nobody argued, and I am saying it again here because you are the man who was on the step and you need to know what you were on the step with."

Elias Venn put both hands on the counter.

"What was the room over the shop."

"I did not go in. I stood in the street for eleven minutes and then I walked home the long way. I know the door and I know there is a bell on it and I know the light in the window was on at about a quarter to eight and I could see a table and two chairs and a lamp. **That is all I have got, and I have gone over it in my head for two days and it is a bell and a table and two chairs.**"

"Who was in the window."

"Two men. I could see two men and one of them had his back to me and one of them had a coat I did not like." Niko Senn said it and then stopped, and then said it again, slower, because he had heard what he had said. "I have been looking at a window for two days because a coat did not like me. I know what that is. That is being a route with a bad knee."

---

"You are not going to look for him," said Niko Senn.

It was not a question and Elias Venn did not answer it for about eleven seconds.

"No," he said.

"Right." Niko Senn put both hands flat on the counter. "I have thought about it since Wednesday and I have worked out that if you look for him then he finds out that somebody looked, and then he works out how he found out, and the only person who can work out how he found out is the one who told him a man was on a step. And there are about six people who know I was on a step, and five of them are in the police, and one of them is me. And the answer to that is not a name. The answer to that is that **you are looking for him, and I am the reason, and that is the shape I have been in since the seventeenth of September and I am asking for it to be different once.**

"You are not going to do it."

"I am not going to do it."

"I want to hear the reason in a sentence I can use on a bad Tuesday."

"Because I would be a man who uses a person to get a name," said Elias Venn, "and I have been that man four times since the seventeenth of September and one of the times it was me and three of the times it was me as well, and there is one of me and I cannot be replaced, and if I go under a bus the book in the shop goes with me, and that is not a reason to be careful, that is a reason to be frightened, and I have noticed the difference this week and I have not done anything about it yet."

Niko Senn laughed for the first time, once, in the back of a launderette with the door open an inch.

"That is the first honest thing anybody has said to me about being careful since September," he said, "and it is worse than the other version, and I am going to use that one."

---

Elias Venn asked him one question and it was the question he had decided in the bus at seven in the morning to ask and not the six he had written down and discarded.

"Was there a printed sheet. Anything at all. A page, a card, a bit of paper with writing on it that was not that."

"No."

"Are you certain."

"I am certain." Niko Senn said it flatly and then qualified it, which is the thing Elias Venn has taught him by example and did not mean to. "Certain about the paper. I have been carrying that card since September in an inside pocket and I know what is in it and what is not in it, because I have taken it out about four times to look at it, because I did not believe anybody was going to take it off me. There was no page. He had a card and a thing on a stick that looked like a pen and that was all, and he offered me the card to keep, and I said no."

"You said no to the card."

"I said no to the card and he said *sensible* and went off up the street." Niko Senn stopped, and it is the exact pause of a man who has arrived at something. "Mr Venn. He said *sensible* to me on Tuesday for saying no, and he said *sensible* to me on Wednesday for saying no to the card, and that is the same word, and I have been carrying the card for nine weeks and I have never once had a reason to keep it that I could say out loud to anybody including myself, and now I do, and it is that **two men have said the word to me and I have never said my own name to either of them.**"

Elias Venn took a breath that he had not planned to take.

"That is the whole of it," he said. "That is the only thing you are actually frightened of and it has nothing to do with me and I am going to be careful with it and I am not going to write it down."

"You write everything down."

"I write everything down and there is one thing in this case I have not written down since the tenth of October, and you know what it is because I told you what it was in October, and I am not going to write it down, and that is the whole of my system and it is one line long."

The machine at the back of the launderette came on and Wen Senn's voice came through the door an inch open, saying *Niko, the blue one is for the woman with the bad knee, not the grey.*

---

Elias Venn did not look for the man.

He said the reason out loud in the shop that afternoon, in his own words and not as a promise, to Otto Vahl, who had not asked, and to Jules Ardent, who came in at half past four with a chisel in his pocket for no reason anybody could establish and stood at the end of the front bench with his hands hanging, and said it twice, once to each of them, and wrote it in his own notebook in the small bad hand at the back bench with the drawer open and the cat in it.

> *22 November. Niko Senn asked for one thing today and I did it. I am not going to look for the man on the step in Halloway Street, and the reason is a person, and the reason is eight weeks old and it is that a card in my pocket makes a man a thing I am carrying, and the day I asked him for it I did not say why and now I have the reason and I still did not ask.*
> *The cost is a man we are not going to get. There is a room over a shop on Halloway Street with a bell on the door and two chairs and a table and a light on at a quarter to eight and two men in it, and Niko Senn has told me all of that from a street, and I have written none of it down on the right page and I have written it on the wrong page, which is the page where I put things I have got no right to hold, and that page has got four things on it and I have not read it since July.*
> *It is the correct price. That is the sentence and I am not going to argue with it in my own book.*

Then he asked Jules Ardent one question, which was about the outside of a door, and Jules came over to the bench and looked at the wall calendar in the back room with this month's Fridays ringed in ink, and said, "A bell on the door, a table, two chairs, a lamp, and a light on at a quarter to eight. That is a room somebody has paid for by the hour, because you do not pay for a room you have got all night, you pay for a room in a building where the other rooms are let out, and if the other rooms are let out then there is a man on the stairs, and that is the whole of what I know and it is the shape of a building and not of a crime."

"And the two men."

"One of them had a back to you and one of them did not, and you have told me about a coat and nothing else, and I would like to say something to you and then I would like to never say it again." Jules Ardent put his hand flat on the front bench. "In a workshop in Bay 6 I used to carry a canvas bag for men I never saw, and I was paid in cash, and every single one of them was in a good coat or in nothing at all, and I never once got a name, and I did not ask for one, and I have spent six years deciding whether not asking was cowardice or shopkeeping." He picked the chisel out of his pocket and put it down again. "It is shopkeeping. I have known that since the twenty-eighth of October and I have not told anybody. Now I have told a shopkeeper, and I am going to leave, and you are not to write it in the book."

"That goes in the book in your own words or not at all."

"I did not say that."

"You did not say it," said Elias Venn. "I am writing it because it is a thing a man said in my shop and if I do not write it down it only happened to me. **You are not a route, and neither is he, and the reason those two are in the same sentence is a fitter in a doorway in September and it is not a small thing.**"

Jules Ardent stood at the end of the bench and did not argue, and then he said, "Somebody has told you there is a man in Halloway Street and you have written his street down."

"He has not. He has written a street down and it is not that one and I have told him not to, and I am not going to tell you why either, and one of us is going to find out in about two years and I would rather it was the other one."

Jules Ardent put his hands in his pockets.

"Then I will tell you what I'll do," he said. "If a man in a good coat comes to this shop and asks for the man with the walking sticks, Otto will give him Otto, and I will be in the back room, and I will not come out, and I will not be the reason anything happens to anybody upstairs."
