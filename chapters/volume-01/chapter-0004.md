# Chapter 0004 — The Witness Who Was There

The room at Saint Orra Reach was booked as a family-observation room, which meant it had a bed rail that came down, a window with no handle, and a strip light that somebody in the estate office had set two shades too warm.

Mara Quill had taken it off the list at nine in the morning by telling the estate office, in the voice she used for people who were about to be unhelpful, that the next patient who was quietly stabilised into a false account in that room would be a police problem.

Elias sat down on the wrong side of the table.

"You keep doing that," Mara said.

"Which?"

"The ear. You sit so your good ear faces me, which is helpful right up until you lean in and I cannot hear you at all, which is when I am the person you most need to hear." She moved his chair. "There. Now you can hear me and I cannot hear you, and we are both poorer."

Niko Senn was twenty-four and had come because Mara had asked him, and he had made that condition clear in the first ninety seconds.

"I'm not being held," he said. "I asked for a lift. My aunt's coming at six. I go back on the bus at six-fifteen whether this is finished or not."

"Noted," Mara said, "and if anyone in this building suggests otherwise, you tell me and I will make it a problem for them."

She had also told him, before Elias came in, that the conversation would be written down and that he could stop at any moment, and that stopping would not be treated as a confession of anything. Elias had listened to that briefing from the corridor and had thought: she gives them the exit before they know they will want one.

He had not done that. That was the whole difference between the two of them, and it took him until about the fortieth minute to understand that it was the whole difference.

"Start wherever you like," Elias said. "I have a form's worth of questions and no intention of using it in order."

The account came in pieces, and the pieces did not fit, and Niko Senn was better than Elias expected at knowing that.

He worked nights. Coin laundry, four nights, on Verge Row, a ten-minute walk from his room. He had taken the Orra line in on the last inbound of the Sunday night and the first outbound at ten past six on the Monday, which put him in Kettle Rows at about ten to one in the morning, walking up Tanager Street with a paper bag of a pastry he never ate.

"I've been going to her door since October," he said. "She's — we're not a thing. We're a queue. You stand in a queue for four years and you're not a thing, you're a queue."

"How long in October?"

"Since she moved. Four years." He turned the paper bag over in his hands. "Her door does the thing. You put your hand on and it knows you. And when you're on the list it goes quicker, and when you're not on the list you have to wait, and there's a board."

"And last Monday, you were on the list."

"Last Monday, I was on the list. Third from the top. I got there at twelve minutes past midnight."

Elias kept his hands flat on the table, well away from the other man's, and made himself say the next thing in the order in which it should be said rather than the order in which he wanted to hear it.

"Take me through the landing. From the stair."

Niko closed his eyes, and his hands stopped moving the bag, and Elias felt the small professional disappointment of a man who has asked a question and got the start of an answer he was not going to be allowed to press.

"I come up. The light's late, the way it's always late, and I thought, that's new." A half-laugh. "I put my hand on the frame. It opened. She was — she was on the far side of the desk, and she said something, and then—"

He stopped.

"And then?"

"And then the mug went over."

"There was no mug break," Mara said, gently. "The mug was on the floor, two metres from the table, unbroken. It had been struck, not dropped."

"I know. I know it didn't break. I'm not saying it broke." Niko opened his eyes. "I'm saying it went over, and it was the first thing after the door. And then the door did the thing — the little click it does when it knows you — and then I was in the room."

"Which was first," Elias said, "the mug or the click."

"The mug."

"Which was first in real time?"

"The mug." He said it with more force, and then, unexpectedly, less. "That's what happened. That's the order. I heard her and then the mug and then the door and then I was in the room."

Elias wrote nothing down, because there was nothing down to write. On the desk swab there was an order with no such sequence in it, and the sequence on the desk did not have a mug in it anywhere, and a door ward that opens after a person has already been in a room is not a door ward doing its job.

He felt the old hot pressure behind the eyes that came with a case turning over.

"Niko." He leaned in. "Look at me. If you could be mistaken about the order, just say you could be mistaken. People get orders wrong all the time under stress. I would rather have a rough account than a clean one that fits what I need. If it went mug, click, room, then I would write mug, click, room, and then we would go and look for what made the mug go over two metres without breaking it. Do you understand me? A rough account is worth more to me."

"Yes." Niko was looking at him. "Detective, I understand you perfectly. That is not the problem."

"Then what is."

"That I was not confused," Niko Senn said, "because I did it before."

The strip light hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a door was being wheeled.

"Before," Elias said.

"Three weeks ago. Maybe four. There's a place, off the Verge Row side, second floor above a tax office, and you go up because there is a card and you give the card." He was careful now, and he was also going very fast, the way people do when they have been holding a thing for a month and have been waiting for the one question that means somebody is listening. "He says he's a practitioner, and he's got — he has a white coat, but it's a smock, and it isn't Concord because Concord is paper first and they take a card and copy it and there's always somebody else in the room. He's on his own. It's forty credits and he does grounding. For anxiety."

"Grounding," Mara said.

"Grounding," said Niko. "He says it helps you sleep. He says I'm not sleeping, and it's because I run rooms at night and my head doesn't know the day has started. He puts his hand flat on a table and tells me to do the same, and then he says: now imagine somebody is in the room with you, and wait for a tap on the table, and when you hear the tap, breathe out and say her name."

Elias looked at Mara.

"Four times," Niko said. "He did it four times. Sometimes with his fingers on the back of my neck, and he said it was to help me feel the floor. And it was — it worked. It worked, it was the only four nights I slept." He put the bag down. "And then in her room, I put my hand on the door, and I was already inside it. I was already four lines in. I heard the door and I was already breathing out, and I said her name, and then the mug went over, and then she was on the floor and I was on the step and I do not remember getting to the step."

He stopped. He was shaking, which he seemed to have discovered only now, and he put his hands flat on the table in exactly the way a man in a smock had taught him three weeks ago, and then took them off again as though the table had bitten him.

Elias felt the floor tilt under a floor that had not moved at all.

He said, very carefully, "Niko, I need you to understand that this is not the same as what happened to you. Rehearsing an order does not hurt anybody. It cannot. It is not a thing that can break a mug or put a woman on the floor. It changes what you expect, and that is all."

"I know what it is," Niko said. "You don't think I haven't worked it out? I've been three weeks working it out at four in the morning. He made me practise a door. That's what I did. I practised a door and then a woman I was going to see was standing next to the thing I'd been made to practise, and she died."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Elias looked at Mara and understood that he had just watched a man be more careful with evidence than he was.

Mara spoke first.

"That is a very good distinction and I want it on the record, because most people never make it and the ones who need to hear it never will." She turned to him. "Niko, the rehearsal did not kill her. The rehearsal made you unable to tell the difference between a thing happening and a thing you were told to expect. Whoever came into that room with a working did the harm. The man in the smock made you unable to testify about it, and that is a different crime and it is a real one and it is prosecutable."

"Is it?"

"Yes."

Niko's mouth went crooked. "The card."

"What about it?"

"It was a card. I could have photographed it. I didn't." He said it without any self-pity at all, which made it worse. "I wanted to sleep."

Elias understood, in a way that was going to be with him for a long time, that if he had been twenty-four and running rooms at night and somebody had offered him four nights of sleep, he would have had the same card in the same pocket and done the same thing with it.

"You did not do anything wrong," he said.

"I did a wrong thing to her."

"You did a wrong thing to yourself, and somebody else did the other thing to her." Elias heard the hardness come into his own voice and heard it land, and took it back. "Niko. Listen to me properly. Whatever happened in that room, it is not going to be written down as a thing you did. Not by me, not on this file, and I have spent a large part of the morning making sure of exactly that. I have put in a file today that I live in that building, because I would rather be the man who wrote it than the man somebody found out later."

Niko looked at him.

"You live there?"

"Eleven years. Two floors down. I signed her tenancy transfer in 2019."

"It was a waiting list," Niko said, "and you were on it. Yes."

Elias's phone went in his pocket. He had a question ready, a good one, the best question he had: if the man in the smock had the tool in his hand at the end of the session, then the man in the smock had come to her room that night, and the man in the smock had a name, and the name would take eleven minutes to find. He had it formed. He was already reaching for the next sentence.

"Detective." Mara's voice. "Stop."

"I'm not—"

"You were about to read the inside of his head at him. I watched you build it." Mara did not raise her voice. "You have been in this room twenty-two minutes and you have never once suggested to him that what he just told you might be a rehearsed account, and you have also never once suggested that what he told you might be the truth that a man was made to forget. You have been waiting for a contradiction and using him as a device to produce one."

"I was going to ask a neutral question."

"You were going to ask whether the man in the smock was tall," said Mara. "Which is not neutral. It is a question whose only possible answer helps you, which is why he will say yes, and in four months' time somebody with better interests will read that and it will be the weakest line in a man's statement."

Elias took his hand off the table. It had been resting four inches from Niko Senn's knuckles, and he had not been thinking about it at all, and that was the answer to a question he had not asked himself.

"Stop the interview," Mara said.

"That's not—"

"I am stopping it. Detective, stop it."

He stopped it.

"Niko." He made himself say it slowly. "From this point I am not going to write anything down. Not in the file, not in my notebook, not on a form. Nothing that happens next is evidence unless you later decide that it is. I want you to know that before you decide whether to say anything."

"Will that hold?"

"Unless you make it evidence, yes."

"Even if somebody upstairs asks you for a version?"

"Especially then. I will tell them I have no version. I have done that before and I am extremely good at it."

Niko Senn looked at the table for a while. Then he said, "There is a thing the Registry said they could do. The woman on the phone last night. She said they could stabilise an account so it doesn't drift, and the account would be signed and it would be stronger than mine, and that the tribunal would take it more seriously than a scared man changing his mind."

Elias felt the temperature of the room go down without any change in the air.

"What is a stabilised account?"

"A witness-binding," said Mara. "They align a person's inner trace to the residue. The account stops being something they remember and becomes something that is held. It is a genuine technique. It is what the Concord does when a witness has to testify about something enormous, and it is very good, and it does not lie. What it does is take the account out of the witness's own hands. Whether that is forever, I do not know. Nobody will tell you, and asking is how you find out that they already have."

Niko said, "That sounded like a good thing."

"It is a good thing," Mara agreed. "Used on a witness who is telling you what happened to them. It is not something to do to a witness who has been handed an order by somebody else, because it would fix the order in, permanently, in glass, with a name on it, and it would be fixed in by this office, on this form, today. And then in four years a defence lawyer would say: how do we know that order was not put there? And the answer would be: we cannot know, because we set it."

"I would never agree to it," Niko said, after a moment.

"No. Nobody can agree to it, honestly, which is why the offer is always made as a kindness and accepted as a service." Mara turned to Elias. "Write on the file that the offer was made and refused and that the refusal is the witness's own and was not prompted by a question from this desk. Please do it in that order, because the order is the evidence."

Elias wrote it. He wrote it exactly as she had said it, including the last sentence, and he did not editorialise, and it took him a deliberate act of will not to.

Niko Senn put his face in his hands for a moment, and then, when he came out of it, he was a different person, and the difference was that he had decided to be in the room.

"Now tell me the rest," Elias said. "Whichever parts you want to, in whatever order they come, and I am not writing any of it down."

"While he was doing the grounding, there was a tool on the table," Niko said. "A brass thing, about the length of my hand, with a jaw at one end and a screw at the other. It wasn't a normal tool. It had a channel cut into the jaw, like a groove, and it was polished, and it had been used a lot, and there was a number stamped into the side of the barrel." He closed his eyes. "I read it because there was nothing else in that room to read. It was the only thing I'd looked at for three weeks. It was JA, hyphen, four four one seven."

Elias wrote it down. He knew he had just agreed not to, and he did it anyway, because a repair number is a thing you look up and not a thing a man's account depends on, and because he was still, on the old evidence, a man who could not let a number leave the room in his head.

"And the man in the smock used it," Niko said. "He picked it up once, at the end, and he put it in a bag, and when I left he was still holding it. I remember the number because I was looking at it and he was looking at me."

Elias put the pen down, and said, "Thank you," and meant the whole of it, which was the part he did not trust.
