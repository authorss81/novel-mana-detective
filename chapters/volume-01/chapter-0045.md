# Chapter 0045 — The Plan, Read Aloud

Otto Vahl read the plan on the back bench over the shoulder of the Cheetham job, holding it about eight inches off the page because he needs the reading glasses he does not wear at the front counter, and he got to the fourth line and put his thumb on it.

"No," he said.

"It is a man on a door."

"It is a man on a door from ten to half past four on a Tuesday afternoon in October." Otto took the glasses off and cleaned them on his sleeve, which he did about three times a day and never when there was nothing to clean. "Ellie, I have a radio bench in that window and a cat and a wireless that is going to want doing at eleven, and I am not standing on the step of the clinic for five hours in a wet coat pretending I am part of it. That is not me being difficult. That is me telling you what a Tuesday is."

"I was not going to ask you to stand anywhere."

"You wrote *a man in the front window, off duty, not in uniform, with no radio on*." Otto put the glasses back on. "You wrote me into your own plan and you did not ask me, and if I turn up on that step on Tuesday then every person on that street knows the shop is part of whatever this is, and my landlord has a ward certificate in March and a dangerous-working anything at all ends it, and you know that, because it is the reason there is a case."

Elias Venn took the sheet off him and looked at the fourth line, which was in his own small bad hand.

"Take it out," he said. "I will take it out."

"You will take it out, and the plan will have three people in it, and the three people will be a police inspector, a mechanic and a member of the public." Otto went back to the wireless. "That is a Sunday. That is me on a Sunday in the spring, and I will do it, and I am not doing it on a Tuesday in a wet coat in front of forty-one people."

"Then I will find somebody else or I will find nobody," said Elias, and wrote *four lines struck out* beside line four with the time, 08:20, because Mara Quill's sheet said that things that are taken out get written down with the time they were taken out.

---

Detective Inspector Nadia Kest came to Marshall Street at nine on a Monday because he had asked her to and she had said she would, and she came in out of the rain in a coat that had been to about four of these things before, and she would not sit down.

"Say the plan," she said. "Not the theory. The plan. Who does what, in what order, with what, and what happens if it goes wrong. And if you say *we will stop it* I am walking back out to the car."

"Fine." He turned the sheet round. "There is a session at the clinic on Tuesday afternoon, thirteen hundred to sixteen hundred, forty-three booked, forty-one counted by Ahmed this morning, and forty-one is Dr Okafor's own number and she has counted it twice. Slot three is fourteen thirty-eight and slot three is Alfred Neve of Tanners Row, seventy-eight, and a release carried in a sleeve in his walking stick is seated and waiting, and Mara Quill says it completes when a record is written about him, and the record that gets written about him is an injection at fourteen thirty-eight. I am not certain of that. I am about seventy per cent certain of it, and Mara is about the same, and neither of us can raise it, because neither of us will put a hand on it."

"Go on."

"Nadia, I cannot interrupt a working. I have told you that since September and I am telling you it again in a room with no witnesses, so that if you write down a plan that depends on me doing it, you will know before you write it that I have told you twice that I cannot do it."

"That is not a plan," said Nadia Kest. "That is a confession with a schedule attached."

"It is a plan for a thing that is not stopping a working," said Elias. "It is a plan to be in a corridor with a tin when a working stops on its own because there is no longer any metal in a channel."

She looked at the sheet for a while. The clinic's radiators were going somewhere behind the wall and the cat was on the blanket on the radio bench and neither of them was looking at either.

"Go through it again," she said. "Slowly, and start with what is written down."

---

They did it in the corridor at Morrow Hill at eleven, with Detective Sergeant Paul Rennick, who holds 441-KR and who wrote it all down with the times in it and read it back before he signed it.

**PLAN — 29 OCT 2029, 11:20, read aloud in the Sequence Desk corridor, minuted by D/Sgt P. Rennick, file 441-KR.**

*Object: one walking stick, ash shaft, licensed threshold bracket, sleeve bonded over bracket. Owner: A. Neve, 78, 3 Tanners Row, Kettle Rows. Owner's written consent to three named persons held.*

*1. The sleeve is to be removed from the stick by mechanical means, in the presence of the owner, by a mechanic, and put into a sealed tin. Estimate nine seconds.*
*2. Officer in attendance: D/I N. Kest, attending, with authority in respect of the owner's property. The item is transferred, not seized. The owner's consent is the authority.*
*3. The order of the working is to be read by D/Sgt E. Venn, member of the public, by contact with the object, in the presence of the attending officer and the mechanic, and the reading is to be reported by the officer, whether or not the officer has understood it.*
*4. No person is to be read. No living person's trace is to be entered. The mechanic's work is mechanical and is not to be described otherwise.*
*5. If a working is in progress at 14:45 on 30 Oct, the room is to be cleared by the attending clinician and not by police, and the item is to be removed in a corridor and not in a treatment room.*

Rennick stopped writing at number three.

"No," he said.

"Paul—"

"That is the fourth examination of a physical object in this matter by an officer who has been censured for the third of them, and you have written it down in a plan that I am about to sign, and if I sign that, then in nine days a Deputy Chief will read a plan with my name at the bottom of it and a sentence in it that you told her in a corridor." He put the pen down. "You are not an officer. You cannot be a witness to a police act because you are the actor. If you put your hand on that stick in front of me, there is no witness, and the prohibition you filed on the twenty-ninth of September says there has to be one, and I am not going to be the reason there isn't."

"That means nobody reads it," said Elias Venn.

"Yes."

"Then nobody knows what is in the sleeve when it comes out."

"Then you will be a man standing in a corridor knowing less than everybody," said Rennick, "which is what you have been since the fifth of October."

He wrote underneath number three, in a different hand of his own: *Item 3 amended. D/Sgt Venn is a member of the public and not a witness to this plan. The prohibition filed 29 Sept 2029 is not varied by this plan. No examination is planned.*

And then, after a moment, he took the pen up again and wrote one more line under it, and read it out before he wrote it, which is what he does.

*If an examination is nonetheless made, it is a breach, it is fourth in this matter, and the officer will record it as one at the time and not afterwards.*

"That is me volunteering to be the man who writes it down," he said. "I would like that understood. I am not agreeing to it. I am agreeing to the cost of it if you do it."

---

Nadia Kest read the plan twice on her own and then sat down on the bench in the corridor, which she had not done in a corridor in front of him before, and said the two things that were hers to say.

"The first is that I will not put this department behind a plan written by a member of the public in his own bad handwriting. So it is going to be rewritten. Not better — rewritten, in the department's words, on department paper, with my name on it, and every line of it something I have agreed to. When that document is finished, Detective Sergeant Rennick and I will both have signed a plan for Tuesday afternoon that says a mechanic will take a piece of metal out of an old man's walking stick in a clinic in Kettle Rows, and if it goes wrong the two names on it will be mine and his. That is the cost. Do you understand the cost?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"Two names on it will be yours and Rennick's."

"The second thing." She did not soften it. "On Monday you are going to take that stick, or have somebody take it, and photograph it in place, and put it back. Not a photograph of the sleeve. A photograph of the stick as it is, on his table, in his kitchen, camera moved, nothing moved, and a note of the time. Because on Tuesday there is going to be a tin, and a tin with a number on it, and if somebody in a corridor afterwards asks you what the stick looked like on Monday, you are going to want a photograph and a time and not a memory. That is not evidence of a crime. It is evidence of a state of mind. It is the only kind any of us can actually get."

Jules Ardent, who had been standing at the end of the corridor with his hands in his pockets for half an hour like a man waiting for a bus he did not want to get, said, "It will take nine seconds."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I have been taking ferrule rivets out for nineteen years and I have never once done it in a hurry and I have done it four thousand times." He came and looked at the plan over Nadia Kest's shoulder. "A punch, a drift, four taps, and the head comes up, and the ferrule comes off, and the sleeve is a loose press fit in a socket and it lifts out with two fingers. Nine seconds. It is not difficult. It is the least difficult thing anybody in this corridor will ever do."

"And if the room is full of people?"

"Then I am a man helping an old man with his walking stick," said Jules Ardent, "and if the old man is sitting in a chair and I am on one knee in front of him with a chisel, that is what it looks like from forty feet, and from ten feet it looks like nothing at all, and that is the entire reason a mechanic is the right man to do this and a policeman is not."

---

He went to the clinic at ten past three on the Monday afternoon and told Dr Okafor the whole of it in about four minutes, standing at the dispensary counter, in a corridor, with a nurse going past.

She listened to all of it and said, "The session does not move."

"I am not asking for it to move."

"You are asking me for Tuesday afternoon to be quiet, and I want you to know that I have been asked for that by somebody else in the last month and I said no, and I am saying no to you in a nicer coat." She put the marker down. "There are forty-three on that list and two of them are seventy-nine and one is eighty-one and they have had this every year since they were old enough to be asked and the whole district comes for it and the only reason there is a session is that we can only afford one. If I move it, forty-one people are told to come back another day in November and about nine of them will not, and two of them will be in a different state of health in November, and I will not do it for a walking stick."

"Nobody is asking you to move it."

"Then here is what I will do." She picked the marker up. "You may have treatment room two, which is the one with the couch and the screen and no window, and it is off the main room through a door that shuts, and on Tuesday afternoon the third slot is in there, and it is in there because room two is where the old ones go and because room two has a door. That is how this building works and it is not in anybody's plan. And I will clear the main room myself if I need to, and not the police, and not a man with a list. And you will tell me at eight o'clock on Tuesday morning what is going to happen, and you will not tell me at two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, because I am not a person who can be useful at two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon and I will only get in the way."

"Eight o'clock," said Elias Venn. "I will tell you at eight o'clock."

"And you will tell me it whether you have thought of anything or not, including the parts you think sound like an excuse," said Dr Okafor. "That is what makes a person who rings at eight usable. Not the news. The fact that she rang at eight."

---

At twenty-five to five, on the ground floor at Morrow Hill, in the corridor by the lifts, Rennick was met by a clerk from the Registry index office, who was on her way to a lift and had a slip in her hand.

"Sergeant Rennick — 441-KR?"

"Yes."

"There's been a file opened against it." She said it the way the index office says things, which is with the time first and the reason never, because the reason is not hers. "Reference 441-KR/6. Opened at nine twenty this morning. A sequence certification, in the ordinary course. There's nothing odd about it and I'm not implying there is."

"Twenty-eight of October?"

"No," said the clerk. "This morning. Today. Nine twenty, and I have written it down twice because it is the third one this month and I do not like the third one." She held the slip out and then did not let go of it. "I can tell you the reference, the time, the class of the application and the office it is going to. I cannot tell you what is in it, because it is not compiled, and I am not going to guess at an empty file at ten to five on a Monday."

"Take it," said Rennick.

Elias Venn was standing four feet away with a sheet of paper in his hand that had four lines struck out of it beside line four with the time against the striking, and he did not come closer, because he was not on the file and there were four people at the lifts.

The clerk said, "Reference 441-KR/6, class: sequence certification, Proof Registry, first schedule. Opened Monday twenty-ninth of October, oh nine hundred twenty, by the index office on a request received the previous Friday. That is all I have and that is all I am permitted to have."

"Thank you," said Rennick.

He wrote it into his own book with the time, and the clerk went to the lift, and the corridor went back to being a corridor, and the two of them stood in it.

"Previous Friday," said Rennick. "The twenty-seventh."

"The day after Mara's figure was in the ledger," said Elias Venn.

"I am not going to say what a sequence certification is opened for," said Rennick, "because I have held this file for four weeks and every single thing that has gone wrong in it has gone wrong because somebody filled in a field eleven on a form on a Friday afternoon and did not ask who would read it in September." He closed the book. "And I am not going to say it to you either, and I know what it looks like when I say that, and you can put it in your notebook as a thing an officer declined to say. I would rather have that on the page than have it in your head. Write it down as a refusal, Elias. They are worth more than the answers and I have spent four weeks finding that out."

Elias Venn wrote it down as a refusal, at twenty-six to five, on the twenty-ninth of October, on the ground floor of a police building on a wet Monday, and closed the notebook, and went down in the lift to a repair shop that has no trade account and a drawer that will not latch, and did not sleep much, and did not dream about the number nine twenty at all, which he noticed, and wrote down.
