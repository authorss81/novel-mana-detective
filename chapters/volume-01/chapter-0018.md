# Chapter 0018 — The Supplier

The register of licensed ward practitioners is a public document and it is kept on the ground floor of Concord Hall on the Quay, in a room with a counter and a queue, and it is public because a licensed man is supposed to be findable.

Elias Venn got there at ten past ten on Friday morning and stood in a queue for fifty minutes, and the woman at the counter was very good at the book.

"Class of registration," she said. "I cannot search by name alone, I have to have a class or an address, and if you have neither I can tell you that a man of that name holds a registration and nothing else, which is most of what people want."

"Supply."

"Supply is a class," said the woman at the counter. "There are four. Clinical practice, ward works, residue examination, and supply. Supply is the one that got added in about 2016 because half the men who were turning up in the frame cases had turned up in the ward works list first and were being described by their customers as ward works men, and the register could not stand it."

Elias Venn gave her the name and the class.

She turned the pages for about four seconds, which is a long time in a public register.

"Orin Sable. Registered on the fourth of March, 2019, class supply, premises 2nd floor, Carrow Lane, above a printer's. Current. Renewed on the fourth of March this year. Endorsements: two. One is supply of ward components within a licensed premise. The other is—" She stopped and read the endorsement twice, which is not what a woman at a counter does when she is bored. "The other is a restriction. Class supply, restricted from carrying out any dangerous working, any working affecting a person, and any attendance at a scene where a working is in progress. That endorsement is dated March 2019 as well, so it is not an investigation, it is part of the original registration."

"Read it to me again, please, exactly."

She did.

There was a person at the counter in front of him who had arrived before him and who was about forty and who had been in a very good coat for a very long time, and who had not been reading a newspaper or a phone or anything at all for four seconds, and who then said, "Thank you, that's all," and went out, at a speed that was not fast and was not slow.

Elias did not follow him, and it took him about a second and a half to work out why not, and the reason was that a man who has just been told that a registration exists does not have to know where the person registered lives in order to know that he exists, and the reason he did not follow was that the only thing he was permitted to do this morning was write four words on a piece of paper.

He wrote them in the queue, standing up, on the back of a form.

*ORIN SABLE. SUPPLIER OF FRAMES.*

And then he stopped, and looked at it, and did not add anything.

---

He did not add anything because everything he wanted to add was not supported.

He could not say that Orin Sable had cut the nine inches of galvanised conduit that had been on a clinic ceiling two floors under a man of twenty-four with a fever of thirty-seven eight and a rash and a clinical hold. He could not say that he had fabricated the channel, or seated the foil, or built the clamp jaw in Jules Ardent's bench room four years ago. What he had was a die profile cut twice at a shop on Ash Street, once to a licensed maker on Pell Street and once in August to a post box with a trading name on it; a fortnight of invoices in August and September for forty sheets of four-tenth foil, sixty lengths of galvanised conduit and a gross of channel section, sold for cash and collected by a courier; a licensed practitioner on a public register whose premises are a room above a printer's on Carrow Lane; and a licence class that says, in the plainest words the Concord has ever put on a public page, *you may supply ward components to other people and you may not go and use them on anybody.*

That last one was not a piece of the case. That last one was the case, and it was the reason there was a case.

He went and said so to Nadia Kest at twenty past one, in the office, with the board written on, and she read the four words and then read the form behind them and then sat back.

"You have got a supplier," she said.

"I have got a name on a public register in a supply class and a fortnight of cash invoices. I have got a die profile that a shop owner will not give me and an attendance code with no name on it. If I take this to a magistrate on Monday I get a sifting interview and I lose the rest of it, because a sifting interview is where a case like this goes to be described as a coincidence by somebody who is going to be right."

"Agreed." Nadia wrote on the pad. "So what goes in."

"Four words and the limitation underneath them. That is what goes in." He pointed at the form. "I cannot put the nine inches on an invoice for conduit. Metal is metal. What I can put on it is a profile, a trading name, a class of licence and a fortnight, and if I write it up as anything else then the first defence counsel who reads it will find the flaw in about four minutes and everything else I have will be read afterwards in that light."

"Write the limitation in your own hand and not in mine."

"That is what I have learned from a woman on a bench with a four year old leaflet," said Elias, "and I would rather say so than have you find it in the file in a fortnight."

Nadia Kest read the four words once more and then said, "You have written *supplier of frames*. Not maker. Not installer. Not operator."

"No."

"Because he is a supplier and because that is the whole of the network, and the moment you write a bigger word on that line a defence lawyer will take the whole line out, and I want to be very sure you have not written a bigger word on that line."

"Supplier of frames," said Elias Venn. "And the file can say that on the same day that a man in a very good coat stood at a counter in this city and found out that I had found a name, and walked out without hurrying, and that I did not follow him, and that both of those are true and only one of them is the reason I did not."

---

Jules Ardent's solicitor had rooms above a tobacconist's in the outer wards with a fan heater and a window that did not open, and Jules Ardent was sitting in them with his hands on his knees in a way that a man sits when he has been sitting for four hours, and he had been at Saint Orra and then at these rooms since half past seven in the morning, and he had not been near Kettle Rows and he was not going to be.

He had not told Elias. He had not told Nadia. Elias found out at eleven o'clock on Wednesday morning from Bess Yonkin at the Tool Registry, who had telephoned the Sequence Desk herself, which she had never done in nineteen years.

"Mr Ardent came in at a quarter to ten on Wednesday," she said, "with the die block in a bag and a note he had written himself, and he asked me to seize it."

"Seize his own die block."

"Seize his own die block," said Bess Yonkin, "and he stood at that counter and read the seizure form out loud to me twice, including the part that says the maker's block is surrendered voluntarily and that all work stamped with the block thereafter requires re-marking at the maker's own cost, which is about four hundred credits a year, and he said *that is correct, read the next part*."

Elias Venn had been standing in a tool shop for about ninety seconds without moving.

"He did not tell me," he said.

"He did not tell me either," said Bess Yonkin, "and I did not know until a bag came through my door at ten to ten on Wednesday morning with his name on it, and I want you to understand something about what that is. A maker is allowed to surrender a die block. It is in the schedule. It happens about four times a year in this district and it is nearly always a man who is finished. It is not a thing a man does on a Wednesday because of a case."

"What else happened?"

"Three things. A maker's enquiry was opened, which is not a charge and is not a hearing and is a file with his name at the top of it and it has a number. A notice went on the door of nine Pell Street at about two o'clock, which is a standard clinical practice notice and it says *unregistered practice, premises closed under clinical practice order*, and it is the notice he told you about on the Thursday before last, in a maintenance shed, nine days ago."

"And the second set," said Elias.

"The second set is in his bench room," said Bess Yonkin, "and the second set is inside a maker's enquiry, and there is no law in this city that lets anybody move a thing out of a bench room that is inside a maker's enquiry, including the man whose bench it is, and I have told him so and he has understood, and he is going to have to sit with it."

---

"He did it to protect the second set," Elias said, in the solicitor's waiting room, twenty minutes later, sitting down on the wrong side of the table so that his good ear faced the man. "He worked out on Wednesday morning, at a bench, that the second set is a piece of evidence that somebody would come for, and that if somebody came for it in a bench room there would be a fight he would lose in a corridor. And he went and put the die block in a bag and made it so that the second set could not be moved by anybody, including the man who would come for it."

"Which also made the second set unusable to us," said Nadia Kest, who was also in the room, in uniform, with her folder.

"Yes."

"That is the whole of what it cost and I would like it in a document, because he is going to be asked by his own solicitor why he did it and he is going to say *to protect the evidence*, and there is a great deal of room in that answer for a man who was frightened and nobody has checked it."

Elias did not look at her for a second.

"Write it with the other half in it," he said. "Write that the comparison was not available to him, and it is not available to me, and it is not available to Doctor Quill, and that it was not available to any of us on Wednesday morning when he put the block in a bag, because the dies are in a seized register and the plate is not on one and the only room in this city that can hold both is on the third floor of a building on a shopping street. Write that on Thursday afternoon somebody in that building asked for the object to be moved and she said no and wrote her reason down in a minute and asked for it to be minuted word for word. So the refusal is in the chain too. He is not the only one who closed a door on that comparison this week and he was not the first."

Jules Ardent had been listening to all of it with his hands still on his knees.

"The solicitor's position," he said, "is that on Wednesday morning you had a case and on Wednesday morning I gave away the dies, and that is a gift to you and a catastrophe to me, and that both of those are true and that the solicitor did not enjoy hearing it in that order."

"You could have told me," Elias said.

"Yes," said Jules Ardent.

"Why did you not tell me?"

"Because you would have come to the Tool Registry with me," said Jules Ardent, "and you would have stood at Bess Yonkin's counter and put a hand on the die block, and you have not put a hand on anything in this case since Wednesday the nineteenth and everybody in this room knows why, and I have spent six days watching a man be extremely careful about one thing and I was not going to be the reason he stopped."

Elias Venn sat in a solicitor's waiting room above a tobacconist's in the outer wards and did not have an answer.

The solicitor did. She was about fifty and she had a client who had confessed to six years of unregistered repairs and she had been in the room for four hours.

"The dies can be compared to the stamp on the plate, yes," she said. "A stamp profile cut on a die and a stamp profile marked on a carrier are the same shape or they are not, and a person with the right plate and the right hours can say which in about twenty minutes. The catch is that the dies are now in a seized register, and the plate is not on a register, and the only building in this city where a seized die and an unregistered plate can be looked at in the same hour is Concord's evidence room on the third floor, and that room's technicians work to the Registry's timetable."

"So it is available," said Elias, "to somebody who is not us."

"It is available to anybody who is willing to go into that building and submit the plate to their chain," said the solicitor. "Including us, at a cost, and including them, for free. That is the arrangement and it is not a conspiracy, it is a room with a booking system."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Do it," said Jules Ardent. "Book the room. Book it this morning. I'll sign anything you want me to sign."

"No," said Elias Venn.

"That is the stupidest thing anybody has said in this building in nine years."

"It is," Elias agreed. "It is, and I have thought about it for about ten seconds and here is why I am not doing it. If that plate goes into a Concord evidence room then every figure Doctor Quill has ever put her name to becomes a figure taken out of an object this Registry is holding, and we spent two days this week learning that the Registry issues an information line for a death before the residue is measured. I would rather lose the comparison."

"You would rather lose the comparison."

"I would rather lose the comparison," said Elias, "and I would like it recorded that Jules Ardent offered to open his own bench room to the Concord to get it, on the morning I refused, and that I said no, and that we are now both worse off than either of us was on Wednesday."

The solicitor wrote that down, which nobody had asked her to do.

---

He went back to Marshall Street at half past four on Friday afternoon because there was one thing left to do that day that he had not done, which was to walk a corridor with a man who had not been in it for six days.

The stockroom door was still on one hinge. The fire officer's notice was still on the frame. On the bench twenty feet away, at the north end, the tin of surgical staples was still holding down a single sheet of transfer paper, face up, and it had been there since ten past nine on Wednesday night, and it had been photographed where it lay at four o'clock on Saturday morning, and it was in nobody's file.

"Ahmed," Elias said. "If somebody from the Registry comes and asks about that piece of paper, do not tell them it is there."

"It is not in a file," said Ahmed, "so it does not exist, and if it does not exist then nobody has to answer for it, and I have been not knowing about it for four days and I am extremely tired of that." He looked down the corridor. "Sergeant. There is a thing you should know and I have been waiting for you to come back for it."

"Go on."

"That sheet is not the only one." Ahmed nodded at the dispensary counter, where a woman in a Ward Works coat was standing with a clipboard, reading the waiting-room board from about four feet away, which was not enough distance for a board of forty-one photocopied pages and a transfer order. "She got here at ten past four and she has been reading the board since ten past four, and she has got a finger on one of the pages of the book, and she has not asked anybody a single question, because everything she wants is on my doctor's wall in my doctor's ink and all she has to do is read it."

The woman on the board was not a woman Elias Venn had ever met, and she had a badge, and she was a Concord assessor, and she was standing four feet from a piece of paper with a stranger's name and a valid vest number on it, and she was going to be asked about it by somebody, and the somebody was not going to be him.

He wrote on the board in his own hand, under Friday's date, and did not soften it.

*28 Sept. Orin Sable named, from the public licensing register, class supply, 2nd floor Carrow Lane, restricted by endorsement from carrying out any dangerous working or attending a scene. Filed as SUPPLIER OF FRAMES and nothing else. Not identified by face, not arrested, not established as the maker of the carrier. Two customers, no shared motive. Jules Ardent surrendered his die block Wednesday, unasked, and the second set is frozen. I refused the Concord evidence room, and Doctor Quill refused it on Wednesday, and the comparison is therefore not available to us and is available to them.*
*28 Sept, 16:40. The transfer order on the corridor bench is a second document with the same four-act box in it. It is in nobody's file. Somebody from the Registry is reading the waiting-room board and a page of the maintenance book is on it with a name in it.*
