# Chapter 0020 — A Line She Would Not Write

They sat in the car on Marshall Street for about four minutes with the engine off.

"Go on," said Elias.

"Item three in the annex asked which office completed the box," said Nadia. "I could have answered one and two off the public concordance in about a minute, and I did not, and I gave the reason in the document as *a departmental position on a publishing convention of a certifying authority is not a position a department should take*, and that is a true sentence and it is not the reason."

"What is the reason."

"The reason is that if the phrase *no release was recorded at the threshold* is in a departmental position, then the Morrow Street matter is live, and if the Morrow Street matter is live, then the Kettle Rows matter has become a matter to which a connected record is relevant, and you become a person with a connected record." She put her hands flat on the wheel. "And the second thing is that your countersignature is the only thing in the world standing between a young man and a bus. Not a certificate, not a hold, not a schedule. *Your signature.* A detective who has declined once is a fact in a Registry file and it can be read by four offices. A detective who has asked for the old case on the same week is a fact that gets attached to the young man's address in every report anybody writes for the next six months, and I have watched that happen to a family once and I did not do anything about it in time, and it is the only professional failure in my career that I have never told anybody about and I have just told you in a car on a Sunday because you have run out of road."

"You should not have done it on your own," Elias said.

"I have not done it on my own. I have done it on my own and I have told you about it, which is the other half, and I am fifty per cent into a fortnight of being extremely pleased with myself about the second half and I would like to get on with it."

"Read me the four hundred words."

"No."

"Nadia."

"No," she said. "You have the copy. It is in your own file with my initials on the receipt, and you have read it, and what I want you to do now is notice the thing I did not do, which was to ask you whether I was right. I did not ask you, because if I had asked you at ten to one on Saturday morning you would have said yes, and you would have been right to say yes, and I would have had to go on a wire to a Deputy Chief at two in the afternoon on the strength of a conversation in a laboratory on the Friday night, and a Deputy Chief is a man who files things. So I did it alone and I am telling you afterwards. You will find that unacceptable. I have found it unacceptable for two days and I have kept doing it anyway, and I would like you to say something about it, because I would rather be argued with than be admired."

Elias Venn sat in a car on Marshall Street on a Sunday afternoon and thought about it properly, for about as long as it took.

"It is unacceptable," he said. "And it is the right decision and you made it for a reason I could not have given in writing, and I have spent twelve years waiting for a document that says *no release was recorded at the threshold* and you have just told me you deliberately kept it out of one, and I want to be honest with you about the fact that about forty per cent of me is furious and the other sixty per cent is relieved, and both of those are about me and neither of them is about the case."

"Good," said Nadia Kest. "Then come upstairs and I will show you a form, and you are not going to like the form either, and I would rather you saw it at half past two on a Sunday than at nine o'clock on Monday."

---

The form was a standard application for access to a certified record. It was one side, it cost nothing, and about four hundred people in the city fill one in in an average year. Three fields at the top for the applicant, the record and the purpose; six administrative fields beneath; and, at the bottom of the page, field eleven, headed *Other matters to which the applicant is or has been connected, in which the record is or may be relevant*, and beneath the heading, in a smaller type, a printed note: *This field must be completed. A request submitted without it will be returned. Disclosure under this field is to the holding office and to any office with a live interest in a connected matter.*

"Every one of these has been done four hundred times," said Nadia, "and about a hundred of them have been done by a detective, and I have read maybe nine of the hundred, because we are not interested in the other ninety-one, and I have read the nine because all nine had this field filled in with something, and in two of the nine the other matter went to the Registry within a fortnight."

"And if I leave field eleven blank?"

"It comes back to you. Unattended. And a request that comes back to you is a request that has been read by whoever returned it." She put her finger on it. "There is no version of this form on which you can ask for the Morrow Street report without the Kettle Rows file arriving at Morrow Hill in the same envelope, or within a day of it. That is the whole of it. That is what four hundred words and a Saturday morning bought you, and it took you four days to spend it, and I am sorry about that and I am not going to pretend I could have held it for you."

Elias Venn read field eleven nine or ten times.

"Right," he said. "Then I am going to fill it in properly, and I am going to do it in my own hand, and I am going to put the case number in it, and I am going to write the whole of the disposition out, because if I write *the matter is connected* then a clerk in Concord is going to make up her own mind about how, and I would rather the sentence was mine."

He wrote it in the small bad hand, and it took a long time, and it went over most of a side.

*Sequence Desk file 441-KR, Kettle Rows, deceased R (top floor, 14 Kettle Rows). Applicant is the assigned detective, retained by D/I Kest on 17 September. Residency at the premises declared in file. Preliminary sequence attestation issued by the Proof Registry on 24 September, with Schedule 1 (Disagreement of the Assigned Detective) attached. Public information sheet of 19 September, wording: a contained accident involving an unregistered ward tool. A named person is held at a named address under a clinical hold filed 21 September and is not a witness to the working. Requested record is relevant to item 2 of the Disagreement and to a comparison note of 29 September which has been filed as a note and not as a request. The applicant has not opened and has not requested any copy of the requested record since 2017. The applicant has no interest in the outcome of the requested matter, which is the attribution of a collapse to a named person, and no standing in it. The applicant's interest is narrower and is this: whether a category in a public code list was completed in a box on a report about that collapse. The applicant states plainly that the answer may be no.*

He read it back, and Nadia read it over his shoulder twice, and then she said, "That last sentence is going to make somebody very angry."

"It is going to make somebody very angry, and it is going to make them angry at the right person, and it is going to do that in about nine days instead of about four years, and I have spent twelve years waiting for a document and I am not going to wait another four years for a version of it that somebody could have built without me." He signed the first field and dated it. "Send it to the Deputy Chief's office by hand. Not by the tray. By hand, and logged, with the time on it."

"Lodged Sunday," said Nadia, "which means it is in the building at about five o'clock, and it is in the Deputy Chief's building at about eight, and it is read on Monday morning by an assistant who has been an assistant for eleven years, and the flag goes up on the file at about eleven."

"Then I am off this case on Monday, or on Tuesday, or on Wednesday, and the Registry will know by Tuesday, and Bettina Haile will know by Wednesday, and the attestation with my disagreement bound to the back of it goes into a file that now has a reason in it for not wanting me near it." He squared the paper up. "I know what it does."

"Yes. Do you want it before the hearing tomorrow?"

There was no hearing tomorrow. There had not been a hearing tomorrow for six days. She had said it anyway, because it is what an inspector says on a Sunday afternoon when a man has just cost himself a case, and because it gives him the shape of the next twelve hours to refuse.

"No," said Elias Venn. "I want it served after the board has sat."

Nadia Kest looked up.

"The board was adjourned on Thursday," she said.

"I know it was adjourned. It was adjourned because the clinical respondent's production was served on the twenty-seventh at four o'clock in the afternoon for a board that sat at ten that morning, and the production was ninety-odd pages of a dispensary's own maintenance book, and the board is now listed for Thursday the fourth of October." He put the pen down. "Dr Okafor published that book on Sunday the twenty-third so that nobody could take one page out of it, and the whole of the Concord's answer to that was to put the whole of it in a file and adjourn the thing she wanted. That is the correct response to the correct act and I want it noted somewhere that I think so, and that I am still waiting for Thursday the fourth."

"Then the application goes out and the flag goes up and you walk into a clinic on Thursday the fourth as a member of the public."

"Yes."

"That is a very bad Thursday the fourth for you."

"It is the fourth of October," said Elias Venn. "Nadia, I have been waiting twelve years for a man in an office in 2017 to be a man filling in a box, and it turns out he is a shelf number, and the only document that says what is in the box is in an archive on the other side of the hill. And everything I have wanted for twelve years costs one thing, which is that I have to write my own name in a field and lose the case that has a young man in it. I have done the arithmetic in a car on a Sunday afternoon. I have been waiting twelve years for this and I am not going to do it at a moment when doing it costs Niko Senn his last piece of paper, and there is no version of me that gets to have it before Thursday the fourth."

Nadia Kest did not say anything for a moment. Then she took the form, squared it with his, and put it in the out-tray, and did not put it in the tray, and stood up and got her coat.

"One thing," she said. "I can get the disclosure routed. Served as a general circulation, four offices learn on Monday that the detective on 441-KR wants the Morrow Street record, and then every report anybody writes about that case for six months carries a motive that is not in the case. I can ask for it to be served personally, on a named person, with a date, copied to the Deputy Chief and to nobody else. That is a line on a distribution schedule and I am entitled to ask for it, and I am."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me for a distribution schedule," said Nadia Kest. "Thank me on Thursday the fourth, in a corridor, in front of a board, where a man is going to come and tell you what happened at four o'clock in the morning to his mother."

---

He walked down to Marshall Street at about twenty past four because there was one thing left on the Saturday–Sunday that he had not done, and it was not on the board, because the board was for places and the corridor of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic was the last of them.

Niko Senn was in the corridor.

He was dressed. He had a plastic bag with a clean shirt in it and his rash had faded to the colour of weak tea and he had been moved out of room two at about eleven that morning because the bed was needed, and was waiting for a taxi to the address of his aunt at ten past five, and was sitting on one of the twenty-two chairs with his back against the wall and his feet up on a radiator that was cold.

"Half past six's too late for me," he said. "It's the bus. Auntie won't have a phone. And I've got to be at the depot Thursday for the board, and they haven't told me anything about the board, and my aunt won't have a phone about that either."

"The board is Thursday the fourth of October at ten. It's a review board, it sits in public, and the clinical hold is what keeps you where you are until it sits, and Doctor Okafor filed it on the twenty-first and it has been upheld twice since, once on Monday when a courier turned up at six with a signature and once on Thursday when it was adjourned to the fourth."

"They adjourned it?"

"It was adjourned because the clinic's own production was served late in the afternoon for a board that sat in the morning," said Elias. "And the production is ninety-odd pages of the clinic's maintenance book, which Dr Okafor put on the wall of her waiting room on Sunday the twenty-third so that nobody could take one page out of it. And the Concord's answer to that was to put the whole of it in a file and adjourn the thing she wanted. That is the correct answer to what she did, and she still did it, and I would like you to remember that, because you are the only other person in this case who has ever chosen a record over a result."

Niko Senn sat with that for a second.

"Say the other thing again slowly," he said. "The one about Monday." And Elias said it again slowly, twice — that the account had marked him discharged at six o'clock on Monday morning, that the file closed, that nobody was coming for him, and that nobody in the city now knew where he was either — and watched a young man of twenty-four go through about nine days of fear in about ninety seconds, in a corridor, with a plastic bag on his knees.

Then Niko Senn put his hand in his pocket and took out a card.

It was a small printed card, the thickness of a playing card gone soft, and it had been in a pocket or a wallet for three weeks and it had a bent corner and a thumbprint on the corner, and it had a name on it that was not a name Elias recognised, and a card number, and no Concord registration mark, and no address on it at all.

"There," said Niko Senn. "I've had that since the Wednesday before she died. He wrote his own name on it, in the room, while I was lying on the floor, and he gave it to me when I left, and I put it in my wallet and I have carried it about for three weeks and I told you in a hospital on the seventeenth of September that I could have photographed it and I didn't, and I did not, and I want you to have it now before I do something stupid with it at a bus stop."

He held it out.

"No," said Elias Venn.

Niko Senn's hand stayed where it was.

"What?"

"No. I am not taking that."

"It's my card. I've had it three weeks."

"It is your card, and I am not going to take it, and if I take it then in about four months there is a piece of plastic in a police store in this city that a man in a wig has ordered produced, and it has my fingerprints on it, and a faint of mine on the front, and it was in your wallet, and the defence will say your fingerprints are on it and the officer's are on it, and they will be right, and you will spend a year being a man who cannot explain a card." He put his hands in his pockets, which was what he did when he wanted them to be somewhere he could account for. "And there is a second thing and I am telling you both. If I touch it, it stops being yours. It becomes an exhibit. And then you are not a man who happened to have a card, you are a man who gave a card to the police, and those two men are treated completely differently by every office in this city, and the second one has a file, and the second one is on a list, and the second one gets a letter."

"So it's just mine."

"It is entirely yours," said Elias. "It is in your name by the fact that you kept it. Nobody has ever asked you for it and I am not going to, and if the Registry sends anybody with a form you ring me before you ring them, and if you cannot get me you take it to the woman at the dispensary counter and you do not hand it over, and she will know what to do with it because she has spent three days putting a whole clinic's failures on a wall so that nobody could take one page out of it."

Niko Senn put the card back in his pocket, and did it slowly, and put his hand flat on the pocket afterwards the way a man checks for a thing that is still there.

"He wrote his name on the back of it," said Niko Senn. "In the room, on his way out. I watched him do it and I didn't think anything about it, and now it's the only thing in the world in this case with a name on it."

"I'm not going to ask you what it says," said Elias.

"You don't have to. I can just tell you. It isn't long and it isn't hard."

"No," said Elias. "Don't. And I want to tell you why, because I have thought about it and the reason is not noble. The day I have a name, I have an expectation. And the day I have an expectation is the day I start building a file around it instead of building a case around the thing in front of me, and I have done that to about four people in six days, and one of them was a mechanic and one of them is a woman who works in a building with a plate in a hard case, and I have apologised to both of them, and I am not going to do it to you on a Sunday afternoon in a corridor." He put his hand out, flat, palm up, and then took it back. "You keep it. If you ever do want to give it to somebody, give it to a person and not to an office. I am a person. Today is not the day."

"Coroner came and went," he said.

"I know. I read it. There is no dangerous-working finding, because there has not been a hearing about one, and there is not going to be one, because on Monday morning the Registry issued its own attestation saying the working was self-directed and that no third party need be looked at, and I did not sign that either, and the one piece of paper I got out of the room was a schedule with my own name at the bottom of it admitting that I cannot rule out the ordinary explanation for what I saw."

"So nobody's coming," said Niko Senn. "For a working. Right?"

"For a working, nobody is coming on the strength of a document. A person is always a different matter and a person is me and about four other people and one of us will be there."

Niko Senn looked at him for a second longer than was comfortable, and then he said, "You know what you are, don't you. You're the one who made them write it down. That's all you've done for a week and it's the first time anybody's ever said it out loud and I want to say it while we're both standing up."

He got up off the chair and picked up his plastic bag and looked at his watch and said he had to go or he would miss it, and he went out into the yard with his hands in his pockets and the bag against his leg, and at the gate he turned round and shouted, "If I ever see that man in the smock I'm telling you before I hit him," and then he was gone up Marshall Street at a walk, twenty-four years old, in a clean shirt, on his way to a bus that would take him away from a building in which somebody had once been the address of a working, and the address had been chosen by somebody else.
