# Chapter 0025 — The Order He Was Taught

Niko Senn had a form in his hand when he came into the interview room at the Kettle Rows station, and he had folded it in half and had been holding it folded, because it took him a moment to work out that he was allowed to open it in front of a police officer and then decided that he was going to open it in front of a police officer and then did it.

"It isn't a statement," he said. "I want that said first, before anybody says anything. It isn't a statement, it's a form, and there's two of them, and I kept the other one."

---

The room was the same one from the seventeenth of September with a different chair in it. There was a clock on the wall that ran about four minutes fast, which both of them knew and neither of them mentioned, and a table with a coffee ring on it that had not been there before and a notice about property in the corridor.

Elias Venn had come in at ten to nine and had not sat down until Niko Senn had.

"Give it to me," he said.

"You said you don't take things off people."

"I said I don't take documents off people." He held out his hand. "A form that a person has filled in about themselves and has brought here on purpose is not evidence in my hand, it's a paper, and if it goes in a store with my prints on it then in four months a man in a wig says the officer wrote it. Give it over and I'll read it in front of you and it will be read out."

Niko Senn gave it over.

**WITNESS SERVICES (SEQUENCE SUPPORT)**
**Form W-1 (rev. 3) — WITHDRAWAL OF ACCOUNT**

It was one side. Fields one to five were printed: name, date of first account, office holding the account, account reference, and the address of the person. Field six was a box, and it was headed, in the same printing as the rest of it:

*6. Stated reason for withdrawal.*

Underneath the fields, at the bottom, in a smaller type: *A withdrawn account is superseded and remains on file. Withdrawal is recorded in the ordinary way and does not by itself impugn either account.*

"A copy of a leaflet I gave you on Monday," said Elias Venn, reading it. "Practice note W-7."

"Yes."

"Write it in, then. In your own hand, and take as long as you want, and I'm going to go and stand at the window because I don't think you should have an audience for it."

Niko Senn wrote in field six, and it took him about four minutes, and he did not stop, and at the end he read it back to himself twice and said, "That's it," and put the pen down.

Elias Venn read it over his shoulder from the window, and then went and sat down again, and read it properly at arm's length with his hands on his knees.

*6. Stated reason. I gave my account on the 17th September and I have been to the free service on 24 September and 29 September. I have been given an order to hold that I did not have before I went, and I cannot say which part of it I saw and which part I was given. I would rather have no account in this than one I cannot stand behind. The leaflet says an uncertain account recorded as uncertain is worth more than a complete one you cannot stand behind, so that is what I am doing.*

Elias Venn read it twice.

"That's true," he said.

"It's all true."

"I know it's true. I want to be careful about which part I'm saying that to. Say the second half again."

"I went to the free service twice." Niko Senn's hands went flat on the table, on purpose, the way they did, and he saw Elias Venn see him do it and did not take them off. "Miss Kaye, at the back, past the certificate office. Monday the twenty-fourth, two hours. Saturday the twenty-ninth, two hours. I had a card in my hand both times and I showed it to her, and I held it the whole time, and at the end she wrote a number off it and put the card in a folder, and I got it back both times."

"Fourteen hundred people have had that in their hand. Don't over-read it."

"I'm not over-reading it. I'm telling you what's true and you can weigh it." Niko Senn looked at the form. "I want to say the rest and then I want you to ask me about the order, and I'll answer, and then I'm going to go and catch the bus."

---

"You said the order was given to you. Say what you mean by that."

"I've got two," said Niko Senn. "The one from the man in the smock, and the one from her. And they're the same exercise."

"Go on."

"The first four times — Neil, at the top of the tax office, in the last week of August — the room was made up. It was always made up. She'd be in the room, and I'd be in the room, and he'd tap the table, and I'd breathe out and say her name, and the name was Tamsin Ro, and I never met her, and I said her name about forty times in a room in August." He said it steadily. "And that was a made-up room with a made-up tap and a made-up name in it, and it worked, and I slept, and everybody's happy. That's the first one."

"And the second."

"The second one's the same exercise." Niko Senn said it like a man laying two pieces of paper on a table to show that they matched. "Room. Something at the door. A name. It's the same three things in the same three places. Except the room was hers and the door was hers and the name was hers, and the tap was a click, and there was a mug, and I did not choose any of it."

Elias Venn felt the room go very quiet and very cold, in the way it had gone cold in a laboratory off Ash Street a fortnight ago, and he heard his own voice come from a long way off.

"When you say the room was hers. On which day."

"The landing. Fourteenth Kettle Rows, the fourth floor, the last flight." Niko Senn said it without any hesitation at all. "She walked me up the actual stair. Twice. Once on the Monday and once on the Saturday, and she did it in about eleven minutes both times, and she stood where I was standing on the sixteenth and she said *this is where you were*, and she was right, and I've thought about her saying *this is where you were* more than I've thought about anything in three weeks."

Elias Venn did not write it down. His hands were on the table and he was aware of them being on the table, and he was aware of what a man in a smock had taught a boy to do with his hands, and he took them off and put them in his lap.

"The room was the fourth-floor room on Kettle Rows," he said. "The room, the door, the name."

"Yes."

"And on the sixteenth, when you were on that landing, was there a tap?"

"There wasn't a tap. There was a click." Niko Senn looked at him. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. There's never a tap. There is a click and I have been listening to a tap for a month and the click is the tap, and that is the bit I cannot — " he stopped, and started again, more carefully, because he had decided in a launderette on Wednesday that he was going to say this properly or not at all. "Mr Venn. I want to say the next part and I want you to let me say it without helping me."

"I'm not going to help you."

"The exercise gives you the order before the thing happens," said Niko Senn. "That's the whole point of it. You get the order, and then the thing comes, and your job is to hold the order and put the real thing in it. And on the sixteenth I put a real room and a real click and a real mug in an order I'd had since August, and it went in, and it fitted, and it's mine now, and I could not tell you this morning which three things I saw and which three things I was given, and that is not me being vague to be helpful. That is me telling you I don't know."

He put the pen down.

"And she was inside it while she did it," he said. "Both times. Not the room. Me. She asked me the questions and she wrote down what I said and she was very good at it, and she was kind, and the first time she told me that nothing she did could put anything into my head, and I believed her, and I still believe her, because it's true, she didn't put anything in my head. I put it in my own head. That's what I'm saying. She gave me a shape and I filled it in."

"Where were you on the Monday the twenty-fourth?"

"At the clinic. From about ten." Niko Senn said it without being asked to. "I was there when the courier came with the order at six in the morning. I was there when she sent it back. I was there at about half eleven and I came in here — " he checked the clock, four minutes fast, and then did not use it — "at about eleven, and I was at the back of the Council at half one. And on the Saturday I was at the clinic in the morning and at the Council at half one again, and I hadn't slept since the sixteenth, and I'd been told I'd been discharged, and nobody at the counter would say it out loud, and the whole of the twenty-fourth and the whole of the twenty-ninth are the two days I remember best, because I was frightened and because a man in a smock had already taught me that the way you remember a bad week is by the shape you were given in August."

"Go on," said Elias Venn, and his voice did not sound like his.

"The thing about the sixteenth is that it isn't finished," said Niko Senn. "I don't mean I think somebody's coming. I mean that whatever it was, it's still going, and it's got my name on it as well as hers, and Miss Kaye used it. She didn't put a room in my head. She asked me for a room and I gave her every one I have got, and the one I gave her was the one that's still happening."

---

The door opened at about a quarter to eleven and a clerk came in with a bundle and put it on the table with a printed slip on the top of it and said, "Produced in answer to the withdrawal, Inspector," and went out again.

Nadia Kest had been in the corridor and came in behind it and shut the door.

"Explain that," said Elias Venn.

"They have to produce it. It's in the notice, it's on the W-7, a withdrawal requires the office to produce the account to the certifying office in the ordinary way." She was looking at the bundle and not at him. "I've never seen it. It's not in 441-KR because it isn't in 441-KR, and it is in there because your name is on it, which is a thing I will be writing about."

Elias Venn put his hands flat on the table, because that is what he did, and then took them off, and then put them flat again, and got them into his lap, and it took two attempts.

He read the slip first. It said the bundle was twelve tabs and that tab 3 was an intake sheet and that tabs 4 to 12 were not produced, and there was a time on the slip in a box.

**RECEIVED: 08:52.**

He looked at it for a moment.

"What time did he come in?" he said.

"About five past," said Nadia.

"Right," said Elias Venn.

He turned to the second tab and then the third, and the third tab was a single sheet with a printed corner on it that said **WITNESS, OPERATIONS — INTAKE**, and the top of it had the person's name and a date, and the date was the eleventh of September, and in the middle of the sheet, in a ruled box, there was a printed heading in capitals.

*DESCRIPTION OF ORDER AS PERCEIVED, BY THE ASSIGNED DETECTIVE, TO BE USED IN WITNESS SUPPORT.*

Underneath the heading, in the box, in a typewriter face:

*In the room before the door made its noise. Then her speaking.*

Under that, on the same sheet, a second ruled box, printed heading: *ACCEPTED — OFFICE USE ONLY*, and inside it a name in capitals and a time and the office.

*J. KAYE. ACCEPTED 21 SEPTEMBER, 14:22.*
*WITNESS SERVICES (SEQUENCE SUPPORT).*

He had read that line in a clear plastic sleeve in a corridor on Marshall Street at twenty to twelve on Monday morning, standing back from a counter with his hands visible and nine people in the room, and written it on the back of his own left hand without scrubbing it off, and it had been in his notebook ever since, and it had been the one name in the file he had gone out of his way for two days not to read.

He read it four times.

*Mr Venn — this is the intake sheet, and the account is not on it yet, and there is a box on the sheet for what the assigned detective perceived, and it is not a finding, it is only a description. It goes in with the account so that the account can be held against something. Can you tell me the order you perceived?*

That was her voice, and he had heard it on a telephone in an office corridor on Friday the twenty-first of September at about twenty past two in the afternoon, and she had asked him a reasonable question in a reasonable way, and he had answered it in nine words.

It was his own words. There was not one letter in it that was not his. It was the order he had read at ten to two in the morning off a jamb in his own building and had never written down anywhere on the seventeenth, and because she had asked politely, and because he is a man who answers a polite question from a competent official, and because at twenty past two on a Friday afternoon he had had no reason on earth to think the words would be kept.

He had read *to be used in witness support* as *to support the witness-support file*.

"You have gone white," said Niko Senn.

"Have I." Elias Venn put the sheet down flat, which took him two goes, and it was the first time in a month that he had handled a document with the flat of his hand and he did not think about it at all. "Niko. Look at me and do not be frightened. I want to ask you one question and it is the only one I am going to ask you and I am not going to try to lead you."

"Go on."

"On the Saturday. The twenty-ninth. What did she read to you."

Niko Senn took his time.

"She read me a sheet," he said. "Not that one. A different one, on her desk, with a clip on it, and she read me a line off it and she said *hold to that*, and then she said, *that is the order the officer saw on the morning, and he has been honest in writing that he cannot rule out the ordinary explanation for what he says he saw, and that is exactly why it needs holding, and it needs holding by somebody who has not got an ordinary explanation.*"

The fan in the corner of the room came on.

"She read it to me twice," said Niko Senn, "and I said it back, and I have said it about nine hundred times since Saturday and I could not tell you for a fact whether I saw it or was given it, and I want to be fair to her, because she was not hiding anything, and if I had walked in there with the sentence already in me she'd have written *unable to supply an order* and gone home at half three."

He looked at the form on the table.

"And that is why I've filled that in in," he said, "and it is why I want it in the file in the words I said it in, because the sentence on that sheet is the sentence I was taught, and I would like somebody in an office to have to read both of them on the same morning."

---

Elias Venn did not read him. He wanted to with a completeness he had not experienced since the nineteenth of September, and he understood for the first time in his working life what the word *trained* was for, and the wanting sat in him like a held breath.

He asked for the times instead. Not the order; the times. When the door opened on the twenty-fourth and when it closed, and the same for the twenty-ninth; how long he had been in the corridor before eleven thirty; who had booked the appointment and when; whether the card had been in his hand when the first session started; and whether he had been asked to hold anything in his hand during the second one. He wrote them in the small bad hand in the front of the notebook and read them back to Niko Senn twice, and Niko Senn corrected two of them.

"What is item three of your disagreement?" Niko Senn asked, at about ten past eleven, when the clock said eleven fourteen. "She used a sentence off it twice and both times she read it out loud and I can still do it."

Elias Venn turned round one sheet in the file, and read it out, because he had read it aloud in a laboratory at Saint Orra Reach to a woman who had told him item three was going to be the whole document, and because a man of twenty-four had just asked him for it and had a right to it.

"The assigned detective states that he is unable to say what that act is, whether it was performed in the room or delivered into the room, who made it, or why. He states that he is aware of at least one ordinary explanation for his own account of it that he is not currently able to exclude."

"That one," said Niko Senn. "That was the one. She said it twice and she said it the way you say a thing that is in a rulebook."

---

He did not tell Niko Senn he was sorry. Niko Senn had not done anything wrong and would have had to be told what to do about being sorry, and there was no version of the afternoon in which a man who had withdrawn his own account, correctly, in writing, in the ordinary way, on the advice of a leaflet, got an apology from a police officer with a face like that.

What he did instead was: he asked Niko Senn to wait five minutes; he asked Nadia Kest to take out her custody book; and he asked her to write a line on it with the time on it and her own signature underneath it, recording that at ten to nine on Friday the fifth of October a person presented Form W-1 (rev. 3) in the interview room at the Kettle Rows station, that a bundle produced in answer was opened in that person's presence, and that the assigned detective's own description of order was read out in that room and had not previously been disclosed to him in any form.

"In eleven days it will be a question," he said, "and in eleven days somebody is going to ask whether the man who wrote that line was told the whole of it, and today is the day I can put my signature under the fact that I was not."

Nadia Kest wrote it. It took her a while, and she did not ask him to slow down, and when she had finished she turned the book round and put it in front of him and he signed it, and the time was 11:26.

"There," she said. "That's the last thing either of us will sign on this file together."

"Is it."

"It's the last thing either of us will sign on this file together," said Nadia Kest. "Elias. Your application goes to you at twenty to twelve."

"I know."

"It comes in a hand, on paper, to a room, and it will be logged, and after it is logged you are not the assigned detective on 441-KR, and the file goes to Rennick, and the witness is out of it, and the attestation with your name on the back of it goes into a cabinet." She put the pen in her pocket. "I have signed a great many things this month and I have signed exactly one of them for you. I am going to be asked about the other one by somebody who is not me."

Niko Senn had been sitting through all of it with his hands on the table and had understood perhaps two-thirds of it, and he got up, and put his coat on, and stopped at the door.

"Mr Venn."

"Yes."

"I did keep the second copy," he said. "They gave it to me at the counter on the Saturday and I have got it in my bag. And I'm not giving it to you and I'm not burning it."

"I would not have asked you to."

"I know you wouldn't have." Niko Senn opened the door. "That's why I'm telling you I've got it, so you don't spend a fortnight working out whether I've handed it to somebody. I'm going to be at the depot at two and I'm going to sleep tomorrow because I've got nothing to be frightened of for about a week now, which is the first time since the sixteenth, and I'd rather be frightened of something that's going to happen than frightened of a thing that's already finished."

He went out, and the door came to, and the clock in the empty room went round to four minutes past whatever it was.

Elias Venn sat at a table with a one-page form, a twelve-tab bundle with nine tabs missing, and his own handwriting in a ruled box on the third one, and he put his hand flat on the form — the form, not the bundle, the one Niko Senn had filled in in front of him and would walk out of the building without, because there are two of them and he had kept the other — and he left it there, and he did not read anything at all, which was the single thing left in the world that he was still good for.
