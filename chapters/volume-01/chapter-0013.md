# Chapter 0013 — The Whole Book

Halvard Reisz was sixty-eight and had come up the stairs of his own building at half past nine on a Sunday morning, which in eleven years he had done four times, and he stood in the doorway of the second-floor flat with a folded sheet in his hand and did not come in.

"I want to read you something and I want you to tell me if it means what I think it means."

"Come up."

"I will not come up." He turned the sheet round. "That is the district's notice of revised ward continuity assessment for the block, dated Friday, and it took effect at midnight last night, and my building is in it."

Elias read it from the doorway. It was a single side of machine-printed paper with a table on it: a block reference, a score, a band, and a date. The score for his block had been 61 in June. It was 49 now. The band had moved from *elevated* to *standard*.

"That is good news," Elias said.

"It is not good news, Mr Venn, and I have been up since six doing the arithmetic, and I would like you to hear the arithmetic from a man who owns a building." He put his finger on the number. "Forty per cent is what the district has taken off the ward maintenance contract for this block. I telephoned the works depot at eight. They were very pleasant. The contract was re-tendered in August on the June score because the June score is the score you can price on, and the re-tender came in forty per cent under, and the block has been on the new contract since midnight, because that is when contracts start."

"Forty per cent."

"Forty per cent of the money that keeps a stair ward, a cold-room line and a landing light alive in a building the city considers ordinary." He lowered the paper. "And here is the part I cannot put down. My score has been seventy-nine for two years and I have not moved it, because my score has never moved, because the light on the second landing is late and has been late since March and nobody has ever once come and looked at it. Sixty-one in June, forty-nine now. Something in this block got measurably better in three months and I have spent the whole of that three months watching the one thing I complained about get worse."

Elias stood in his own doorway and did not say the sentence, because the sentence was that the score had improved because fewer faults were being reported, and that fewer faults were being reported because a helpful man with a card had been answering them, and that he had learned the second half of that on Friday night from a night porter in a corridor while a working was sitting in a room behind a steel door.

He said: "Mr Reisz, I have a photograph of nine people in a corridor at half past eleven at night on Friday, and I have a night porter's statement, and I have two system timestamps forty-one minutes apart. If I gave you all three, and if you asked me in a room with a panel in it what I thought had happened in this block since June, I would have to tell you that I do not know, and I would have to tell you that my knowing would not be evidence, and you would be relying on the same man who lives in your basement."

"Then what do I do with it?"

"Keep it. Do not put it in a letter to the Council. If it goes in a letter it becomes a man's opinion in a man's handwriting, and you will be quoted in about a fortnight and then somebody will ask what the police think about the ward light on the second landing, and the answer will be that the police think it is a ballast." Elias held the door wider. "Come in for a cup of tea and I will show you which document I mean, and then you will decide."

Halvard Reisz came in for a cup of tea, and Elias showed him nothing, and they both pretended that was not what had happened, and the man went back down his own stairs.

---

The Kettle Rows Community Clinic was holding a piece of paper over the dispensary counter at eleven.

"Sunday," said Sana Okafor. "It has come on a Sunday. Do you know what comes on a Sunday? Nothing comes on a Sunday. The registrar's office is shut. The Council's ward contracts office is shut. And this has come on a Sunday, which means it came out of a machine, and that means somebody set a machine going in June and the machine has been running for three months and it did not need anybody's permission this morning."

She put it flat. It was a consultation response form, one page, with a box on it marked *estimated persons at risk from ward failure, this address*.

"You have to put a number in that box," she said. "And it has to go back by six o'clock this evening because the contract starts at midnight."

"What does it say you have to put in it?"

"Any number I like, in a reasonable range, and it is not checked against anything, and it goes on a public register at the works depot, where anybody can come and read it." She looked up. "So I have about forty minutes to decide what the number of people in this building is going to be, in public, for the rest of time."

"Twenty-two chairs," Elias said. "Nine usually full. Two beds upstairs. Ahmed on the desk at night. You, me, and Jules Ardent on a ceiling at four in the morning on Friday."

"A hundred," said Sana Okafor.

Elias did not say anything, and it took him about two seconds to work out why he had not, which was that he had been about to correct her in a corridor in front of a man holding a form, and that he had no standing whatever in the number of people in her waiting room, and that she had told a private laboratory in a corridor on Wednesday evening that a hundred was the number that stops a van turning into that yard, and that she had been right then and was right now, and that a survey that said nine would be a survey that said *come and get us*.

"Write a hundred," he said.

"Write a hundred," Sana Okafor agreed. "Not because it is true. Because it is the number that makes the arithmetic work and I am the one who has to live in the answer." She wrote it, and initialled it, and did not post it. "Now. What did you come in for, because you came in on a Sunday morning with your coat on and you have not said anything for four minutes and you never come in without something you have decided not to do."

---

He said it in a corridor, standing up, at half past eleven, and he said it the way a man lays something on a table.

"I want the maintenance book. I do not want it brought here and I do not want it carried out of that room and I do not want it in a police file. I want the Wednesday page photographed where it lies, again, with a second witness this time, and the two prints side by side with the timestamps on the back, and one of them going into the Kettle Rows file with a number on it."

Sana Okafor did not answer for some time.

"Ask me the question properly," she said. "Because you have not asked me anything. You have told me what you want and it is very interesting that you have not once said my name in it."

"Doctor, I am asking you whether I may photograph the Wednesday page of your clinic's own ward maintenance book, in your own stockroom, where it is lying, with you standing there, and put one print into a police file. I am not asking to take it, and I am not asking to move it, and I am not asking you to be a witness to anything except that you were in the room while a photograph was taken."

"That is the question." She was still not agreeing. "Now the other one. Why the second witness."

"Because the first one is a night porter in my own case, taken at four in the morning by a man who is the assigned detective, and I have thought about that photograph for about thirty hours and I have concluded that the version of it that will be attacked first is that I arranged the frame. Ahmed gave that exhibit a witness who is not me, and he did it in about four seconds on Friday morning, and I did not have to ask twice, which tells me something about what I am like."

"And the second witness is me."

"It would be you, or Ahmed, or a person from this building who is not on my side and not on yours, and you are the one who is in the corridor. It would be better if it was somebody who disagreed with you."

Sana Okafor laughed, once, mostly out of air.

"You want me to bring in somebody who disagrees with me so that my own book has a hostile witness in its history."

"I want somebody in the history of that page who is not paid by either of us."

She turned and looked down the corridor, at the north end, at the steel bench with the tin of surgical staples and the single sheet of transfer paper lying under it, and then at the stockroom door standing eight inches open on one hinge, and then at the notice she had taped to the frame herself at half past seven on Saturday morning.

"Here is my terms," she said, "and they are not negotiable and you may as well not try."

"Go on."

"One. The book does not leave that bench. Nobody carries it out of that room, including you, including at midnight, including if the fire officer is angry. It is a clinic's book and it lives in a clinic's stockroom and if the Concord comes with a seizure notice for it they can have it when the clinic opens and not before, in front of me, and I will read every name on the notice aloud to my waiting room."

"Agreed."

"Two. Every page." She held up one finger. "Not the Wednesday page. Every page. Forty-one months of it, in ink, on both sides, and it is going to be copied on a machine in this corridor and every page of the copy is going to be pinned to that board in the waiting room where the transfer order is, with the date range written across the top of it."

Elias did not understand for about four seconds.

"That is the whole book."

"That is the whole book," said Sana Okafor. "You are asking me for one page and I am giving you forty-one, and here is why. The moment one page of that book is in a police file, then one page of that book is a thing somebody can hold up, and somebody will hold it up, and then the sensible question becomes *what else is in the book that I have not been shown*, and the answer to that question decides what the book means. So I do not give you a page. I put the whole book on my own wall, in my own ink, on my own board, before anybody can ask, and then there is no page anybody can take out of it, because all forty-one of them came out at once and they have been in a waiting room where nine people can read them for two days."

"That is very good," Elias said, and meant it, and was aware in the same breath that he had been about to win a small argument and had instead been handed something better by a person who had more to lose than he did.

"Third," she said, "and this is the one that costs, so I am going to say it out loud and then I am not going to say it again. When forty-one months of that book is on a public board, the Concord will read it. And in forty-one months of that book there is a bolt I never got to, a slow trap under the cold room, a landing light on the second floor that is not to code and has never been to code, a water heater with a service date eleven months old in a building I do not own, and a Wednesday in February where a man came for four hours and signed a book and I could not tell you one single thing about him because I did not write his name down."

She put the pen down.

"Nowhere on that board will there be a date on which a patient's ward failed. I will take that risk and I will take it deliberately. I have a public board and I am going to fill it, and what I am going to fill it with is every fault I have ever failed to fix, because that is the only way to be allowed to be angry about the ones somebody else has. If I publish only the Wednesday page, then in six months somebody comes to my door with a clipboard and says *Doctor, your own book shows you cannot hold a stair ward*, and they will be right, and there will be nothing on the wall of my own clinic saying that the stair ward in the next building is worse. I am not going to be the only document in this district that admits anything."

"Third," Elias said. "Accepted. And for the record, in front of you and not in a file, I think that is the most expensive thing anybody has done on this case and it is not a money price."

"It is worse than a money price, it is a sleep price, and I am forty-eight next year and I have been awake at five for nineteen years." She picked the pen up again. "Ahmed can be the second witness. He is not on your side and he is not on mine, he is on the side of a man of seventy who stood with his hands folded, and that is the side this week has been short of."

---

They did it at twenty past two, from the stockroom doorway, with Ahmed on the left and Dr Okafor on the right and the corridor and the bench and the twenty feet between them all in one exposure, and Elias not crossing the threshold, and Elias's name on the back of the print in his own hand this time, with the date and the time, and Ahmed's name in the corner because he had insisted, again, on the page number.

On the Wednesday page, in a hand that belonged to nobody in the building, there was a name and a vest number and a signature and a job.

*D. MEARS. W-4471. Check the line.*

Nobody in the room said anything about it for a moment.

"That is a name," said Ahmed.

"It is a name and a number," said Sana Okafor. "Mr Venn, I am going to say the thing I have been not saying since Saturday, and then I am going to go and answer a consultation form. That name is on the wall of my stockroom and it has been since Wednesday night, and the whole of this building has been walking past that book for four days on the assumption that the handwriting is the problem. It is not the handwriting. It is that nobody has ever asked about the number."

"I have asked about the number," Elias said. "My inspector asked about the number at about ten this morning, and the answer came back at twenty past twelve, and I came here at eleven and I did not have it yet."

"Sit down," said Ahmed, and pulled out the stool.

"W-4471 is a live vest number. It was issued in April to a contractor on the district list and it is live, and the name on it is D. Mears, and D. Mears is a real man in Kettle Rows, and he is sixty-one, and he did ward work for the depot for twenty-two years, and he resigned in June." Elias said it in the order she would want it in. "So either a man with a real vest and a real name did it, or somebody used a genuine number with a name that belongs to somebody else. And I have no way of telling you which, because both of those are ordinary."

"Then which do you think," said Sana Okafor.

"I think somebody stood in my stockroom on Wednesday night and wrote a contractor's name in my book because a contractor's name is the name that gets a man through a door at twenty-one ten without a counter-signature," Elias said. "And I think that whoever it was, he did not know it was a lie, and I have got no evidence of that at all."

He photographed the page. He did not touch the book, he did not touch the sheet on the bench, and when Dr Okafor said *that will do* he said *that will do* and put the phone away.

At ten past four, a young man from the Proof Registry came to the clinic with a document request in a plastic wallet, because a request that has to be carried through four streets does not go through a system, it goes on a knee. It was the hearing pack. It ran to six pages and one of the pages was a schedule of documents to be produced, and Dr Okafor read it standing up, and then she read it again, and then she handed it to Elias and pointed at item four without saying anything at all.

*Item 4. The dispensary ward maintenance book, complete, for the period September 2017 to date, including all pages bearing entries by persons not employed by the clinic.*

Somebody already knew the book was there. Somebody had known it well enough to ask for all of it, and to say which pages they wanted, and the answer to the second half of that question was written in a stranger's hand on the Wednesday page in a book on a steel bench, and somebody in this city had asked the question without ever being in the building.

"They want all forty-one months," said Sana Okafor.

"They want all forty-one months," Elias agreed, "and they have told you in writing that they are interested in the pages with strangers in them, and you have known since Saturday morning that you are going to answer that by pinning forty-one months to your own wall, and you knew it before I got here."

"I knew it Wednesday night," said Sana Okafor, "at about ten past ten, with a stranger signing my book."
