# Chapter 0006 — What Elias Touched

The residue laboratory at Saint Orra Reach was two rooms behind the hospital's linen office, and it had a bench, a stool, an extractor hood that made a noise like a man trying to be polite, and a shelf of salt-glass in padded sleeves that Mara Quill had taught a ward porter to handle and no one else at all.

She had the plate from the threshold standing in a rack in the middle of the bench under a paper sleeve, and beside it a second rack that was empty, and beside that her own written sheet, which was four lines of figures in pencil because pencil could be corrected and ink could not be argued with later.

"Sit down," she said. "Not on the stool. On the chair by the wall, and put your feet up on the frame, because in about ten minutes you are going to be a man who cannot hold a cup."

"That bad?"

"That bad." She was already logging. "This is a comparison, not a reading, and I want the distinction in your head before we start, because in about forty minutes you are going to find out what you are when you are not being reasonable."

She had the brass clamp on a cloth in the middle of the bench, and it was uglier than it had sounded in a witness's mouth: about the length of a hand, dull from use, a screw at one end and a jaw at the other with a channel cut into the jaw that ran the length of it like a groove in a table.

"Situation," Mara said, and used her hands rather than a form. "Jules Ardent's bench, nine Pell Street, four this morning. Transfer form signed by him and witnessed by your Inspector Kest, who I like enormously and who also wrote 'transferred, not seized' in her own handwriting, which is the single most useful sentence anybody in this city has written this week. The tool is in this room, on a loan from Mr Ardent's bench, and I would like it to be understood that it is a loan, and that it comes back."

"When?"

"Midnight." She put a finger on the top sheet. "Which is not a laboratory preference. I want you to read the sheet before I explain it, because I want to know whether you can read a document you do not like."

Elias read it.

The transfer form carried Nadia's notes: a bench-book entry copied verbatim, the terms of the loan as Jules had himself written them on the seventeenth, and beneath it a clause in typed form that somebody at the tool registry had attached to the stamp record and that Jules had never bothered to open, because the tool had been in his hand for four years and a person does not read the fine print on his own work.

What brought that clause down onto all three of them was a line near the top of the same page, which re-issued the tool against rehabilitation account 44-9 for the working day it was moved on. All three of them had signed it without reading, because a signature is a thing you give a man who is in a hurry at four in the morning.

*Tool issued against rehabilitation account 44-9 is returnable to the issuing account at the close of the working day of issue. On default, the tool reverts to the account and the account holder may enter the premises of any party in temporary custody of it.*

"So we have until midnight," Elias said.

"You have until midnight. I have until midnight and four instruments." She took the sheet back. "Which is why I need a police signature on the custody line, and by police signature I mean a named officer in the room or on a live line, and there is one more thing about the custody line which is the part I would like to be unkind about, and I am going to be unkind about it now so that I do not have to be later."

"Elias."

"Your Inspector Kest signed the transfer at ten past four this morning. A custody signature is not the same as a transfer signature. The transfer moved the tool. The custody signature says that a named police officer has taken responsibility for the sample, in the room, with the log running, and that everything that happens to it from this second is on that officer's name. That is not paperwork. That is a promise to whoever this belongs to that nothing unrecorded will touch it."

"And the only officer free is Nadia."

"Your Inspector Kest is on the second floor of this hospital holding a witness named Rafi Ilori, who is a porter at the Kettle Rows night market, who has been talking for five hours and will not stop, and whom she cannot leave because he is the only person in the city who has told us that he saw somebody on that stair and is now contradicting himself about the hour." Mara's voice did not change. "I have asked her for twenty minutes. She has offered me ten. Neither of us can leave."

"Then I can be that officer."

"No."

"I can be that officer. I am a police officer. I am—"

"You are the man whose gift is *contact*, Elias."

The room was very quiet, and the hood made its noise, and Elias understood with a clarity that was not unwelcome that she was right and that he was about to argue anyway, which was the most useful information anyone had given him all day.

"All right," he said.

"Say the rest of it."

"All right. I am the thing the clause is written about. If I am the officer of record, then every number you write this evening is a number with my handprint in it, and every error in it is mine, and no matter how carefully you do this the sample will have a civilian's touch in its history. And the Registry will use that. Not next month. At the hearing. And the thing the Registry has in its hand is a tool with a suspicious six-month history in a district with a dead woman in it, and a custody line with a civilian in it, and that is enough to get every reading you take tonight thrown out and to get this room's standing questioned."

Mara looked at him for a moment and then put her chin in her hand and something in her face changed very slightly, which from Mara was a standing ovation.

"Yes," she said. "So it is Nadia's name or it is nothing. And the ten minutes she has offered me is eleven in reality, and I want you to do something with them, and I want you to do it at the window, and I want you to do it without coming into this room."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Be useful, and be far away." She handed him a clipboard. "Nineteen forty-one. Read."

---

It was the order, not a schedule, and it was a document of the kind that gets written in a great hurry by a person who wants to be fair and does not want to be blamed.

*In the matter of a working at 14 Kettle Rows, top floor: the Sequencing Desk records that residue, a working frame, and an object associated with the account named in the maintenance ticket are likely to be located at a dispensary and treatment premises in the Kettle Rows district. The Registry directs that such stock be treated as ordinary maintenance stock in the event that premises inspection is not completed before the following morning, and instructs that no item be removed from such premises pending inspection.*

"They are giving me permission to look after they have taken the paperwork," Elias said.

"They are making sure that if anything disappears tonight, it is a clinic employee who loses it and not a police officer who took it. Look at the date on the third paragraph. It is signed at six-fifteen this evening by a Ward Works inspector and it is delivered to the clinic at six-forty and the clinic's night staff is four people, one of whom is seventy-one and one of whom started at nine."

He read it twice. It was not a threat. It was worse than a threat; it was a schedule.

"Thursday morning," he said. "By Thursday morning that dispensary's stockroom will have been attended to by four people and a form, and there will be a note in a ledger saying routine maintenance, and the object will be gone and the object will have never existed."

"Yes."

"And nobody can do anything about it, because it is a lawful instruction."

"Underneath the law, no. Above it, quite a lot." Mara did not look up. "Your clinic is not a Concord facility and Concord inspectors do not have that authority over it, and if a Kettle Rows tenant or a patient stood on the steps and said *no*, there is not a warrant in this city that would make them stand aside by Friday. But nobody will stand there, because a clinic on a Wednesday evening has ninety people in it, and every one of them is here because a certificate has already failed them once, and none of them is going to trade a prescription for a fight with the Proof Registry."

Elias set the clipboard down.

"Sana Okafor will," he said.

"Dr Okafor will be asleep at ten past eleven and awake again at five, because she has been awake at five o'clock in the morning for nineteen years. Do not build a plan on a person being brave at eleven at night." Mara put her pencil down. "Go to the window. You have four minutes. And Elias — when you come back, your hands stay in your pockets until I tell you they are allowed out. Because I have seen what you do when you do not know what you are looking at."

---

He went to the window, and he was useful for eleven minutes, which he had not been for four months.

There is a thing a detective can do at a window that almost nobody does, and it is this: he stops being the man in the case and becomes the man who knows the shapes of the shapes. He pulled a chair round, sat down, and made a list of every route by which a physically carried object could leave Saint Orra General between eight this evening and eight tomorrow morning — the service ramp, the linen lift, the pharmacy hatch, the ambulance bay on Vine Street, which everybody forgot about and which had a camera that had been broken since the previous winter and had an eleven-week-old repair order on it.

He wrote the list on the back of a form, and then he wrote the four names in the case who had a reason to want an object to travel, and then he wrote, underneath, in a hand that got worse as it went:

*Whose order is it to be there? If the Registry wants the stockroom papered by morning, the Registry knows the stockroom. Registry knows the stockroom => somebody told the Registry where to point. Somebody told the Registry. Who does the Registry take that from?*

And then, underneath that:

*Nadia has Rafi Ilori. Rafi Ilori saw somebody on the stair. Rafi Ilori is on the second floor of this hospital and cannot be left. If I am in a building that a man stood and watched last night, then somebody else is not being asked anything, and I should be where somebody can see me.*

He read that last sentence twice and it stayed true, and he got up and went and stood in the corridor outside the laboratory with his hands in his pockets and waited for Mara Quill to finish her ten minutes with the room.

---

Inside, Mara had laid the threshold plate and run the first comparison, and the comparison was good, and the clamp was on the cloth, and the room was ready, and Nadia was not there.

At 8:52 Mara called him in.

"Gloves on," she said. "Left hand. No, the other glove, the thinner one, and no, you may touch the screw and you may not touch the channel. Say it back."

"The screw, not the channel."

"Good. And this is a comparison. You are not going to perceive anything. I am going to run a plate over the jaw and the channel and the stamped face and the screw, and it is going to tell me whether the order in that brass is the order that was in her room, and you are going to stand there and be quiet so that my numbers are clean."

The hood was on. Mara's hands went into the plate the way a reader's hands go into a book.

"Numbers," she said, at intervals. "Nine-tenths of a second, which is ordinary wear. Two-tenths at the jaw lip, which is somebody pushing it sideways. Nothing in the channel. The channel is clean."

"Clean means what?"

"Clean means nothing has been laid in it. Which is disappointing, and which I wrote down before you asked." She moved the plate. "Now the stamped face, and there is a faint thermal differential across 4417, and that is where somebody had their hand, and the hand was warm, and the differential is the age of the hand, and the age of the hand is—"

The plate went over on its side.

Elias had been leaning on the bench.

It was not leaning on the bench. His hand was on the bench, flat, over the channel, and he had no memory of putting it there, and Mara was saying a word in a voice that was not raised, and the whole room had gone the way it went in the small hours in a stairwell on Monday: not dark, but *elsewhere*, with the extractor fan arriving after the rest of it and a smell of scorched salt that had no business being in a laboratory four storeys from a room where nothing was burning.

The clamp was not a room. Nothing was a room.

There was an order, and he knew this one, and it was hers, and it was running in brass.

It started in an address and it went to a person and it turned a person, and it came back to a channel, and it sat down in the channel the way a hand sits down in a mould, and it *fitted* — that was the horror of it, the thing he would not be able to put down for days — it fitted because it had been cut for it, because somebody had measured this jaw and cut a channel to match it, and the fitting was so much more precise than anything a living hand produces that the order in the brass was smoother than the order in the room.

And then it came to the end.

And the end was wrong.

It went to the release and the release did not arrive. It went to the place where the release should be, and there was a shape there, a late shape, a second thing that was not this working, and this one and the second one arrived at the same joint and they did not both fit, and the channel gave up in the middle of the mouth of the jaw, and the order stopped in a way that a working does not stop, and it was the same failure, the same joint, the same half-finished joint that had happened in the desk of a dead woman's room in a building where he lived.

The last thing before the floor came up was the person. The address in the brass was not a shape any more. It was a man of twenty-four who had put his hand flat on a table in a room above a tax office, and who was standing in a doorway, and who was the only living thing in the entire order.

Then the tile, and a cold hand on his collar, and Mara's voice arriving from a very long way off.

"Elias. Elias. Do not move your hand."

His hand was not on the clamp.

His hand was on the clamp, flat, over the channel, with the glove on it, and the glove was not on the clamp any more; the glove was on the bench and the clamp was on the bench and the glove was two inches to the left, and the place where the glove had been on the clamp had gone warm, and warm in a laboratory bench, at eight fifty-three in the evening, meant something had been laid down in a place that had measured as empty ninety seconds before.

"You put your hand on it," Mara said. She was not shouting. She was doing something with a cloth and a pen. "Elias. Look at me. You put your hand on the channel of a sample in a working laboratory, unlogged, eleven minutes after I asked you to stand in a corridor, and you did it while my plate was running, and you did it in the four seconds when the plate was over the stamped face and not over the channel."

"It came—"

"I know what came. I have been doing this for eleven years and I know exactly what came, and I am telling you what happened in my laboratory, and I am going to be the one who says it, and I want you to hear the sentence in the right order." She put the pen down. "You have put a living hand's contact into the middle of a residue I had measured as empty. I do not know what you added. I know it was not part of any working that I had recorded, and I know that after this moment every number I take from that clamp describes a surface that has a man in it. The channel is finished. Not ruined — finished. It can never be measured again by anybody, and the reason is a person."

Elias got himself onto the stool.

The room was very bright and very far away and there was a taste in his mouth like a coin.

"Two hours," Mara said. "You are going to shake for two hours and you are not going to be able to keep anything down, and I am not going to pretend that is the worst of it, because the worst of it is at midnight and it is a courier with a van." She put the cloth over the clamp and left her hand flat on top of it. "You do not touch that again. Not tonight, not this week, and not while I am standing here. It is mine and it is the case's, and you are a man who is permitted to stand in the room."

After that, in the small hours, he lost the ability to keep his hands to himself and the ability to keep water down, and his whole body did a thing it would not stop doing, and the thing it kept doing was remembering an order in brass that had a living person's name in it.

He said it to Mara once, in the corridor, at midnight, with the courier waiting downstairs:

"It didn't finish. It got to the release and the release did not come, and it broke at the same joint it broke at in her room, and that is not a coincidence, that is a *specification*. Whoever cut that channel is cutting to a pattern and the pattern fails at the same place, and the failing is in the pattern and not in her. Which means whoever did this did it wrong, and did it wrong the same way, twice, and I am the only man in this city who knows the shape of the wrong, and I cannot prove one word of it, and it is going to stay that way for a very long time."

"Then it will have to stay that way," said Mara, "with my name on it and not yours, because yours is not a thing that can be put in front of a tribunal." She looked at the sealed bag. "And Niko Senn is in the clamp, and Niko Senn is on my screen, and Niko Senn is not a thing you get to use because he is in a piece of brass."

"Nadia is still upstairs?"

"Since five. She has sent down twice. Both times for a chit, not for a decision." Mara picked up the bag. "Go home. Do not touch the plate either. Do not touch the door of your own building. Sit somewhere with no doors in it and be sick in private, and tomorrow, when somebody asks you what the channel read, you will say that it was too fresh to compare and that I said so. And I will have said so, in writing, at eight fifty-two this evening, before your hand was anywhere near it."

At the foot of the stairs, in the cold, with a courier drinking tea out of a paper cup and a sealed bag of brass in a box going out to a post code in the outer wards, Elias understood two things.

The first was that Jules Ardent was going to drive that box himself, because he had written a promise in a bench book and he was going to keep it, and that a good man keeping a promise was the most dangerous thing in this case.

The second was that the printed file on Mara's desk, which he had not been near, which he was not permitted to be near, and which he had photographed from a chair in a corridor like a man in a story about a man like him, had a name in it. Niko Senn had been booked into a rehabilitation clinic on Verge Row through an account that had no patient in it, on a date he was certain he had never attended, for a treatment he did not remember, and a transfer notice on that booking had come out of the printer in that building at six o'clock that evening, initialled at the counter and waiting for a signature that nobody had yet been in a position to refuse.

Elias stood on the steps of Saint Orra Reach and did not go home, because there was one person awake at eleven forty-five at night in the whole of the Kettle Rows district who was a doctor and not a criminal, and she had ninety people in her building and a four-page instruction in her hands, and it was not going to be her night to hold the stair.

He got in a taxi and paid the driver to take him the long way, so that it was nearly midnight when he arrived, and by then the courier's van with a box of brass in it was out on Marshall Road, turning off towards the ring road and the outer wards, with a heat-sensitive label on the side and a signature on the manifest belonging to a man whose name was not Jules Ardent's.
