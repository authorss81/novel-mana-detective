# Chapter 0046 — The Tuesday Session

Jules Ardent had been standing in treatment room two since a quarter past two with a cold chisel in his right hand and a steel drift in his left, waiting, and by ten to three he had put them in his coat pockets twice and taken them out twice, and the fourth time he left them in the pocket and said, out loud, to an empty room, "It is nine seconds. I have been doing it four thousand times and I have never once done it in a hurry."

He said it because room two had a couch and a screen and no window and the door shut, and because the light in there was the good kind, and because it was the only sentence he had that made the room a room where a mechanic worked instead of a room where something was about to happen to an old man.

The clinic had been running since one o'clock. Four staff, forty-three on the list, forty-one in the building, the main room with twenty-two chairs and nineteen people standing up in it because the chairs had gone at half past two. The rain had come in under the yard door and the floor by the mats had the shine of a public swimming pool. Ahmed was on the desk with a stack of receipt backs and a pen, because Dr Okafor had never given him a proper book and he had never once asked for one.

The dispensary counter ward light went out at twenty-two minutes past two and came back.

Jules Ardent, in the corridor outside the dispensary with his hands in his pockets, said, "One."

Nobody had asked him.

---

The main room went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when a doctor stands up in it, and then it went loud, and then it went onto the covered walkway and out into the rain.

It took her ninety seconds. Dr Okafor did not use a whistle and did not use a bell and did not say the word fire. She walked to the middle of the main room at half past two and said, in the voice she used for the seventy-eight-year-old in the back row, "Ladies and gentlemen, I am going to move you all out into the covered walkway for about four minutes while I take the dispensary off the circuit. If you are on your feet, stay on your feet and go out. If you are on a chair, get up when the row in front of you gets up and do not go faster than the person in front of you. Ahmed will be at the door counting you in and out. Nobody is in any trouble. This is about the light and not about you."

Forty-one people went out into the rain in ninety seconds, and Ahmed counted them out on the back of a receipt and got forty-one, and stood at the door and counted them back in four minutes later and got forty-one.

"It was the light," said Ahmed afterwards, to nobody, twice, to two different people, and he was right and it was not the whole of it.

Elias Venn was in the corridor outside room two with a folded sheet of A4 in his inside pocket and his hands empty. Mara Quill was at the end of the corridor with her case closed and her hood over her head, where she had been since half past one and where she was going to stay, because she had told him on the Saturday that she was not going to be in a treatment room with a patient of hers in it and had then said the reason, which was that she was the only person in this city who was going to have to tell Alfred Neve afterwards what had been inside his walking stick, and she could not do that from behind a screen with a plate in her hand.

Detective Inspector Nadia Kest came in at twenty past two with a tin under her arm, and put the tin on a chair outside room two, and opened her notebook, and wrote the time, and said, "Nine hours ago I was in a corridor at Morrow Hill with a plan I did not want. It has been read aloud and minuted and it is signed by Rennick and me, and there is a line in it in his handwriting saying you are not a witness to it. I am telling you that before it matters, and not after, so that you cannot say afterwards that you did not know."

"I read it at six o'clock this morning," said Elias.

"You read it at six o'clock this morning," said Nadia Kest, "and Rennick wrote that line at twenty past eleven, and I am telling you that I asked him to write it and that he wrote it because I asked, and that is the whole of my contribution to what happens in the next ten minutes."

The third slot was called at 14:36 and Alfred Neve came in on his own, with a walking stick.

He had it in his right hand. He always had it in his right hand; that was how he had come in the first time, on the twenty-fifth of October, and he had it the same way now, and he sat down on the couch in the middle of the room, and when the nurse asked him to he put the stick on the chair beside him with the ferrule end toward the door, because that is where a man puts the end of a stick.

Nurse Adeyemi had the tray. She had two hundred of those injections in her and she had the patient's name on the label in her own hand and she checked it twice, because that is what the form says.

What was in his head in that corridor, with his hands empty and forty-one people in the room behind him, was Niko Senn, and it was this: *he is not in this building and he is not on any list in it, and if anybody in this corridor has a piece of paper with his name on it I would like to hear about it now and not in November, and I am not going to read him and I am not going to touch him, and I am not going to ask anybody to bring him here.*

He said it out loud, in the corridor, to four people, at 14:36 in the afternoon on the thirtieth of October, and Jules Ardent said, "Nobody," and Nadia Kest said, "Not in this building," and Mara Quill said nothing at all, which was the answer that meant she had checked her own case in her own head and found it clear.

Nurse Adeyemi put the injection in at 14:38.

At 14:38 Alfred Neve said, "That is a funny one," in the voice of a man remarking on the weather, and put his left hand out flat on the couch, and then his right hand went off the arm of the couch and down onto the ash shaft where it lay on the chair beside him, and his face did a thing that Dr Okafor, who was six feet away, saw before anybody else in that corridor did.

"He is grey," said Sana Okafor. "He is going. Everybody out of this corridor — *now* — and nobody in this corridor is to touch him, and you—" to Elias Venn, "you go in and you do not touch him, and you are going to tell me why in about ninety seconds, and if you cannot tell me in ninety seconds I will put him on a trolley and you can tell me on the ward."

She went into room two, and the room went, in about forty seconds, from a doctor and a patient and a nurse to a couch with a man on it and a doctor and a nurse, and the rest of the corridor was empty.

Elias Venn went in.

---

He put his hand flat on the shaft of the walking stick where it lay on the chair beside a man of seventy-eight, and he did not touch the man, and the room came up out of the floor the way it always did, and it was not a room.

The ash was a length of wood that had been in this house for nine years and it was hot in a way that ash is hot when it is holding something, and there was no furniture in it and there were no faces in it and there was a man in it who was *not* the man on the couch and never had been.

There was a bracket, which was a licensed one, seated in a socket in the wood, and the bracket had been fitted on Wednesday the twenty-fourth of October at ten past two in the afternoon in this house by a man with a card with four lines on it, and the bracket had been bound, and addressed, and had turned, and had been released, in this kitchen, in front of a kettle, six days ago, and all four of those were a man's own and were done properly and were in his body this afternoon.

And then there was a lid on it.

> **MARGIN — The turn is his. The release came in from somewhere else. It is in the metal, and nothing in this room will stop it but the metal.**

Elias Venn came off the wood and put his back against the wall of a treatment room in the Kettle Rows Community Clinic and found that his legs were doing the thing they did, and said, out loud, in order, so that somebody else would do the arithmetic.

"Anchor. His stick. Nine years in this house, and a bracket fitted on Wednesday, and that is genuinely his, and I am not reading the man, I am reading the wood, and I want that on the tape. Address. Not the room. **The record.** It is waiting for something to be written down about him and it is not waiting for a body. Turn. Real, complete, done in this kitchen on Friday by a man in a good coat, and it is his, and it is four inches of channel section sitting on the top of it that is not. Release." He was breathing through his mouth. "The release is seated. It is in the sleeve. It has no settling curve, it was made somewhere else and brought here, and it is not going to happen when a bracket reaches its stop, it is going to happen when somebody writes down that a man of seventy-eight has had an injection at 14:38 this afternoon."

"And can you stop it," said Nadia Kest, from the doorway.

"No," said Elias Venn. "I have told you six times since September that I cannot stop a working and I am telling you in front of a witness in a room where a man of seventy-eight is on a couch. **I cannot stop it. There is nothing I can do to that man and there is nothing I can do to that stick. I can tell you the order of it.**"

"Then what is the plan."

"The plan is the metal," said Elias Venn. "The channel is in four inches of channel section and it is bonded over the bracket and the bracket is in the ash. If the channel comes out of the ash, then the release goes out of the channel, and there is nothing there for it to be a release *into*, and it is a piece of split ash with a hole in it. That is the whole of the plan. It has been the whole of the plan since Monday. I have got no other idea and I have had eleven days to get one."

Jules Ardent was already on one knee in front of the couch with his hand flat on the ash shaft so that it would not roll off the chair, and he said, "Then I am doing it," and Mara Quill, from the doorway, said, "Do it now and not after you have finished talking," and Elias Venn said, "Do it now," and Jules Ardent put the chisel against the rivet head and tapped it four times.

The rivet head came up. The ferrule came off in his hand. The sleeve came out of the socket with two fingers.

He had it in the air, and turned, and put it in the tin, and the tin was on a chair by the door and he put his hand in and lifted the lid with his thumb, and the sleeve went in, and he put the lid down, and the tin was closed.

"Time," said Nadia Kest, and she was already looking at her own watch and she said it before anybody else asked her to.

"Fourteen forty-seven," she said. "And it started at fourteen thirty-eight."

"Which is nine minutes," said Jules Ardent, from the floor, "and I have never in my life taken that long to do anything, and I was not slow, and I want that written down, because somebody is going to write down that I hesitated and I did not."

"You were not slow," said Mara Quill. "You had to wait four minutes for the doctor."

"They were the four longest of my life and I have not told anybody that and I am not going to start on a Tuesday."

---

The dispensary counter ward light went out again at 14:47 and came back, and stayed on, and nobody turned round.

That line had been off a wall since the fourth of October, because a man with a ladder and a borrowed tool and two brass saddle clips that had come out of a broken conduit in a ceiling had re-hung it at four o'clock in the morning on the twenty-second of September and would not be paid for it and would not have his name on it. It flickered twice in five weeks and Jules Ardent had come in both times and had looked at it and said nothing, and it held.

---

At 14:44, a man came into the corridor from the covered walkway.

He was on the venue list, which was pinned by the dispensary counter and typed on the agency machine on Thursday, and his line said fourteen forty-five, and he came in at 14:44, which was a minute early, and Ahmed looked at the line, and looked at the man, and let him in, because Ahmed had been on that desk for eleven years and had never once asked anybody on a list for anything.

He was not described afterwards by anybody who was in the corridor, and that is not a gap in the account, it is what happened: there were five people in that corridor and a nurse inside a room and one of them was on the floor with his hand on a piece of wood, and the one who saw the man best was a man of fifty-odd with a pen and a stack of receipt backs.

He stood against the wall by the yard door for eight minutes with a canvas holdall on the floor between his feet.

At 14:52 he took Alfred Neve's walking stick off the chair beside the couch, where the old man had put it down himself at 14:36 because the nurse had asked him to, and he put it under his arm, and he went out into the yard, and the yard gate is a gate and not a door and it was open because it rains, and it took about four seconds.

Elias Venn was on the floor of a treatment room with one hand on a shaft. Jules Ardent was holding his other wrist, because Elias Venn had gone down onto his side at some point in the fourth minute and had not noticed, and had said *don't let go of that* twice without opening his eyes. Mara Quill was saying seconds out loud in a flat voice from the doorway, and had been since 14:38, and had got past five hundred and forty. Nadia Kest was six feet away with a notebook and a recorder and a tin on a chair and had not gone to the door, and said afterwards that this was the single hardest thing she had done in twenty-two years and that she would not have believed it in advance.

"You could have gone out there," said Elias Venn.

"I could have gone out there and left a room with a man on a couch and a device in a tin and one officer," said Nadia Kest. "That is a choice and I made it at fourteen fifty-two and I would make it again, and there is a witness to it and it is you, and you may complain about it in about a year."

---

Ahmed timed it.

He had his watch out and he had it out because the dispensary counter ward light had gone twice and he had decided, standing at a desk for eleven years, that if a thing was going to happen in his building he was going to know how long it took.

"From when the old gent went down on the couch," he said, on Wednesday morning, to three people separately, and then again on Friday, "to when the gate went. Fourteen minutes. Fourteen exactly, near enough, and I would have said fourteen and I would have said it twice because I have got nothing else to do and because the men in the alley were shouting about a door."

"You did not ask him anything," said Nadia Kest.

"He was on the list," said Ahmed. "I have never once asked anybody on a list for anything. That is the job."

---

Nurse Adeyemi had Neve on a trolley with oxygen by twenty past three and his pulse was there and his colour came back, and Dr Okafor said the word *vasovagal* twice and then said, "It is not vasovagal," to nobody, and did not say what it was.

Nadia Kest photographed the sleeve laid on the lid of the tin with a steel rule beside it and the time off the camera on the back of a receipt stub, and then wrote on the stub: *Item recovered 30 Oct 2029 14:47, Kettle Rows Community Clinic treatment room two, one length of channel section, split-ash seat, bonded to licensed bracket, owner present. Transferred, not seized, to M. Quill, residue laboratory, Saint Orra Reach, on the written consent of the owner dated 27 Oct 2029, in the presence of the undersigned and of D. Sgt E. Venn, member of the public. No person was examined. No living trace was entered.*

And at 15:20, with the room empty and the corridor empty and the rain still going on the covered walkway where forty-one people had stood for four minutes about two hours earlier, Mara Quill said the only sentence that mattered to her that afternoon, to a man of seventy-eight on a trolley who was asking about his coat.

"Mr Neve. Your stick was taken out of the room at eight minutes to three this afternoon by a man with a list, and it is not here, and I am not going to be able to tell you that to your satisfaction, and I would like to help you understand that I am not saying it to your satisfaction on purpose."

Alfred Neve looked at her for a long moment, on oxygen, at seventy-eight years of age.

"Young woman," he said. "You will get me another one."
