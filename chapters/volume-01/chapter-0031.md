# Chapter 0031 — The Drawing Exists

The bell over the shop door rang at nine on Monday morning and Elias Venn looked up from a toaster with its back off to find Halvard Reisz standing in the doorway with a folded paper in his hand and the expression of a man who had rehearsed being reasonable.

"Mr Venn. The ward certificate," said Reisz. "The assessor wants the shop's maintenance record for the shared circuits. Not the flat. The shop."

Elias put the screwdriver down on the bench where he could see it. The back room stood open behind him with the carrier bag on the bench under the window and the folio under it, and Otto Vahl's radio bench in the front window with the cat asleep on the folded blanket. The ceiling made its wasp noise and then stopped.

"Good morning," said Elias. "What record?"

"The trade accounts." Reisz unfolded the paper and did not hand it over. "He says a repair shop that holds trade accounts for ward material is a premises that handles ward components. He says if you handle components you need the component schedule. I told him you mend kettles."

"I mend kettles," said Elias. "And radios, when Otto lets me."

"He wants the Factors slips." Reisz looked at the bench, at the bag, at the cat. "Cutter Street Factors. Twenty years you have had that account, and now a man I have never met wants to know whether the man who mends kettles buys channel section. I told him you do not. I would like to go on telling him that."

Elias wiped his hands on a rag. The Factors account was a book with carbon slips and a monthly statement, kept in the drawer under the till. Channel section had never gone through it. Foil had never gone through it. The last entry was a gross of fuse wire and two elements for a wash boiler, eleven days ago.

"You can go on telling him that," said Elias. "The shop buys wire and elements. Nothing that holds a working."

Reisz folded the paper again. "Then write me that. One line. So I can show him."

He wrote it on the back of a repair docket with the date, 15 October 2029, and his name, and Reisz took it and went out into Kettle Rows with his shoulders a fraction lower than they had come in. The bell rang and did not ring again for an hour.

Elias stood in the shop with the toaster open in front of him and understood that the fortnight had started taking things in a different order from the one he had expected. Not the file, which was gone, and not the board, which belonged to somebody else. The trade book in the drawer under the till. The lawful income that paid the rent on the rooms above it.

He closed the toaster, tested it, and put it on the shelf for collection. Then he took the stamped copy of tab 61 out of the folio in the back room, looked at the reference line in the title block until he had it word for word, and copied it into the notebook with the date under it.

*DRG HW-CS-03, Sheet 1 of 2, Issue B. Issued 30 Sep 2029.*

Under that, because a man who writes a reference without writing what it is for will forget why he wrote it, he added: *Drawing office reference. Register behind a counter. Needs a case number.*

And under that, because the batch-0003 review had taught him to write the thing he was not going to do: *Niko Senn is not a route to a counter. Do not borrow a standing.*

He put the stamped copy back in the folio. He did not take it out of the shop.

Otto Vahl came through from the front with a chassis in both hands and set it on the back bench with the care of a man handling somebody else's livelihood.

"Reisz?" said Otto.

"Wants one line for the assessor," said Elias. "Shop buys wire and elements. Nothing that holds a working."

Otto grunted. He had repaired radios in the back of that shop for eleven years, through three owners of the building and two recessions in the parts trade, and he knew what a Factors book meant. The drawer under the till held the carbon slips in date order, the statements filed behind with a bulldog clip, the handwriting changing twice where the Factors had changed clerks. Twenty years of fuse wire, elements, flex, plugs, solder. Nothing that had ever needed a licence class.

"He will be back Thursday with a schedule," said Otto. "The assessor. Wants the slips."

"Then he brings the schedule to me first," said Elias. "Drawer sticks until then."

"It sticks," said Otto, and went back to the front, where the cat had claimed the warm spot by the urn and was ignoring a customer who wanted a kettle element before Friday.

Elias worked the morning bench. A toaster, a wash boiler, two irons. The work was ordinary and it steadied him in a way the file never had. At half past twelve he ate bread and cheese standing at the back bench with the notebook open beside him, and wrote nothing, because there was nothing new to write. The reference line sat on the page in his small bad hand with the date under it, and the line about Niko Senn under that, and the two together were the whole of his standing: a thread he could see and a man he would not use to pull it.

At two he walked to Marshall Street for solder and stood for a while outside the clinic, looking at the waiting-room windows without going in. The board was in there with ninety-odd copied pages on the left and the registrar's transfer order on the right and four sheets of his own in the middle, flat under four drawing pins. Ahmed had rung last night to say two men had looked at it twice and touched nothing. He did not go in to look at it himself. A member of the public with no file does not stand in front of his own paper in a clinic corridor in the middle of the afternoon.

He bought the solder, walked back by way of Tanager Street without stopping at the depot, and was at the bench by three. The depot appeared in his notebook that afternoon only as a line he drew a box round: *M.O. — will not write. Named absence.*

---

Jules Ardent opened the door of 9 Pell Street at ten to five with a rag in his hand and a smear of oil on his forearm, and looked at Elias for a long moment before he stepped back.

"You had better come in off the step," he said. "The notice is still on the door and people read it on the way past."

The standard clinical practice notice was wired to the handle with the seal showing. Elias read it without stopping, the way you read a thing you have already read on a wall eleven feet away from the man it names. Jules took him through to the scullery with the bench in it, where the kettle was already hot, and poured two mugs without asking.

"I have had the assessor," said Jules. "And the Tool Registry. And a man from the contracts office who wanted to know whether I do continuity units." He sat down. "I told them all the same thing, which is that there is a notice on my door and a bench room I cannot open and a die block in a building I cannot get into. It is a short conversation. They keep having it."

Elias put his hands round the mug. The room smelled of cutting oil and tea.

"I telephoned you from a call box on Monday last," said Elias. "I asked you one sentence about a drawing."

"You asked me whether *per* something is a dimension." Jules looked at him. "I said it is a referral to another document."

"I am here to tell you there is a drawing." Elias said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it to a man who had supplied the sentence that opened it. "Carrier specification CS-3, issued the thirtieth of September, sheet one of two. Channel section with a seat in it. The seat has no dimension on it. The note says *per description relied on* and gives a depth. The channel agrees with the stockroom carrier in width, wall and lip."

Jules did not move. Then he put his mug down.

"And you are not going to tell me what is on it beyond the reference."

"I am going to tell you the reference line and nothing else." Elias took the notebook out, opened it to the page, and turned it round so Jules could read it without touching it. "DRG HW-CS-03, Sheet one of two, Issue B. Material callout for channel and foil. That is all I am giving you for a week, and that is not distrust. I have read four hundred pages in a day and I would like one of them to be mine."

Jules read the line twice.

"Issue B," he said. "Then there was an Issue A. A title block gives a hand and a date, Elias. It gives who drew it and when and what stock it is cut from. It does not give why."

"That is what I needed," said Elias. "One sentence about what a title block can and cannot say. I have got it now."

They sat for a while with the kettle ticking behind them. Somewhere above them a floorboard went.

"Somebody is going to ask me about a drawing," said Jules at last. "Friday or the week after. I said that on the telephone and I am saying it again with a notice on my door. I would rather hear it from you than from a notice, and I would rather you told me which notice."

"I will," said Elias. "When I know which notice it is, I will come to this door and tell you before it does."

Jules nodded once. Then he said, in the voice he used for metal, "A physical explanation is not a mystical answer, Elias. If somebody drew a seat with no dimension on it, somebody did not have a number. Somebody had a description. That is all that sentence means, and it means it in any shop in this city."

"I know."

"And if you go behind a counter to read who drew it, you need a case number." Jules picked his mug up. "I know that too, because Bess Yonkin told me in nineteen years she has never telephoned the Desk, and then she did. A register opens for an officer. You are not an officer."

"No."

"Then how?"

"I do not know yet." Elias closed the notebook. "But I am not bringing a young man of twenty-four into it to get one. Niko Senn has a card in his pocket and a withdrawal in a file and an appointment on Thursday morning, and none of those is a standing I can borrow."

Jules looked at him for a long moment, and something in his shoulders eased by a fraction.

"That is the first thing anybody has said in a fortnight that makes me think you will not get us all sealed into something," he said. "If an officer opens the book, I will come to the counter and read the channel with you. I will not touch the book. I will read at arm's length with my hands where the keeper can see them. That is the whole of what I will do."

"That is the whole of what I am asking."

Elias stood. Jules walked him to the door and stood in the doorway with the notice ticking against the glass in the wind off the street.

"Elias," he said. "The seat in that drawing. If it is finer than anything I ever cut —"

"It is not yours until somebody measures it," said Elias. "And nobody has measured it yet."

He walked back to Kettle Rows in the dark with the notebook in his inside pocket and the stamped copy still in the folio in the back room where he had left it. Otto Vahl was at the front bench with a chassis open, and the cat had moved from the blanket to the warm spot by the urn.

"Assessor again Thursday," said Otto without looking up. "He wants the Factors slips. I told him the drawer sticks."

"It sticks," said Elias, and went through to the back room and stood for a while looking at the carrier bag on the bench under the window.

He took the notebook out and wrote under the morning's lines: *Jules told a drawing exists, 15 Oct. Reference only. He will attend a counter if an officer opens the book. Issue A exists.*

Then he wrote the time, 18:20, and put the notebook on the shelf above the bench where it had lived for eight days. The cat came in from the front, looked at the bag, and went back out.

The shop telephone rang at half past seven. It was Priya Sandoval from the Kettle Rows station, to say a woman had been at the counter asking whether the four sheets on the clinic board were a police notice, and the desk had told her correctly that they were not, they were a member of the public's paper on a third party's wall. Elias thanked her, wrote *board asked about at station, told correctly, 15 Oct 19:30* in the notebook, and put the pen down.

He ate soup standing up, washed the pot, and sat for a while with the receipt stub in his wallet in his hand. The stub said *received as a document, not evidence, not a note on any matter, no file*, and on the back in Nadia Kest's hand where it recorded the single copy on the clinic wall. A photograph off that wall sat in Paul Rennick's pocket, and both of them said it was a photograph and not a copy. The arithmetic of paper in this case was the only thing that had never lied to him.

At half past nine the telephone rang. It was the clinic, and it was not Dr Okafor. It was the night porter, Ahmed, to say that the waiting-room board had been looked at twice that day by two different men, neither of whom had touched anything, and that he had written both times in the book.

Elias thanked him, wrote *board looked at twice, 15 Oct, touched nothing* in the notebook with the time, and sat for a while in the back room with the light off.

He had told one man one reference line. He had been told one thing about title blocks. He had no case number and no officer and no bench. What he had was a register that existed behind a counter on Cutter Street, kept by somebody who kept it for makers who paid for drawings, and a week in which to find a lawful way to have it read without moving anything in it.
