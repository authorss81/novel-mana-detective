# Chapter 0027 — The Back Room

Otto Vahl's radio bench had been in the back room of Venn Repairs for four years and it had to come out of it, and it was not the bench that was the problem.

"It goes in the front," said Otto Vahl. "By the window. I can work at the window, I've got a light, I've worked at a window for nine years in a different building."

"You could work in the yard."

"I could work in the yard in July." He was coiled over a chassis with a fault he had been chasing since Wednesday, and the ceiling had been making a noise like a wasp since Wednesday, and neither of those things had changed. "Elias, I'll say this once and then get on. I've had your board up in here six years and never once asked what was on it, and I'm not going to start. But there's a room in this building where you keep a book that's got a woman's death in it, and about nine people on this street would let a policeman in there without a form, and one of them's the cat."

"The cat's on your bench."

"The cat's on my bench because the cat's been on my bench since she died and there's nowhere else in this shop for a cat to be that isn't in the way of you or me," said Otto Vahl. "So the cat's on the bench, and the bench goes in the front, and you get the back room, and I'm not having the cat moved, and that's the end of it."

"That's the end of it," said Elias Venn.

"Good." Otto Vahl went back to the chassis. "There's tea in the urn. It's yours now, which is the whole of what I've got to say about it."

Halvard Reisz came up his own stairs at half past ten on Sunday morning with a sheet of paper in a plastic wallet and stood on the landing in his coat.

"I want to ask you one question and I'd rather have the honest answer than a nice one. Does the file touch the shop. The certificate on the block expires in March and the ward contract's been cut since August and I've done my own arithmetic and it doesn't add up and I haven't got anybody to give it to."

"I don't know."

"You were the officer."

"I was the officer on the seventeenth to the thirty-first of September and I was relieved of it at forty to twelve on Friday morning and I have not seen a document about this building since the Friday before last." Elias Venn stood in the doorway of the back room with the door open behind him. "Mr Reisz, one of those two things is an answer and it is not the one you wanted. If I did know, I could not tell you, because I have no standing, and a man with no standing who tells a landlord something is a man with a motive, and that is a sentence somebody will write about me in about four months."

"Then I'll ask somebody else."

"Ask a landlord's association. Ask a firm of solicitors who do ward certificates. Don't ask a department and don't ask a doctor, because a doctor will tell you the truth in a corridor and it will be in somebody's file by Tuesday." He put his hand on the door frame and left it there for a second. "And don't ask me again in a month, because if you ask twice it's a pattern, and a pattern is a thing people build things out of."

"You've gone thin," said Halvard Reisz.

"I've been off a case since Friday, which is the first time in eleven years, and I am going to go and read a book."

---

He read it from about eleven until the light went, in the back room, at the table under the window, with the door shut and the urn from the shop on the second bench, and it is the only thing he did on the seventh of October 2029 that was not an errand.

Part Three had eleven modules and he had read four of them. He read the other seven, in order, in the small bad hand, with the module and the page against each one, and about a third of the way through the afternoon he got to Module 7 and stopped for a while and then went on and finished it and then went back to it.

**Module 7 — Accounts of Exposure.** Nine pages.

Two thirds of the way down page four, in a paragraph headed *Conduct of the session where the account is an account of an exposure*:

*The session is conducted with the account the person gives. Where the account given is an account of a working the person has been exposed to, the exposure is the anchor, and the order the person is asked to hold is the order the person gives. The office does not supply the order.*

Two lines below it:

*Care is to be taken to distinguish the order perceived from the order expected. The manual does not describe a method of distinguishing them, because no such method is established.*

And on page five, in a box, under a heading that said *Effect of attendance on a subsequent account*:

*Where a person has been exposed and has attended a session, the account thereafter is held to the order. A person who has been given an order by a practitioner and has perceived an order are in the same position, and the office is not able to distinguish them. Where a person withdraws, Practice note W-7 applies. The office notes for the guidance of practitioners that there is no evidence on the file that there was a previous account.*

Elias Venn read the last sentence about nine times and then got up and went and stood in the yard for a while because the room was too warm.

Everything on that page was true, and it was printed, and it was in a book on a wire rack that anybody could buy for three credits, and the woman at the back of Concord Hall had a certificate on the wall for teaching it, and she had told Niko Senn, on the twenty-ninth of September, that nothing she did could put anything into his head, and that was true.

And the sentence at the end of page five was the one that would finish him, because it was not a lie and it was not a threat and it was not even an opinion. It was a note about a research position, in a public book, and what it said was that a man who honestly withdrew an account because he could no longer tell what he had seen had withdrawn the only account there was, because nobody could put a hand on the other one, and Niko Senn's own memory of the seventeenth of September was now, on the only record in existence, something with no evidence behind it at all.

He wrote the three sentences out in the back room in the small bad hand, with the module and the page and the printing, and he dated them, and he did not write what he thought about them anywhere, because he had noticed over fourteen days that the things he did not write down were the things that got used.

---

Mara Quill came into the shop at about ten past four on the afternoon of Sunday the seventh of October, which she had not done in six years, and she came in the way she came into rooms, which is as if the room has been waiting for her and she is confirming it.

"You cannot come to the laboratory," she said.

"No."

"And I cannot come into room two."

"No."

"I am in a shop in Kettle Rows and there is a cat on a bench by the window and a radio that is switched off, and there is no plate in this building and no Concord register and no record of me standing in it, and I would like that noted in whatever you are about to do."

"What am I about to do."

"You are going to publish something," said Mara Quill, "and you are going to tell me that you cannot, and then I am going to tell you that you can, and I am going to be extremely tiresome about it, and that is why I have come now instead of on Wednesday, so that you have a whole evening to be angry at me before anybody's name is on a wall."

He put his hands in his pockets.

"Sit down."

"I will not sit down. If I sit down I will be here for two hours." She stood in the middle of the floor with her coat still on. "Go through it. The names."

"The names."

"The people that a document naming a Registry intake sheet and a certificate number and a practice note makes into a door somebody can knock on." She said it without any drama at all, which was how she said everything that mattered. "Say them out loud. You have not, which means you have not thought about it, and a man who has not thought about it is a man who is about to do it to five."

He stood by the table for a while.

"Niko Senn," he said. "Because his order is in it, and his withdrawal is in it, and a minute from Thursday names him at fourteen Kettle Rows on the sixteenth of September, and a document that puts those three things in one paragraph is a document that describes a young man as a route."

"Sana Okafor. Go on."

"Because of the ninety-odd pages, and because her board is the only public wall anybody in this case has, and because the moment there is something on it that a Registry intake sheet answers, her production stops being her failures and starts being her involvement."

"Jules Ardent. Because he is the only one of us with a form of his own that says he is under enquiry, and a man under a practice notice who is named in a public document is a man who will be given a second notice, and a third, and eventually a room."

"Marguerite Osei. And that one is different from the three."

"That one is different from all four of them, and she is the one I have not been able to stop thinking about since I read a plastic box on a Wednesday." He stopped. "I have been in a municipal depot and read four letters and no names, and if my name goes near that box then somebody in an office is going to ask her for it by post, and she will have to answer, and answering is the part that costs."

"Mara Quill," said the woman standing in the middle of his shop floor. "You have missed one."

"You are not in it."

"I am in it because you are going to write down that a number of mine is the depth in somebody's drawing, and you are not going to write my name anywhere, and the day somebody comes to my door about it they will not need my name." She put her handbag down on the bench, which was the first uncareful thing she had done. "I certified four figures in a room in Concord Hall on the twenty-seventh of September and there is a certified copy in a drawer in that building that I cannot open, and on the day I refused the stencil I asked for it to be minuted word for word, and you know exactly what I said to you on that bench, because I have been waiting a fortnight to see whether you would do it."

"A refusal and a request are the same piece of paper with two names on it," said Elias Venn.

"Yes. And now there is a piece of paper with a number of mine on it that is the depth of a seat in a piece of metal, and my name is not required, and there is no minute in the world that stops anybody using a number."

"Then don't let it in."

"I cannot stop it. I have no register and therefore no standing to say a number is mine, and that is the price of the decision I made in 2019 and I would make it again on a bad day." She picked the handbag up. "I am telling you that I know it, that I am not going to stop you, and that you are not to put my name on a thing going on a wall in a clinic where half the people who come through cannot read."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to write it on a Thursday and then take it out on the Friday, which is the same document." She looked at him. "Elias. I am going to be honest with you, because I have come two rooms into a Kettle Rows shop to do it and I would not have come for a paragraph. Both of us are right. If you publish, you hand the machinery a public, dated, signed, four-sheet document that names an intake sheet, and every frightened person standing next to those four names becomes a person a thing has been done to. If you do not publish, then the only account in the world of what a man of twenty-four holds in his head is an account put there by an office at the back of Concord Hall, and it will be the account, and I will be asked about my four figures by somebody who has read it, and I will be sitting in a room being helpful, because that is what I do."

---

"I can't do it in a file," Elias Venn said. "I haven't had one since twenty to twelve on Friday morning and I couldn't get anybody to take it."

"I know."

"And I can't do it in a report, because a report is a department's document and I am not a department. And I can't put it in a letter to a paper, because a paper will write *police witness trained by the Council* in the first paragraph and a man of twenty-four will be a story in Kettle Rows by Friday and gone by the middle of next month."

"Then what is left."

Elias Venn looked at the table under the window, which was a bench he had been working at since he was twenty-two, with a vice on the end of it and a drawer of screwdrivers under it.

"A note," he said. "By hand, in my own words, with no case number and no form and no route and no addressee, that a member of the public has written and put somewhere a person can read it. Not a request — a request has a route and a route has a reply and I have learned what happens to replies. Not a finding, because I am not entitled to make one, and not a certificate, because there is no such thing and I have been on the wrong side of that particular word for a month."

"Where."

"That is the question, and I have had four days." He put his hand flat on the bench, the way he did, and then took it off. "And the answer is the only public wall in this case that belongs to somebody else, and she will only do it if I can say out loud who it hurts, and I could not do that this morning, and I have not been able to do it since Friday at about ten to eleven."

Mara Quill put her coat back on.

"Then do that part on Wednesday," she said, "in front of nine people, and do not do it in a corridor first. And do not do it in a corridor afterwards either." She got to the door and stopped with her hand on it, in the middle of the shop floor, with a cat asleep on a bench by the window. "And Elias. When you write it — do not put a person's name in. Not one. If a name is in one of those documents, write the document. The minute of Thursday exists and it names him and you have to be able to point at it, and he does not have to be in your four sheets as well."

"That is Mara Quill's minute talking."

"It is a minute taken word for word in a room in Concord Hall on the twenty-seventh of September by a woman who was asked for it," she said, "and I would like it to be useful for once."

---

He wrote it from about seven in the evening until about half past ten, in the small bad hand, on four sheets, and the first thing he wrote at the top was the title, and he got the title right on the fourth attempt:

**A NOTE BY A MEMBER OF THE PUBLIC.**
*On the origin of a description of order.*

*1. The writer is a member of the public. He has no file, no case number, no office, and no standing. He is not making a request and this is not a finding and this is not a certificate, and anybody who processes it as one of those three things has been told in the four lines beneath this one what they have done.*

*2. The writer lives at the address in which the working occurred. He says so first, because it is the first thing that will be said about anything he writes, and it is true.*

*3. The writer's own account of the order of that working is unverified. He has said so in a document that is on a file and he says so again here. He has no instrument and he is not certified and nothing he perceives is evidence.*

*4. What follows is about where a written description of an order came from, and not about how anybody died.*

Then the documents. Certificate C-441-KR-04, issued 16:10 on 19 September 2029, and the name of the office that issued it. The intake sheet of 11 September 2029 opened in the same office, and the printed heading on the box in it, and the fact that a description was entered in it on 21 September 2029 at 14:22 by telephone. Form W-1 (rev. 3), and practice note W-7, and the sentence in it about an account not being impugned. The leaflet, and the four lines in the box on it, and the fact that the same four lines appear in Part Three, Module 4, page eleven, of a publication on sale at a forms counter. Module 7, page four, and the sentence about the office not supplying the order. Module 7, page five, and the sentence about there being no evidence that there was a previous account. The minute of the review board of 4 October 2029, item three.

And a closing paragraph, in which he said that a description of an order made by a police officer in answer to a question asked of him in an office corridor had been entered into a document, and that that document was in an office, and that the officer had read the office's name on a certificate in a clinic twenty feet away from his own front door and had not read it, and that he was not going to explain why, because the reason was in item two and three and the reason was a man who lives at the address.

And then the paragraph about names: *The name of a person appears in some of the documents listed above. It is not reproduced here. This is not a protection; it is a statement that the writer's objection is to the practice and not to the person, and that the writer would be obliged to say the same about himself.*

Elias Venn read it four times and did not improve it, and at half past ten he folded the four sheets and put them in an envelope and wrote his own name and address on the front of it and nothing else, not even a date, because a date is a date.

Nadia Kest came in at a quarter to eleven and stood in the doorway of the shop in her coat and read it standing up on the bench by the window, with the cat, and took about seven minutes.

"That's four sheets."

"Yes."

"It names a certificate, an intake sheet, a form, two practice notes, a publication, a curriculum with two modules in it, and a board minute." She read the last paragraph again. "And it does not contain a single person's name except yours."

"Yes."

"I've been an inspector for eleven years and I have never seen a document like it, and that is a statement about how the other kind is written."

"I need it received."

"I know what you need. And I am not going to file it as a note. You are not on the case, I have signed a retention review with my name on it, and if this turns up in a department file with my handling mark on it then the first thing anybody does is ask what the inspector knew and when." She took a receipt book out of her handbag. "So: miscellaneous receipt. No case number. Four sheets, handwritten, received from a member of the public at this address at a quarter to eleven on the seventh of October. Received as a document. Not evidence. Not a note on any matter. No file."

She wrote it in her own hand and gave him the stub and kept the counterfoil, and then she wrote a second line underneath the first one, which said: *To be produced on request of the addressee only. Not to be indexed. Not to be circulated.*

"Elias. That second line is a departmental instruction and I have never written one in my life and I have just written it, and I want you to know exactly what it is, because you are going to rely on it. It is not law. It is a line in a book in a drawer, and any office in this city can look at a receipt number and ask for the counterfoil, and if they do, I will have to say that I wrote the line, and I will, and it will be in a minute somewhere, and it will be the last thing I do for you as an officer and I am quite happy about it."

"Is it the last thing?"

"It is the last thing I am going to do for you as an officer," said Nadia Kest. "Everything else from here is either me being a person or me being wrong, and I am not going to be able to tell you which, and you are not going to ask me, and Wednesday morning is going to be extremely unpleasant for both of us."

She got her coat on and looked at the four sheets folded on the bench under the window.

"Leave them where they are," she said. "You will want to sleep on it and you cannot sleep on it in a file, and a man who has slept on nothing writes the fifth sheet in the voice of the first four."
