# Chapter 0008 — The Clinic That Will Not Close

The Kettle Rows Community Clinic occupied the ground floor and the yard of a former paint works, and it had a waiting room with twenty-two chairs, nine of them occupied at ten past seven on Friday morning, and a smell of boiled plates and disinfectant that Elias Venn had learned to identify within two streets.

He came in off the street with nothing in his hands, which was a decision he had made on the corner of Marshall Street and had thought about for most of the walk.

"Officer." Sana Okafor was forty-seven and had been a doctor for twenty-two of those years and was standing at the end of the dispensary counter with her coat on over her clothes, which meant she had come in before her shift, which meant she had not gone home. "It is seven in the morning. If you have come to search, I would like you to do it after the eight o'clock rush, and if you have come to search, I would like you to do it without my patients watching."

"I have not come to search."

"You have come to talk to Niko Senn."

"Yes."

"Then come and stand where I can see you while you do it," said Dr Okafor, "and do not ask him anything until I have decided which of my rooms you are allowed to stand in."

---

The waiting room in twenty minutes.

There was a man of about seventy with a foot that had gone the colour of a bad plum, and a woman with a baby asleep against her shoulder in a coat still buttoned, and two men who had both come in before opening and were now sitting on either side of her, and a boy of about eleven who had come in on his own and was doing the thing children do in waiting rooms, which is to look at everything.

Nobody looked at the television. The television was showing a municipal ward-safety bulletin with a woman in a Civic Concord vest standing under a ward light and explaining that district protection had been *intermittent* and that residents should report flickering to a line. She had been on it since Wednesday.

Somebody at the back was saying, in the flat tone people use for things they have said before, that it was the one on Kettle Rows they'd killed, and that the man who lived under her was the one, and that they should be doing something about the man who lived under her.

Elias had heard worse things about himself and had once heard better, and there was no version of this morning in which he stood in front of twenty-two chairs and said his name.

He went and sat down at the end of the row instead, and looked at the floor, and let it happen, and it took about ninety seconds and then stopped, which was somehow worse.

---

"The patient is in room two and he is not on a list yet," Sana Okafor said, in the corridor outside the dispensary, "because I have not put him on a list yet, and the reason I have not put him on a list yet is that everything in this building is a list."

"Understood."

"You have written that down in a way that means you have heard me and I would like to hear which part."

"That you have not put him on a list yet," Elias said, "and that nothing in this building is a list until you have made it one, and that you are the only person in the district who gets to make that decision, including me."

"Even the Concord."

"Especially the Concord. Doctor, I am not Concord. I am a detective sergeant in a building that has just received a four-page instruction from the Proof Registry about your dispensary stock, signed at a quarter past six on Wednesday evening, which arrived at your door at twenty to seven on Wednesday night, while your night clerk was alone on the floor, and which permits an inspector to treat everything in your stockroom as ordinary maintenance if nobody gets to it by this morning."

Sana Okafor stopped walking.

"Say the last part again."

"Your stockroom. It was attended to last night."

"It was attended to," said Dr Okafor, "because a man came at ten past nine with a Ward Works vest and a work order and a signature that I have not seen before and could not see properly, and he signed the maintenance book, which is a book, and he did the thing he said he was going to do to the dispensary ward line, which is *check the line*, and my night clerk is seventy-one and stood there with her hands folded and let it happen because he had a document."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall-ish. Not tall." She said it without any drama at all, and Elias felt something drop in him like a floor giving. "Very neat."

"Mrs Okafor—"

"I know," she said. "Yes. That is why I have been awake since five and why I have not called you and why I am telling you in a corridor and not on a telephone. Because if I had telephoned you at six this morning you would have come here with four officers and a form, and at ten past seven this morning there are nine people in my waiting room and every one of them is here because some other institution has already decided once today that it knows better than they do about their own bodies." She put her hand flat on the corridor wall. "Choose. And I want to hear you say it, because I have been choosing all night and I would like to know what I have chosen against."

Elias thought about it for longer than she expected, and she was watching him, and the thing he was thinking about was not the case.

"You are not choosing against the police," he said. "You are choosing against a raid at ten past seven in the morning. If there is a lawful thing available that does not put a uniform through your waiting room, I would like it, and I would like you to have asked for it first, and I am sorry that I have been in this building's doorway for four days and have not once asked you what you needed before telling you what I needed."

Sana Okafor looked at him for a moment. Then she took her hand off the wall.

"Niko Senn is in room two," she said. "He has not slept. He has a room because he has a fever of thirty-seven eight and a rash that I am treating, and he is not frightened of you, and he is frightened of a piece of paper, and you are going to have to be worse than the piece of paper. If you cannot be, then sit in the waiting room with the television on and I will talk to you at nine."

---

He was better than the piece of paper, but not by much.

Niko Senn was sitting on the edge of the bed in a clinic shirt with the blanket folded, and he had a paper cup of water he was not drinking, and when Elias came in he put the cup down and stood up, and Elias told him to sit, and Niko sat.

"They've written a treatment down," Niko said. "They've written down that I did eleven days of something in November. With a name for it. I've got a name for it now. I don't even have a *thing* to remember and they've given it a name and eleven days."

"I know. I have seen it."

"What is it?"

"I am not going to read it to you," Elias said. "Not because it is frightening. Because it is a description of a person who attended eleven days of a course of treatment at a clinic on Verge Row, and you did not, and the only person who is allowed to tell you what your file says is somebody who can hand you the file."

Niko almost laughed. It was a bad laugh. "Is that true? Is there somebody?"

"Yes. Me. But not yet, and not in this room, and I have to be able to say where I was when I said it."

He had not expected to be able to say that and he found that he could, which was new.

"I've got to tell you something," he said. "About what this is costing. On Monday there was a woman from the Concord who wrote down that I live in the building where the woman died, and that is now in the case file, and I heard about it off you and not off her, and it means that when this goes to a hearing, the first thing anybody will say is that the detective could not be trusted because he lived upstairs. And I have not got an answer for that. I have got a piece of paper in which I got there first, which is a smaller thing, and it is the only thing I have got."

Niko Senn looked at him.

"And what happens if I go on that bus?"

"Then I have a case with a witness who is alive, and I have to build the rest of it out of four minutes of my own memory and a piece of brass that a man drove back to the people who stole it out of a promise he made in a notebook." He said it flatly, because flat was the only way to say it. "You are not a piece of my case. I want that on the record in front of you because in about nine days somebody with a good reason is going to tell you that you are."

"Are you going to stop it?"

"I am going to try to stop it legally, today, without anybody's boots in this waiting room. I am going to fail at some part of it. And I am going to keep trying after I fail, which is the only part of it that is actually a promise."

---

The lawful thing was available, and it took eleven minutes, and it was Dr Okafor's.

She wrote a clinical hold. She wrote it in a way that took her forty minutes and three drafts and two phone calls, and it said that patient N. Senn, twenty-four, presented on Wednesday the nineteenth with an unexplained febrile illness and a rash of undetermined origin, that the patient's account of prior treatment was materially inconsistent with the clinical record in his possession, that a transfer of place of residence under a continuing treatment schedule was incompatible with an unresolved differential, and that any movement of the patient prior to review by a second clinician would risk harm to the patient and to the record.

The part that did the work was the last sentence, which was: *This clinic has no record of a completed course of treatment for this patient, and declines to certify one on another party's behalf.*

That sentence put the document inside the medical question instead of the police question, and a clinical hold under a continuing-treatment schedule is not a police instrument at all. It is a form a doctor fills in, and the account can challenge it, and the account can appeal it, and the account cannot get past it before a review board sits, which was a Thursday.

They filed it at ten past ten.

At eleven minutes past ten, a courier brought the transfer order in.

She was not Concord. That was the first thing Elias noticed. She was thirty, she had a clipboard, and she was a contract registrar for the rehabilitation account, which is a job that exists in a city where an account is a legal person, and she was apologetic, and she had a signature box, and she put the clipboard on the counter and said that she needed the patient's signature and a witness and would wait.

"Nineteen sixty-five," said Niko Senn, from the corridor.

She did not understand.

"Registry nineteen sixty-five. It's a Witness, Operations Office, Morrow Hill, outbox, four fifty-one on Wednesday." Elias said it before he had decided to say it. "That is the number on the sheet she is going to hand you, and it is the second one on the sheet, and the first one is a certificate number, and the certificate was issued at ten past four on Wednesday afternoon, and the file it was issued from was opened at four forty-nine."

He should not have known that. He knew it because Nadia had put a routing list on his desk at seven in the morning with three Registry timestamps on it, and he had read it in the taxi, and the taxi had been slow.

The registrar turned the first sheet round to look at it.

"Sign and witness," she said patiently. "That's all it is."

Elias did not touch it. He asked her to put it on the counter flat, and then he stood at a slight distance, with his hands visible, the way he had stood at a counter in a records store on Monday afternoon, and he read.

It ran to a single page. It was a transfer of place of residence under continuing treatment, patient N. Senn, from an account premises on Verge Row to the account premises, effective on signature, with an escort requirement where clinically indicated. It named the receiving facility as *account premises, Verge Row* and it named, in the referrals block, *continuing care on discharge from account premises — Kettle Rows Community Clinic, Dr S. Okafor, from the twelfth.*

And underneath the signature block, on every one of these documents, in a box that exists so that a sequence can travel with a person instead of travelling separately, there was a sequence.

Four acts.

Elias read the box. It took him twice, because the first time he miscounted and had to go back to the top of the form and start again at the printed instruction. A transfer of residence is not a working; it is a piece of paper moving a person's address. And yet the box was there, and it was filled in, and it had four acts where the printed instruction above it described a three-act standard transfer, and the fourth one was the shape of a release.

He did not touch it. He did not lean over it. He stood back and looked at four printed lines on a sheet of paper, on a counter, in a room with nine people in it, and he understood that he was one sentence away from saying the thing that had been sitting behind his teeth since the stairwell on Monday morning, and that if he said it here, in front of a registrar and a doctor and a patient and a waiting room, then he would have said it first, and he would have said it without a measurement, and no tribunal in the world would ever be able to use it.

"Thank you," he said. "I am going to have to ask you to wait."

---

Sana Okafor came out of the treatment room and looked at the counter, and read the sheet standing up, and did not touch it either.

"Dr Okafor, the patient is—"

"The patient is not signing anything," said Dr Okafor, "and neither is this clinic, and I have a hold, and there is a board on Thursday." She was already writing on a pad. "I am not going to detain him. He is free to leave. He is not going to be *transferred*, and if he wants to walk out of my door this afternoon then that is a thing he is entitled to do and I will write down that he was free to do it, which is the only protection either of us will have on Thursday."

The registrar looked at the two of them. "This is a scheduling matter."

"Then you are free to go and schedule it," said Sana Okafor, "and I would suggest you mention to whoever you report to that a community clinic in Kettle Rows put a hold on a Witness, Operations account transfer this morning, because that is a thing that will be in a report, and it will not be a report about me."

She gave the registrar a card, and the registrar took it, and then, at the door, she stopped and said, half turning, "The patient should know that if he does not attend on the date, the account treats that as discharge."

"He will be told," said Dr Okafor. "By me. In person. Not by you."

The registrar went out. Through the glass you could see her stand on the step in the sun and make a call, and then stand there for the length of the call, and then go up the street with her head down and the clipboard under her arm.

"Well," said Dr Okafor, to nobody. "That is done until Thursday."

Then she turned round to him.

"You read that sheet," she said.

"Yes."

"In four seconds, standing back, without touching it, in a room with a queue behind you." She was not accusing him of anything. She was working something out. "You said the first number and the second number, and the time on the certificate, and you got that right, and I do not think you can read a document like that the way I can read a chart. I think you can read something *in* it."

"Yes."

"Then here is the thing I want to say to you, and I want you to hear it in this room rather than in a corridor later." She came closer. "There is a boy in room two who is twenty-four years old and has been frightened into a shape by somebody, and everything in you right now is telling you to get hold of him and keep him. And I understand that, because I have wanted to do exactly the same thing to a great many people for twenty-six years. But if you keep him, then in nine months he is a case you have kept. And a case you have kept is a boy who does not exist outside a building in Kettle Rows where the policeman who lives upstairs is the one who saved him."

Elias did not say anything, because there was nothing that would not have been a lie.

"You have a way of arriving at the correct conclusion by a route that takes something from somebody on the way," said Dr Okafor. "I have watched you do it four times in a day and I have only known you a day. Do you know how I know?"

"Because you are the first person in four days who has had nothing to gain from being careful."

"That is nearly right." She looked at the sheet on the counter. "The other half is that I have been the person who was careful, for a very long time, with very little, and I would like somebody to hand me a reason. So: what happens on Thursday?"

"Elias's answer is that Nadia Kest can hold the transfer for seventy-two hours, that a clinical hold holds it until the board, that you can give evidence about the merged record, and that I can put the Registry's own timestamps in front of a tribunal and show that a sequence certificate was issued four hours before the order that carries it."

"And?"

"And I do not have a measurement that says where the four acts on that sheet came from," he said. "I have a printout and a set of timestamps and a witness with a rehearsal in his head, and there is one woman in this city whose instruments can say whether four acts in a box on a form came out of a room, and she is two wards from this building, and I have been told by her that if I want her to put her name on it I have to stop being the man who touches things."

Sana Okafor looked at the counter, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed — one syllable, mostly tired.

"Then do not touch things," she said. "That is not advice. It is the entire reason this building still exists, and I will not have it insulted while it is standing."

She picked up the transfer order by the edge, and put it in a clear sleeve, and wrote on the sleeve the date and the time and the name of everybody who had read it.

"Four of us have now read that document," she said, "and three of us are not police. It is going in the clinic's own file, and there is a copy on the board in the waiting room for anybody who wants to read it, because in my experience the only thing that stops an institution taking a person is other people knowing what it wrote down."

The boy of eleven got up off his chair at that and went and read it, standing up, the way children read things, with his finger on the line he was following.

---

He went out into the street at ten past eleven with nothing achieved and everything that could be achieved by a man who had no authority, and there was a woman on the step of the yard with a shopping bag who looked at him for a moment and then said, not unkindly, "You were in the room over Tamsin."

"Yes."

"Eleven years." She shifted the bag. "I am not saying it to be cruel. I am saying it because I have to go into that building twice a week and I am going to be thinking about it, and I would rather you heard me say it than worked it out."

"Then thank you," Elias said.

She went in.

His phone rang at four minutes past twelve.

It was Nadia, and she was in a car.

"Two things," she said. "One: the C-ear has come back as a full draft with a hearing date on the fourteenth, and they want a countersignature from the assigned detective by six this evening, and the Registry's own legal officer has flagged the schedule as 'at risk' because the assigned officer is resident at the scene. Which is a piece of administrative cruelty so refined that I have to assume somebody enjoyed writing it."

"And two?"

"Two: the C-ear has a preliminary draft attached to it, and I have read it, and I want you to hear three words out of it before you hear anything else." A pause. "It says *self-directed, single subject, no third-party address*."

Elias shut his eyes on the pavement outside a paint works in Kettle Rows.

"It doesn't name him," he said.

"It does not name him at all," said Nadia. "He does not appear in it. Not as a witness, not as a person, not in a footnote. He is a twenty-four-year-old with a fever in a clinic and he has been struck out of a document in a file that has not been written yet."

"Then I will not countersign it."

"I know. I have known since Wednesday, and I have been waiting for you to say it where I could hear it, because it means I have to send the refusal up a chain on a Friday afternoon, and I would rather have been able to say that you said it with witnesses." He heard her take a breath. "Elias. Where are you?"

"Outside the clinic."

"Good. Because Mara Quill is two wards from you, and she has been in her laboratory since seven this morning with a plate and a piece of paper, and she asked me at eleven whether you were free at one. I said you were standing outside a clinic doing nothing. She said, and I am quoting her, *tell him to come and look at three pieces of paper and not one object*."

Elias looked up and down Marshall Street, at the ward light over the dispensary counter, at a queue of nine people behind a door he had been trying and failing to get into for four days.

"One o'clock," he said.
