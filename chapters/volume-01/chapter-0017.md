# Chapter 0017 — What She Will Not Certify

The letter had come on Monday afternoon and it was on the bench under the extractor hood on Thursday morning because Mara Quill had read it four times and had not yet decided what to do with it, and a decision not made is still a decision that is being made, and she had known that about herself since she was twenty-eight.

*You are invited to attend a preliminary practice review, Clinical Standards office, Civic Concord, 14:00 Thursday 27 September, regarding material submitted in connection with Sequence Desk file 441-KR. You are asked to bring all plates, sleeves, instrument logs and written figures in your possession relating to that file. It is noted that the instruments in your laboratory are not on a Concord instrument register. The review is preliminary and is not a disciplinary hearing.*

Then, in the second paragraph, in a sentence that had clearly been put there by somebody who had been argued with and had lost gracefully:

*This office notes that no instrument in the city has found anything unusual in the room over the deceased, and that any material you are able to provide would be the first to indicate otherwise.*

Mara Quill put the letter on the bench and put a second plate on top of it, because the plate from a room in Kettle Rows was the only object in her building she trusted more than she trusted the hood, and she had been carrying it around the laboratory for three days taking it from the rack to the bench and back, which is not a thing a careful person does.

She had four figures and a sleeve and a notebook and she had the two break measurements in pencil and the plate itself was in a hard case with a padlock on it, and she was going to take all of it into a building belonging to the institution that had written the letter, on a bus, on her own, on a Thursday afternoon, and there was no version of the afternoon in which that was a good idea and there were about nine in which it was a bad one, and the reason she was doing it anyway was that the alternative was to say no and sound like a person who was afraid of a room.

She took the plate out of the case, put it in a document wallet with the sleeve visible, and took the wallet out to the bus stop.

---

The Clinical Standards office is on the second floor above a shop that does nothing but replace watch straps, and the room it occupies has a window and a table and six chairs, and the assessor who received her was a woman in her forties called Priyanka Lyle who had a file in front of her, and who stood up, and who offered her a chair, and who asked whether she wanted water before she sat down.

That was the first thing that told Mara Quill she was in the wrong kind of fight. In the wrong kind of fight, somebody tells you what you are within ninety seconds.

"You will not be examined today," said Lyle. "This is a preliminary review. The purpose of it is that an officer has said something in a hearing about your work, and I would like to know what your work is, before anything else in this building is built on it."

"I said nothing in the hearing."

"You said nothing. What you did was stand in a corridor on Friday afternoon and ask for something to be minuted, before the officer said it, and it was minuted, and now I have a minute that says the residue in this matter was measured by an instrument whose operator is not a licensed examiner and does not hold a Concord registration, and that if anybody wishes to say that no instrument found anything unusual then that sentence is true." Lyle turned a page round. "Who told you to say it?"

"Nobody."

"Then why did you say it?"

"Because it was true, and because it is true now, and because I have spent four days watching a document get written before the evidence, and I would rather my own limitation be on the record in my own words than arrive as somebody else's surprise in a courtroom in four years." She put the wallet on the table and did not open it. "May I say something about that sentence before I give you anything else, because it is going to be the whole of this meeting and I would like to get it out of the way in order."

"Please."

"It is true and I am not going to argue with it, and I am also not going to soften it, and if this office is going to build anything on my material then this office needs to know exactly what it is standing on." Mara took her hands off the wallet. "I hold no Concord licence. I have decided not to hold one. My plates are not on a register and never will be, and everything I have said in this case is the testimony of an uncertified person about an instrument, and I put my name on all of it and no name of yours, and I will put my name on all of it again this afternoon if you want it, and I will not put Elias Venn's name next to one figure of it and I would like that minuted too."

"That is not a question in your favour," said Lyle.

"I am aware of that," said Mara Quill. "I am telling you the ground floor before you look at the walls."

---

The second part of the meeting took an hour and eleven minutes and the reason Mara Quill thought about it for the next four days was not the numbers.

She gave them. Four figures in pencil from a plate she had lifted in a corridor on Marshall Street in front of two witnesses, signed on the sleeve, dated, initialled in the corner: the phase signature of the fifth thing on the desk, the absence of any settling curve, the two measurements taken at twenty-six minutes to nine on Wednesday the nineteenth and the note she had dated at ten to nine saying the channel in a clamp measured empty at that hour and unmeasurable from that hour forward. She gave the two break measurements in the metal of a carrier that had come off a wall. She gave the log numbers for each.

And when Lyle asked, in a completely reasonable voice, how the channel came to measure empty at ten to nine and then to describe an act at eight minutes to nine, Mara Quill reached into her document wallet and took out a single sheet of paper, and put it on the table face up, and said:

"That is my note. It is dated Wednesday nineteenth, ten to nine, and it is the only document in this case in which I say something about myself while I was saying it, and it goes in, and I would like it copied and I would like your copy to have the same words on it as mine."

Lyle read it.

*19 Sept, 20:50. The channel of the above measured EMPTY at this hour. Plate running on the stamped face. D/Sgt Venn in the room. I am looking at a number. I am not looking at the door, the corridor, the sample, or the officer. At 20:52 a hand of the officer's went into the channel, ungloved as to the channel, which I had told him not to do, and which he had been told not to do by me, at the window, at nineteen forty-one. Everything I have measured from that channel from 20:52 onward describes a surface with a man in it. I was the person looking at the number. I have written this at 21:03, thirteen minutes after, from memory, because I did not write it at the time, and I want it on the record that I did not write it at the time. — M.Q.*

"That," said Lyle, "is the most damaging document in this file and it is in your handwriting."

"Yes."

"You are aware that it is a statement that your professional conduct on that evening was a contributory cause of the loss of the case's best measurement?"

"I am aware that it is a statement of what happened," said Mara Quill. "I am also aware that if I hand this office four figures and do not hand it this, then in a year somebody produces it without my name in the room and I have spent six years building a practice on the fact that I write down the inconvenient part, and there is a very short version of my career in which I only write down the convenient part."

Lyle made a photocopy and signed and initialled the copy and gave it back, and then she sat back, and said the thing that Mara had been waiting for since the letter arrived.

"So. The stencil."

---

Roy Prentice came in at ten past three with a Witness Operations folder and a coat over his arm and the manner of a man who has been unpleasant to somebody all day and would like to put it down for twenty minutes.

He was not cruel either. That was the difficulty. He asked for the foil stencil, and he asked for it in a form that Mara Quill could not dismiss as an office exercising a power, because the power was real and the request was not an exercise of it.

"The foil element bonded inside the channel of the carrier that came off the clinic wall on the twenty-first," he said. "The stencil. It is the only physical object in this file that links the first carrier and the second carrier to one production method, and we have a licensed maker's two sets, one of which is contaminated and one of which is in a bench room we cannot lawfully enter, and we have a break in metal and a seam on a plate, and we have eleven figures that came out of a piece of brass that is now in an outer ward. The stencil is the one object that a tribunal could be shown and that somebody could put a hand on."

"It is a stencil," said Mara Quill. "It has no charge of its own. It is a piece of shaped metal that puts a sequence into a channel. Mara Quill will tell you what it is, because you have come here for it: it is a stamp, and a stamp is not a working, and a stamp does not run on its own."

"I know what it is. I want to know where it is, and I want to know whether it can be brought into a facility with a chain and a register and a person who can be cross-examined on the custody of it."

"It cannot."

"There is no registered facility within forty minutes of this city that is not Concord-run, and there is a Concord evidence room on the third floor of this building with a chain and a register and a technician," said Prentice. "It would take an hour. I would give you the receipt myself."

"And in nine months," said Mara Quill, "the object is a registered object, and every figure I have ever produced in this case is a figure taken out of a registered object, and the moment a figure is a figure taken out of a registered object, the only person who may speak to it is a person this Registry may serve with notice. You would not be doing it to me. You would be doing it to the case, and to the four figures on the threshold plate, and to a plate that is not on a register precisely because it was lifted in a corridor in front of two witnesses and signed by a woman who does not hold a licence."

"Then what would you have me do?"

"I would have you write down that the stencil exists, that it is on a plate in a laboratory in two rooms behind a hospital linen office, that the plate is in my name, and that I am not transferring it." She put both hands flat on the table. "And I would like my refusal to be minuted word for word, because a refusal that nobody wrote down did not happen, and because in four years the difference between those two things is the whole of whether anybody can be cross-examined on this."

"Word for word is not a thing I have ever been asked for."

"Then it will be the first," said Mara Quill, "and I am aware that is a piece of vanity, and I am doing it anyway."

---

Elias Venn was in a corridor on the second floor of a building on a shopping street at half past four on a Thursday afternoon, with a bench and a fire notice and a vending machine that took notes, and he had been on the bench for eighty minutes and had been told twice by a woman from Witness Operations that he could not come in, and had agreed both times without arguing, and had spent the eighty minutes reading a leaflet about the Witness Service that was four years out of date.

She came out into the corridor and sat down on the other end of the bench and did not speak for about half a minute.

"You have been on that bench for an hour and a quarter."

"I have."

"You could have gone in. I have given you a great deal of latitude about a great many things and I would have let you in."

"No," said Elias. "You would have let me in and then you would have spent an hour being a woman with a policeman in the room, and I have had four of those in six days and they are all the same hour."

That was the argument, and they had it on a bench over a vending machine, and it took nine minutes and both of them were right.

"You gave them the four figures."

"I gave them the measurements. I did not give them the word *copied*. I did not give them a name. I did not give them a motive. I did not give them the plate." She turned round on the bench. "And I gave them the note, Elias. The one from Wednesday night. About me and the number and the channel."

"You did not have to."

"No," said Mara Quill, "and that is the part I would like you to sit with for about a minute, because you have been waiting four days for me to soften it and I have not and I am not going to, and the reason I put it in a building's file is that it is the truest thing I wrote this week and I am not going to be a person who writes down the convenient part."

He sat with it. He was, he found, angrier about it than he had been about anything else in the week, and the anger was not the useful kind.

"You have spent four days letting me believe that you had asked for a note you had not yet written."

"I did not let you do anything. You built a version of me out of the two things I said out loud on Friday and left out the third, and the third was the one about me." She was not looking at him. "That is the mistake you make and I have watched you make it four times. You take the parts of a person that you can work with and you build the rest of her out of paper, and the paper one is always more cooperative than me."

"Is that fair?"

"No," said Mara Quill. "It is entirely unfair, and I have been extremely unfair about it all day and I have just put a note about myself into an office's file, so we are approximately even."

Then she said the other thing, which she had been carrying up the stairs and down the corridor and had not intended to say on a bench in a shopping street.

"You have been wanting to ask me something since Friday afternoon and you have not asked it."

"I have."

"What."

"Whether you would do all of that again if I was not the assigned detective on it."

"I would put the plates on a register this afternoon," said Mara Quill. "I would give them four figures and the note and the log numbers and I would let the printer hold them. I would be a different woman with a different standing in about four months and I would not be doing it for you and I would not be doing it for Nadia Kest. I would be doing it because four figures off an uncertified plate is a rumour and four figures off a registered plate is a measurement, and I have wanted that for six years, and the only thing standing in the way is a sentence I said out loud to you in a laboratory in 2019 for no reason anybody can now remember."

"I remember it."

"I know you remember it," said Mara Quill. "You were extremely tiresome about it. That is not why."

And then she said the other thing, and she said it in the flat voice she used for figures.

"My brother is called Iven Quill. He went into a clinic that was not licensed on the fourteenth of October, six years and seventeen days ago, and he has not come out, and nobody in this city has a single document about him because he was not a patient in any system, he was a customer of a room. I have not said his name out loud in this building in three years, and I have said it to you on a bench in a shopping street, and there is no lead attached to it and I would like that on the record by somebody. Witness."

Elias did not ask her a single question about it. It took him about four seconds of wanting to and then he understood that she had handed it to him the way she handed over a measurement: as a thing that is hers, given without a condition, to a person who will not immediately try to work on it.

"Right," he said.

"That is the whole of what I have," said Mara Quill. "Thank you for not asking."

They sat on the bench for another four minutes, and then she stood up and told him four practical things in the order she would say them, which was her way of being kind.

"One, there is nothing in those five documents that is a crime, and there is going to be a request about them tomorrow morning from a person whose name I do not know, and when it comes you will want to say no, and you will not be able to say no, because the figures are in a file now and a file is a thing you can be asked for."

"Two?"

"Two, I have said four numbers out loud in a room in that building this afternoon and there is a certified copy of them in a drawer in that building, and that is the first time in six years that my figures have existed in a drawer I cannot open, and I am going to be a different woman about that for about a week and I would like you to be told now so that you can put up with it."

"And three?"

"Three, you are not coming into the laboratory this week, and neither of us is going into room two at the clinic without Doctor Okafor, and the second half of that is not about the plate. I mean it as a professional instruction and I am going to write it down, and if you come in I am going to be extremely unpleasant and I will not apologise afterwards."

Elias got up off the bench.

"You want to tell me the fourth thing," he said.

"The fourth thing is that a refusal and a request are the same piece of paper with two names on it," said Mara Quill. "I have just refused a thing and put my reasons in a minute, and tomorrow somebody is going to ask for the thing I refused and use my reasons as the justification, and that is not cynicism, that is how a record works. Somebody refused the stencil in writing. That is now a reason to have the stencil."
