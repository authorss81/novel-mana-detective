# Chapter 0043 — A Sheet With Five Lines

The sheet of A4 was on the bench with four lines written on it and a fifth one ruled off and empty, and Mara Quill had a pen in her hand and was not writing on it, and Elias Venn had been in the laboratory since a quarter past ten and they had been on the second line for fifty minutes.

"You are not going to tell me what the second line says," he said.

"I am going to tell you what the second line says in about four minutes, and I have been waiting for you to stop trying to write it for me for about forty of the fifty." She put the pen down. "Sit down. You have been standing at that bench since you came in and you have read the same four lines eleven times, and I do not know how I know it is eleven, and I am not going to tell you."

He sat down on the stool, because there is one stool in that room and there are two of them, and the second is a fire bucket.

The residue laboratory is two rooms behind the linen office of Saint Orra Reach and you get into it by signing a book at a counter, and the counter belongs to the laundry, and the woman at the counter has been there for nineteen years and her name is on the book every week and nobody outside the building has ever heard of it. Mara Quill's name is in that book four times a week and it is the only register in the city her work is on, and neither of them ever said that out loud, and Elias Venn thought about it while she was talking and did not say it out loud either.

"Line one," she said. "*Whose object, and who has said it is theirs, in their own words, with the date.* Not *the subject*. Not *the item*. Whose, and who said so, and when. If you cannot write that line down then you have no business putting a hand on it, and the reason you cannot write it down is usually that you have been given the object by somebody who does not own it."

"That is right."

"Line two." She picked the pen up and turned it so that the nib was away from him. "This is the one you want to argue about, so let us have it properly. I want: *what is being done to the object, in one sentence, that the person could repeat.*"

"That sentence has to carry the sequence, Mara. Otherwise we are writing a consent to pick up a walking stick."

"Yes," said Mara Quill. "And that is where you are wrong, and I have been wrong about you in this room twice before, so take a chair."

"I am on a fire bucket."

"Take a chair, Elias."

He took the stool and she took the other one, which was a thing she had never done in six years of knowing him, and which she noticed and did not comment on, and neither did he.

"Niko Senn's whole case," she said, "is that an order he perceived and an order he was given cannot be told apart by anybody in this city, and that is true, and it has been true since the fourth of October and it is the most important fact any of us have. Now consider what happens if the sentence on that sheet contains the sequence. Alfred Neve is a man of seventy-eight who has had a thing fitted to his own walking stick by a stranger in a very good coat, and if the sentence on the sheet says *we are going to look for a release that was not performed* — do you know what he does with that? He tells it to a neighbour. He tells Mrs Achterberg, who he has already told about the bracket, because he is the sort of man who recommends a thing. He takes it to a ward authority building and says a policeman had a sequence out of my stick. Within a week that sentence is in a file in Concord Hall and in about a year it is a sentence about a man who has never heard of it and who cannot answer it, and it will be true, and it will have been said by him, and it will be the only copy."

"That is the wrong way round," said Elias.

"It is the only way round that exists." She wrote the line. "*What is being done to the object, in one sentence, that the person could repeat.* And the sentence gets said to him in his own kitchen, and he is given the chance to say it back to somebody who is not us — that is the test, that is what makes it a consent and not a form. He has to be able to walk into a corridor at the clinic on Tuesday and say *a man is going to look at my walking stick because somebody has been at my door*, and say it in his own words, and be believed, and if he cannot do that then he has not consented to anything except that we were in his kitchen."

Elias Venn sat on a stool in a laboratory behind a laundry and looked at five lines of handwriting.

"Write it that way," he said.

"It is written that way."

"You were pleased with yourself."

"I was correct," said Mara Quill. "There is a difference and you have been taught it by a machine that sells the difference for three credits, so you may forgive yourself."

"Line three," she said, and wrote as she talked. "*Who is doing it, and who is watching.* Names, not roles. Not *the laboratory* and not *the police*. If nobody can say the name of the person who is watching then nobody is watching, and that has been the entire problem since the seventeenth of September, and it cost me a plate and you a file and me eleven years of standing and it will go on costing somebody."

"Line four. *What has been measured before any hand goes on it, and by whom, in whose log, under whose number.*" She drew a line under it. "This is the one that is mine. It has been mine since the ninth of October and I have never written it down anywhere, because I did not have anybody to write it down for. On Monday night I have a figure or I have nothing, and if I have nothing then the only figure in the room is yours, and yours will be in a notebook, and a notebook is not an instrument."

"Line five," said Elias.

"*Who consents, in writing, and what they were told.*" She put the pen down and looked at the sheet. "That one is yours, and it is the only one I am frightened of, and I have written it last, and you may notice that."

"I noticed."

"You were not meant to."

---

Then she sat back and said the thing she had been carrying since Friday afternoon, and she said it in the flat voice she used for numbers.

"I am not taking a figure today."

"Understood."

"I want to say why, because you will assume it is a refusal and it is not a refusal, it is an absence." She stood and went to the hard case on the shelf and did not open it. "There is nothing in this room to measure. There is a man in Tanners Row with a stick and a card and a licensed collar, and until he hands it to me there is no object on this bench that is not already on one, and I have three plates in this case and a hard case and a padlock and a room nobody can find, and if I take a figure off a man's walking stick on a Saturday because he was nice about it I will be a woman who measures things that have been handed to her in a kitchen."

"Line one," said Elias.

"Line one," said Mara Quill. "I am teaching you my own discipline and I resent it and it is going to work."

"And Sunday?"

"And on Sunday I will take a figure if he gives it to me, and I will take it in his kitchen with him in the room, and Jules will write three lines in his bench book, and you will not put a hand on anything, and I will say so out loud in the lane before we go in, and you will say the prohibition out loud, because you have said it out loud twice now and it is the only sentence of yours I have ever wanted on a record."

"And after Sunday?"

She did not answer that straight away. She took a cloth and started wiping the bench where there was nothing on it, which she did when she was going to say something she had not decided to say.

"Fig. 3, Q," she said. "Two point six oh four millimetres. I certified it in pencil, in my own name, in a Concord office, on the twenty-seventh of September, and a draughtsman wrote it on a drawing on the twenty-eighth, and a man in a bay in The Cut cut twelve seats to that depth, and one of those seats is four inches of channel section and it is either in a bay under the city or it is in a walking stick in Tanners Row."

"Elias."

"The number is mine and the number is why a hole can be cut in a piece of ash that a man puts on his own step every morning and puts a coin on to see if it is real." She stopped wiping. "And on Sunday afternoon I am going to have to sit in that man's kitchen and explain to him, in words, out loud, with his kettle on the table, that the reason a stranger could put a thing inside his walking stick is that I certified a number in a drawer in Concord Hall and that number is published as a manufacturing dimension in a carrier specification, and that I tried to keep it in a drawer and a drawer is not a register."

"You do not have to."

"I have to," said Mara Quill. "He is owed a sentence by a person and there is no version of that sentence that a plate can carry, and I am the only one who can say it who is not a policeman, and I have no standing whatsoever, and I am going to say it badly, and I am going to say it in front of a witness because that is the practice now."

Elias Venn did not offer to do it. He wanted to, and he did not, because she had not asked and because there was nothing he could do that would not have been a man with a sequence in his mouth standing in a seventy-eight-year-old man's kitchen explaining a woman's figure to him.

"I will be in the lane," he said. "Outside. Whatever time it takes."

"You will not."

"I will, and you will know it, and that is the part that is arranged."

---

They signed it at twenty past one. Two signatures on a sheet of A4, hers on the line and his under it, in the ordinary way two people sign a thing they are both going to obey, and no stamp and no witness and no form number, and she tore the sheet in half along a rule she had folded it on before she wrote it, which she had done with every plate sleeve for eleven years, and gave him his half.

"This does not leave this building in a file," she said.

"It is in my notebook, which is in my own shop, which is a private room."

"That is worse and it is better," said Mara Quill, "and I have decided not to argue about it this week. If it is in a file it becomes an instrument somebody else can issue. In your notebook it is a sheet of paper a man promised to work under, and the only person who can produce it is you, and the day you produce it is a day you have decided to be honest about having kept it."

"Saturday the fourth of October I asked you whether I could take a plate and you said no."

"I said no in writing and in your hand and there is a breach note in my file with my name on it and yours beside it." She put the plate of food she had brought down from the linen office canteen in front of him, which she did without a word and without comment, and which he ate, and which was the first proper meal he had had in ten days. "That is a different thing from a sheet of paper with five lines on it. I have thought about that for two months and I have not got to a better answer than *no, it is a different thing, and I have no idea which is worse*."

He was at the door when she said his name, and she was at the bottom drawer of the bench with an envelope in her hand that she had not opened and had taken out anyway.

"Two of these," she said, holding it up so he could see the string-tie and the wax and the Concord crest. "They are not paper, they are foil. A certified copy of Fig. 3, Q goes to the holder of the original and a certified copy goes to the office, and the office copy is in a drawer in Concord Hall in a building I do not work in, behind a counter I have to be let past, in a corridor with a counter of my own four streets away that is not on the same street." She put it back in the drawer and shut the drawer. "I have taken that envelope out four times since the ninth of October. I have opened it zero times."

"That is not a system, Mara."

"It is the most honest arrangement in this city and it is a drawer." She locked it, which she did not usually do in front of him. "When I am frightened I do the tidying. I have tidied this room eleven times since Friday afternoon and I have not measured anything and I have not touched a plate, and if you tell anybody that I will deny it and it will be true, because the tidying is not evidence of anything except that I am frightened."

"You are frightened of Fig. 3, Q."

"I am frightened of a number I certified in pencil in nine minutes in a warm room with nobody in it," said Mara Quill. "Go home. Sleep. Do the four jobs. Sunday I have to say a sentence to that man and I have not got it yet and I will not have it by Monday either, and I am going to have to stand in his kitchen and improvise the worst three minutes of my professional life, and I have done that before, and I did it in September, and I have been putting it off ever since because I do not know yet whether the number is what I think it is or whether the number is what somebody needed it to be."

---

He left at four with the sheet folded twice in his inside pocket, where a note from a municipal clerk in three numbered items had been living since the tenth of October, and the two things were in the same pocket and did not touch each other, and one of them was still unanswered and would be until he had an answer that was not an apology.

---

He slept five hours on Saturday afternoon, which was more than he had slept in one go since the seventeenth of September, and he woke at seven to a ceiling making its wasp noise and lay there and worked out that the noise was the same as it had been every night of his life and that he had a day under him that had a beginning and an end in it, and that this was the first Monday-to-Friday he had had since June that he could account for.

He went down at half past seven and Otto had gone and the wireless was under a cloth and the four jobs were in the book. He did the Cheetham job's element, which was nine credits out of twenty-nine in the till, and the Welch job's flex, which was two, and he did them in cash because there was no account to charge, and he wrote both in the carbon book in his own hand with the date, and he fed the cat, and he put the door up and left it up because a Saturday in October is a day when people come in.

Three people came in. A man with a toaster he did not need fixed and who bought a bag of fuses. A girl of about nineteen who wanted a bicycle lamp mended and paid for it. And at about half past nine, a woman in a wet mac who asked whether he was the man off the papers, and he said he was, and she said her mother had read them, and he said he was glad they were being read, and she apologised for coming, and he told her she had nothing to apologise for, and she left, and the door stayed up, and the shop did the thing a shop is for.

At half past ten he wrote the Saturday out with the times and put the notebook on the shelf in the row where it always went, beside the folio and the stamped copy of CS-3 and the censure and the Factors letter and the bag of four hundred and six pages that was not going to be evidence of anything now.

*Sat 27 Oct. Laboratory, ten fifteen to four. Five lines, signed, one copy each, the original stays in her building and I am not going to argue about that again this week. Line two is hers and she is right: the sentence has to be one a man can say back to somebody who is not us.*

*No figure. Nothing on the bench that has not already been on one. She is teaching me her own discipline and she told me so.*

*Fig. 3, Q is on the list. She has to tell Alfred Neve tomorrow why a hole can be cut in his stick, and she has no standing, and she is going to say it in front of a witness because that is the practice now.*

*Sat: slept five hours. Ate. Four jobs, two of them cash. Door up. Three people came in and one of them was a stranger who wanted to tell me her mother had read the four sheets.*

*Sunday, ten o'clock, Tanners Row, two people and a piece of paper.*

Otto Vahl came in at ten and put the wireless under a cloth and did not turn the light on, and stood at the front of the shop in the dark for a while with his coat still on, which he does about twice a year.

"Ellie," he said. "Mrs Achterberg came down at six this evening and asked me whether it was true that there is a man in a very good coat who goes round Kettle Rows fitting things to people's walking sticks, and I said I did not know, and she said that a man at number three in Tanners Row had been given one and had told her the name, and that she has a hip like his and she wants to know whether it is safe."

Elias Venn put the cloth down.

"What did you say to her?"

"I said I did not know," said Otto Vahl, "and then I said I would find out, and I have not found out, and I am telling you that I have said I would find out, and that is two words more than I have said to that woman in six years and I would like it noticed."

The cat came down off the radio bench in the dark and went along the top of the till and looked at the notebook on the shelf and did not want it, and Elias Venn stood at the counter of a repair shop that had four jobs in the book and no trade account and one thing in it that was not a kettle, and did not write anything down, because the thing that had to be written down was somebody's name, and he did not have one, and the only name he had was a trading name on a piece of paper in a room under the city.
