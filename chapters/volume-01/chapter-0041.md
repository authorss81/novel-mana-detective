# Chapter 0041 — The Unregistered Repairs

Jules Ardent came through the door of the repair shop at twenty to nine with a canvas bag over his shoulder that he put on the front bench and did not open, and stood next to it with his hands hanging, waiting to be told what to do with them.

"You have not opened it," Elias Venn said.

"I have not opened it."

"You have been outside since half past eight."

"I have been outside since half past eight."

Elias put the cloth down and came round the counter. It was a Thursday in the last week of October and the shop smelled of the tea urn and the paraffin heater and the particular dust that Otto Vahl's radio work made out of bakelite. Otto had the wireless on the bench behind him at low volume because the ceiling was doing its wasp noise again, and he did not look up, because men bring bags into this shop most days and it is not his business.

"Go on then," Elias said.

Jules untied the neck of the bag and put four things on the bench in a row, and then a fifth, and then stopped with his hand still in the bag.

"Everything I borrowed," he said. "The hacksaw is Bramwell's, that's the small one, the copper is the roll out of the cold room's spill tray that came off the stockroom carrier job, the drift punch is mine and I never gave you that, and the four-inch brace is Otto's and I would like it back before he notices."

"Take them in the back."

"No." Jules Ardent, who had been charged with carrying other people's work for money on Tuesday and cleared of building a frame on a measurement, and who had a bench room he still could not open and a die block still with the Registry, put both hands flat on the bench. "I have not come for the tools. I have come about the four."

Elias did not say anything, and the thing that came into the room was not a reading and not a flash of anything; it was a man who has been carrying something since Tuesday and has now put it on a bench and cannot get it off again.

"Sit down," Elias said. "You are standing like the delivery van."

Jules sat on the stool.

"There are four," he said. "Unregistered repairs. In September. I did them in four kitchens in four streets and I did not log them, and I am telling you because you have been in this shop every day for a fortnight asking me what I have been doing, and because you are the only person who has asked me in a way that I have believed."

"How long have you had them?"

"Since the fourth of September."

"Twenty-one days."

"Twenty-one days." Jules nodded. "I have written them down. I have not written them down here. They are in the back of the bench book at nine Pell Street and they have been since the twenty-fourth, and I want them to stay there."

Elias sat down on the other stool, which is not a thing they had done in the shop in two years, and the two stools are at the same bench and the bench is at the same counter and it is a repair shop and there is a chairback with a folded blanket on it and a cat asleep on the radio bench in the window.

"I want the dates," he said.

"I know you want the dates."

"I want them because dates are the only thing that turns a thing into a person, and I have spent this month learning that at four hundred credits a lesson." He put his own notebook out of his coat pocket and put it on the bench between them and did not open it. "I am not going to write them in there. I want to hear them and I want to know you have written them, and then I am going to write four things: today's date, the number four, and the fact that the book is yours. If I write the streets down in my book then a woman from a department is one question away from four kitchens, and I am not doing that to four kitchens on the day you are cleared of building something."

Jules Ardent looked at the notebook for a while.

"That is either very good or it is very convenient," he said.

"It is both," said Elias. "It is the second one because it is convenient for me and it is the first one because in the middle of October I had four sheets of paper on a wall in a waiting room and I worked out afterwards that the worst name on them was mine, and I have not got a better answer than that yet."

They did it in eleven minutes and it took eleven minutes because Jules made him go back over the second one.

"Fourth of September. Sill candle holder, brass, re-seated the collar. Nineteen credits, and I have never taken nineteen credits for that, and I took it that day because the man's wife was in the room and he had offered me a drink and the drink was still in my hand." He wrote it as he said it, in the back of the bench book, in pencil, with the date at the top of the line. "Seventeenth. Halloway side street, off the Verge Row end. A ward light on a landing, a bracket, four weeks of my time and a part I had to cut, and I did the whole thing and logged the fitting and did not log the bracket, because the bracket is a component and a component is not a working, and I have been telling myself that sentence since the seventeenth of September and I would like you to tell me whether it is a sentence or whether it is a place to put a thing."

"It is a place to put a thing," Elias said.

"Right," said Jules, and wrote that down too, and did not soften it.

"Twenty-second. A clinic, not yours. A dispensary in the outer wards and I will not write the name of it in this shop, and the work was four saddle clips and a length of conduit and the ward came up, and I did it on a Saturday at four in the morning because the place could not have a man in it in daylight." His voice did not change at all, which was the frightening part. "You know that one. You were there."

"I was there."

"Twenty-fourth. A man at his own door in Tanners Row. Walking stick, ash shaft, wanted a ferrule. I said I did not do that any more and I did not, and I came back on the twenty-fourth at ten past two in the afternoon because he was seventy-eight and he had asked twice and because I am a fool." Jules stopped writing. "I did that one properly. Licensed bracket, licensed collar, log book, receipt with a number on it, a card with four lines on it that I did not write, and a list of six households. I gave the tool away because he was seventy-eight and it was ten minutes and there was nothing in it for me."

Elias Venn sat very still on a stool in his own shop with a cloth in his hands.

"The twenty-fourth," he said. "Who was on the card?"

"Desmond Coyle. Forty-four, mobile, a van with a bench in it. He came to the door, and he told me the tool was for an old neighbour, and I have got that in the book because I wrote down the time and the reason like the module says." He looked up. "Mr Venn. I know what you are doing. You are asking me whether Coyle is a man you go and see."

"I am asking you what you saw at the door."

"I saw a man in a very good coat and good shoes stand in a lane in Tanners Row and be pleasant to an old man, and I saw him not come in, and I saw him look at the inside of that man's house over his shoulder for about four seconds while the old man was getting his coat off." Jules Ardent shut the bench book. "That is what I saw. I am not going to be the man who connects a coat in a lane to anything in my book, because the moment I do that I am not a witness, I am an instrument, and I have been an instrument twice in a month already and I did not enjoy either of it."

They sat with the bag of tools on the bench between them for a bit, and the cat came down off the radio bench and went along the top of the till and looked at the bag and did not want it.

"You gave a tool to a man in a lane and you did not log it," Elias said, "and you have just told me that, and on Tuesday a woman with a stopwatch read out a line about a seat in brass that was finer than anything you made, and that line is the reason you are not charged with building a frame."

"Yes," said Jules.

"That does not make you innocent of the other four."

"No," said Jules. "It makes me a man who did four small things in September and one thing in October, and if you are the one who has to write it down, write all five, because if you write four I will know you have written four, and I will not be able to work with you again."

"Five," said Elias, and wrote it: *25 Oct, five admissions in a bench book at 9 Pell Street. Not four.* He put the date and the count and the fact that the book is Jules's, and nothing else, and turned the notebook round so Jules could read the three lines and then took it back.

Then he said the other thing, because there was one more and it had been sitting in the room since Tuesday.

"Niko Senn is not the fifth."

Jules looked up.

"I had a fifth," he said. "The twenty-seventh. It was the same, it was a landing light in the outer wards, and it was a man who had been up three nights, and I did not do it, and I want you to know I did not do it. Coyle asked me in a doorway on the twenty-sixth whether I wanted it and I said no and I said no in front of his van. He did not argue. He said *suit yourself* and he got in the van." Jules put both hands on the bench. "I have thought about that doorway every day since Tuesday and I have worked out that I said no in front of a witness on purpose, and I have worked out that a man who asks a mechanic in a doorway knows that mechanics are alone all day."

"You did the right thing and it made you a name on a list," said Elias.

"Then it was the right thing," said Jules Ardent. "I did not do it for you and I will not have it in your notebook as though I did."

"Your book," said Elias. "And his date is his and his card is in his coat, and I have not asked him for it four times and I am not going to ask him for it a fifth, and the last time I refused it I was wrong about why."

"Were you?"

"I was right about the card and I was wrong about the reason, and I have had two months to think about it and the reason is that a card in my pocket would have made him a thing I was carrying." Elias folded the cloth. "Come on. Bring the bag."

"Where are we going?"

"The clinic. I want to say your five out loud in a room with people in it before the month gets any worse, and there is a man at the counter who counts heads and he has offered me one telephone I would use to say no." He picked up the bag and it was heavier than four tools. "Also you have a bench job there on Friday and it is two days away, and I would rather we went together than that you went on your own and did not tell me."

He carried the bag through to the back room before they went, because it had been on the front bench for half an hour and there were four jobs in the book for the week, and because the back bench was where he kept things that were not the shop's.

Otto Vahl was at the back bench with a wireless he had not finished and the till open beside him.

"Twenty-nine credits," Otto said, without being asked. "It was twenty-nine on Wednesday. There is a kettle element in the drawer for nine and a flex for two, and if you take the element we do the Cheetham job on Friday, and if you do not take the element we do it in the second week of November. That is the whole of the arithmetic and I have been doing it since Wednesday with nobody to hand."

"I will take it on Friday."

"Take it now. I do not want a book with one thing on it out of date on Friday." Otto wrote the price in the carbon book with a date against it, in his own hand, which was a thing he had started doing on the twenty-third of October and had not mentioned to anybody. Then he looked at the canvas bag on the bench beside it, and at the five things that were not in it.

"Whatever is in there," he said, "I do not want to know and I am not going to be asked. But I am going to say one thing about the drawer, because you have not opened it since Wednesday and it has not shut properly in four days and yesterday it shut, and I would like that on the record from somebody."

"Go on."

"That book." Otto put two fingers on the carbon book without opening it. "Twenty years of fuse wire and elements and flex, in date order, behind a bulldog clip. There is nobody left in this city who is ever going to ask to see it. That is the honest position and I am not going to pretend otherwise." He took his fingers off it. "But a shop with no trade account is a shop that has to be paid before it can buy, and a shop that has to be paid before it can buy is a shop that cannot lend anybody a bench for four months. That is what a Factors letter does. It does not take the till. It takes the next three months."

Elias Venn looked at the drawer, which opened clean.

"Then it does not get a bench," he said.

"It does not get a bench," said Otto, and went back to the wireless, and did not say anything else about it, which was the correct amount.

Jules Ardent was standing in the doorway with the bag, and he had heard all of it, and he said: "You could use my bench."

"No," said Elias.

"It is shut."

"I know it is shut. That is exactly why not." He tied the neck of the bag. "A man with a shut bench room offers it to the one man in this city who cannot be in a room with an officer in it without it being a thing on a form. If I sat in there in November somebody would ask you why."

"Then what do I do with the five," said Jules Ardent.

"You keep them in your book," said Elias, "and you keep the book in the room you cannot open, and you do not write another one."

They walked up Marshall Street at twenty past ten in the kind of rain that is not weather, it is just the air in Kettle Rows being wet. The waiting-room door was open and the smell came out of it: floor disinfectant, wet coats, forty-one people's worth of a morning, and under all of it the cold iron smell of a building that has a ward circuit and has not slept.

Dr Okafor was at the counter with a list on a clipboard and she was not reading it.

"Forty-one," she said, before either of them had said anything. "I have counted forty-one twice. I have got forty-three on the list and two of them have gone home, and there is one more on Ahmed’s pile that is not on my list at all, because Ahmed writes them on the back of a receipt, because I have never given him a proper book." She put the marker down. "And I am going to tell you the thing that has been sitting in my head since half past eight, and then you can both go home, because it is not an emergency and I would rather it were not for an hour."

"Say it," said Sana Okafor's voice, which was the voice of a woman who has run a clinic in a poor building for nine years and has had four of them.

"There is a man in the second row of the back chairs who has been here since twenty past eight and who has asked me three times whether he has already had his injection." She picked the marker up again and put it down. "He has not. I have checked the list twice. His name is on the list for the thirtieth and he is here on the twenty-fifth, and when I asked him he said he came on Thursday because it was Thursday, and then he said he was sure he had been and could not remember when, and then he asked me again about the injection."

Elias Venn stood in the doorway of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic in the rain with a canvas bag of borrowed tools in his hand and a man beside him who was very good with a ferrule, and found that the thing he had come to do and the thing that had happened to him were not the same size.

"How old is he," he said.

"Seventy-eight," said Dr Okafor. "He is called Alfred Neve. He lives at number three in Tanners Row and he has been coming to this clinic for two years and he has never once been late and he is never difficult and he is the sort of patient you put at the front because he will go home and do as he is told."
