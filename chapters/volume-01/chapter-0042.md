# Chapter 0042 — A Man Who Fits Ward Tools

The kettle was on the table in Alfred Neve's kitchen when Elias Venn arrived at ten past ten on Friday morning, and there were two cups on the table and neither of them had been used, and Neve said that was because he had made the tea before he knew whether there was going to be two of them, and that if there was one of them he would have had a cup in the sink and he would not have washed it.

"Sit down," he said. "I have got the thing out. I have had it out since Wednesday because I wanted to show somebody at the clinic and there was nobody to show."

He put it on the table between the bread and the letter rack, and Elias Venn did not touch it, and did not go near it, and stood where a man stands in another man's kitchen.

Number three, Tanners Row. Two rooms downstairs, a yard, a coal shed with a padlock on it that had not been opened since the summer. Seventy-eight years old, a widower of nine years, and the last of a trade that had gone out of the city in his lifetime — he had driven river traffic on the western cut until the barges stopped, and he had done thirty-one years of it, and he mentioned it once, in the middle of something else, and then did not mention it again.

The stick was a good one. That was the first thing and it was the thing that made Elias's stomach go cold, because everything else in this case had been cheap. Ash, a shaft worn shiny at the grip and not at the ferrule, a brass ferrule riveted on with a proper job, and a licence number stamped into the collar in a small neat punch.

"He put that in," said Neve, pointing. "Twenty-fourth. Ten past two in the afternoon. He was here twenty minutes. He said the collar was a licensed bracket and the number was on it and the receipt was in my coat."

"What did he call it?"

"A ward bracket. Threshold bracket." Neve took his hand away. "It holds the step."

He said it the way a man says a thing he has demonstrated twenty times.

"Show me the step," he said, and did not wait. He went out into the yard with the stick and put the tip on the concrete slab at the kitchen door and let go of the top of it, and stood there.

The stick stood up on its own. Not leaning. Standing, the way a handrail stands, with the ash shaft not quivering.

"I put a coin on it," said Neve. "First day. I had a two-credit piece and I put it on the step and I took my hand away and it stayed where it was. I have not been able to put that down in a sentence. It is a stick and it holds a step."

Elias Venn stood in the wet yard of number three, Tanners Row, and looked at a piece of ash standing on a concrete slab on a Thursday morning, and understood that everything he had come to do was going to be about a thing that worked.

"You have told somebody about it," he said.

"I have told two people." Neve was pleased. That was the unbearable part. "Mrs Achterberg at number fourteen in Kettle Rows — she has a hip like mine and she has been using a frame since the spring, and I told her the name and the day, and I said, when he comes, let him in, he does not want anything. And I have told the man from the brass shop on Tannery Lane because I asked him about the ash and he wanted to know where it came from."

"You have told two people the name and the day."

"And I would tell a third," said Neve, "because that is what you are for. You do not sit on a thing like that. If your own doctor gives you a thing that holds your step, you tell people. That is not being daft. That is being useful."

Elias Venn said nothing at all for about four seconds, and then said, "I am not a doctor."

"No," said Neve. "You are the man off the papers in the waiting room with the shop. I know who you are. Everybody in Marshall Street knows who you are, and it is not a bad thing to be, and I have read them and I did not understand one word of them and I would not want anybody to think I had."

That was the moment he understood how it was done. Not by frightening anybody. Not by hiding anything. By giving a man a free thing that works, and being pleased about it, and being the sort of man who recommends it.

"How long have you had it," he said. "Six days. Does it want anything. Batteries, charging, a service—"

"Nothing. I asked him. He said it is a bracket and it is brass and there is nothing in it that eats." Neve smiled. "He was in a very good coat, that is the thing. You do not get a very good coat fitting a bracket in a lane."

"No," said Elias. "You do not."

---

"What I have to tell you is not good news and I am telling you now so that you can be angry this evening instead of frightened tomorrow," he said, in the kitchen, with the tea finally poured and both cups used.

He told Alfred Neve three things. That a man had been at his door on 24 October. That the same man had written a card with four lines on it and a list of six households, and that Neve was the sixth, and that four of the six had already been visited. That there was a list at a booking agency in Draggery Street, and Neve's name on it for the thirtieth, entered at fourteen fifty-one on the same Friday, forty minutes after the bracket went in, and a second line on the same page that did not belong to a patient.

Alfred Neve put his cup down.

"Forty minutes," he said.

"Forty minutes."

"He said he was passing and he came to see about the bracket and I said I would have the autumn session anyway."

"You did nothing wrong."

"I am aware that I did nothing wrong," said Neve, "and I want you to be aware that I have been aware of it since about half past two yesterday afternoon, and I have not been able to put it down, and I have got up twice." He looked at the stick standing in the corner by the door where he had put it. "What do you want to do about the card."

"Nothing," said Elias. "It is yours."

"You have not asked for it."

"I asked you to show me the stick and I have not asked for the card and I am not going to." He wrote it in the notebook afterwards, not in the kitchen, in the lane: *N. keeps the card. Asked for the stick, not the card. Refused the card for the fourth time in this case and the reason is not evidence, it is that the last one I refused is in a coat of his own at number six in Alderney Row and I have not seen him since the tenth of October.* "If anybody comes from an office and asks for that card, you hand it over, because an office is an office. If a man in a lane asks for it, you do not."

"That is not much of a rule."

"It is the only rule I have got, and it is not nothing." Elias closed the notebook. "One more thing. May somebody look at the stick? Properly. A person who is allowed to, with you in the room, and with a piece of paper that says what they are doing and what you agreed to."

"Yes," said Alfred Neve.

Elias Venn stood in a lane in Tanners Row and did not use the yes.

"Not today," he said.

"You have just asked me."

"I have just asked you, and you have just said yes, and I have got no paper, no authority and no right to walk into a house on Kettle Rows and look at a man's property." He put the notebook away. "I am a member of the public. There is a difference between asking and doing, and I have spent this year learning it from people who were better at it than me. I will come back with something and I will show it to you before I use it, and if it turns out there is nothing to show you, you will have lost a morning and I will have lost more."

Neve considered that, and then said: "You will come Sunday?"

"I will come Sunday. Ten o'clock. And I will bring two people, and I will tell you who they are before I come."

"Will you tell the bracket man?"

"No," said Elias. "I will not tell the bracket man anything at all, ever, and if he asks you, you do not know me and you have never seen me in a lane."

"Mr Venn," said Alfred Neve, "I am seventy-eight years old and I have lived in this house since 1974 and I do not know how to lie to a man in a very good coat."

"Then tell him a man came about the session," said Elias. "That is not a lie. That is Tuesday."

---

At the Kettle Rows station, at twenty-five to twelve, Detective Sergeant Paul Rennick did three things and no more.

He said that a member of the public could take a statement of what a person chose to say about their own affairs, in that person's own words, and that it was worth nothing and it was still worth taking. He said that nobody could look at that stick, photograph it, or write down one figure off it until the owner signed something and the man doing it had an office behind him, and that the office in question was his and that he was not going to lend it to a man who had been censured for the third examination of an object in this city five days ago. And he said that the owner's address was now a fact with a name on it, and that he was going to write *Alfred Neve, 78, 3 Tanners Row, Kettle Rows* at the top of a page in his own file and that was all he was going to do with it, because there was nothing else to do with it yet.

"You could ask him," said Elias. "A formal request. In writing. To look at it."

"I could," said Rennick. "And if I did it today I would have told somebody in a lane in Tanners Row that a police officer wanted to look at his walking stick, and I would have done it on a Friday afternoon with no document that says why, and I would be finished by Tuesday." He closed the file. "You have got a date. Ten o'clock, Sunday, with two people. That is a better position than a warrant on an empty ground and it will still be a better position on Monday morning."

---

The session list came to the clinic on Friday afternoons from a booking agency in Draggery Street, and Dr Okafor kept it on a spike by the dispensary counter because a session list is the only thing in that building that tells you how many people are coming.

Elias Venn read it standing up, with Jules Ardent on one side of him, because Jules had asked what a booking list was and had never seen one.

*AUTUMN SESSION — KETTLE ROWS COMMUNITY CLINIC — TUESDAY 30 OCTOBER, 13:00–16:00*
*Attending: Dr S. Okafor. Staffing: agency, two nurses. Booked: 43.*
*13:30 — slot 1 … 14:10 — slot 2 … 14:38 — slot 3 …*

And then, under the times, in the same typed column, between slot three and slot four, a fourth line that had come in from the same machine with the same reference number at the head of it.

*14:45 — WARD TOOL CHECK (SERVICE ATTENDANCE) — SABLE SUPPLY, CLASS SUPPLY, PREMISES: 2ND FLR CARROW LANE — PAYMENT: ACCOUNT.*

Alfred Neve's name was in slot three.

"He told me the third one," said Jules Ardent.

"I know. And there is a man due to be in this building at fourteen forty-five on Tuesday, on a service attendance, on an account, in a clinic where the third injection of the afternoon is due at fourteen thirty-eight."

Jules Ardent looked at the list for a long time.

"They have booked the tool check for seven minutes after the injection slot," he said. "That is not an accident and it is not a coincidence and it is a thing somebody sat down and worked out. Somebody knows what the slots mean."

---

They went to the agency in Draggery Street at ten past two on Friday afternoon, and the woman behind the counter was called **Yvonne Pratt** and she was twenty-nine and had a cardigan on over a uniform and a telephone headset she took off one ear to listen to, and she was kind about it in the specific way that people are kind about a thing they have decided to refuse.

"I can give you the room number," she said. "It is on the door. I cannot give you a client list, I cannot give you a booking for a named person, and I cannot tell you who came in on the telephone this week. I do not know if I could do the last one and I am not going to find out, because if I find out once I will have to remember that I can."

"That is correct," said Elias Venn. "I want to write down what would open it, exactly, in your words if you will let me, and I am not asking you to break anything."

Yvonne Pratt considered him for a moment and then said, "You are the man off the wall."

"Yes."

"Right." She straightened the cardigan. "Then here it is, and I will say it the way the handbook says it, because the handbook is on the wall behind you and I have read it. A client list opens for a formal request in writing from an office that holds a record relevant to it, with a reference on it, and it is answered in seven working days, and the answer is a copy, not the original. Or it opens for the client's own written authority, given to the person asking, in the client's words and signed, and brought in by them or sent to us marked for the attention of the person who booked them. Anything else is a favour and I do not do favours, because a favour is a list that only exists in one person's memory."

"That is a better answer than I would have given," said Elias, and wrote it out in the notebook standing at the counter, and read it back to her, and she corrected one word — *copy, not the original* — and said that people always got that wrong and it mattered.

"Can I ask you something," he said, "and you can say no?"

"You are going to ask me about the ward tool line."

"I am going to ask you whether it looked odd when it came in."

Yvonne Pratt took the headset off and put it on the counter.

"It came in on Thursday at twenty to four with the rest of the Tuesday list," she said. "It had a reference like every other line and a class and a premises and an account, and it was on a Tuesday session at a community clinic, and I thought, that is odd, a service attendance in a room with a doctor in it, and I put it on the list." She looked at him steadily. "I put it on the list because it paid, and because it was not my job to decide, and because I have a wage and the wage is from the agency and not from the ward. I have thought about it since Friday morning and if you ask me whether I would do it again I would not know, and I have never not known before."

"You have told me," said Elias, "which is more than I expected and less than I need."

"I have told a man in a wet coat in a shop," said Yvonne Pratt, "which is what my aunt would call talking to strangers, and if anybody asks me tomorrow I will say I gave him the room number and the handbook and nothing else, and that will be true."

The agency line has a premises on it and the premises is on the same street as a print's, and I am not writing that down in this book tonight, because a line in a notebook is a road and I have got a man coming to Sunday who is seventy-eight and told his neighbour to let the fitter in.*
