# Chapter 0002 — A Room With Two Orders

The corridor on the top floor of the building had been turned into a corridor where people walked carefully, and by six in the morning it had become a corridor where people did not walk at all.

Elias had been awake for all of it. He had slept from about four until a quarter past five on Mrs. Achterberg's landing chair, which she had brought down herself and set beside her own door without offering it, and he had dreamed about the frame and woken with his jaw aching from clenching.

At ten past six the last run of the night came in from the river.

He was standing at his own landing when he heard it change pitch, the way it did every morning, and this time he did the arithmetic on purpose. Tamsin Ro had gone out at ten forty-eight the night before. Her shift ended at six. The depot's last inbound came in at ten past six, and she would have walked in from the corner, and she would have unlocked that door with her thumb on the ward, and she would have gone straight to the kettle.

He listened for the stair. It stayed quiet.

At seven, Otto Vahl came up from the ground floor with the cat under his arm, and then stopped so abruptly on the fourth landing that he put his shoulder into the wall.

"She said Thursday," he said. "She said if her shift ran over I'd feed it Thursday. It's Monday."

"I know," Elias said.

Otto Vahl was sixty-one and repaired radios in the back of the shop and had a way of standing that suggested he had once been shouted at professionally. He looked at the tape across the threshold, and at the two officers eating vending-machine sandwiches, and at Elias.

"She's not in there," he said.

"No."

"Then where's Tamsin?"

Elias had said this sentence once before, to a sister in a hospital corridor in a case that was not this case and had never entirely stopped hearing it. He found that he had to stand up straighter to say it again.

"Mr Vahl, come downstairs with me."

They sat in the shop office for eleven minutes, and the eleven minutes were the longest part of the night, and at the end of them Otto Vahl had his hands flat on the bench and was asking whether the cat could sleep in the office, and Elias said yes, and then said that the cat would probably be staying for a while, and Otto said, in the voice of a man who has already decided something, that the office had a chair he was going to keep there anyway.

Halvard Reisz, who owned the building and had been standing in his own doorway since half five looking like a man waiting for a bus that had gone past, came in at eight.

Reisz was sixty-eight. He held a ward certificate for the building's shared circuits that ran to three pages and expired in March, and every word of it depended on the top floor not becoming a certified dangerous working, and he knew it, and he did not pretend not to know it. He asked Elias, before anything else, whether the report would say that the building's wards had failed.

"Not yet," Elias said.

"If it does," Reisz said, "I will lose the certificate. And then the stair ward goes to a boot-leg installer in Saltmarket, and you may tell the tenants what that is like. I am not asking you to lie. I am asking you to tell me the truth in the order in which it becomes usable."

Elias wrote that down, because a man who tells you what he needs is more useful than a man who does not, and because it belonged in the log.

Wren Abasi arrived at nine with a Ward Works inspector's coat over a grey dress and a fold of printout in her hand that she did not offer to anyone.

She was polite. She was also, Elias understood within about a minute, extremely good at her job, and what her job was this morning was to get the room shut.

"I am going to want this sealed," she said, "and I want it sealed in a way I can defend, which means a residue lift before anything else goes in and touches the frame. I have called Saint Orra. Ms Quill says twenty minutes."

"Then take the lift first," Elias said.

"Not on your description. On a description that could go into a certificate, please."

"Certificate," said Nadia, from the doorway, "has not been requested."

It had in fact been requested, at 7:20 that morning, by a Proof Registry clerk who had called the Kettle Rows station and had spoken to a different sergeant, and the request was sitting in an inbox and would be answered without them if nobody answered it here. Elias did not say that. He had already done the arithmetic on how many of his careful impulses were going to end up written down as obstruction, and the answer was enough of them.

Abasi put her printout on the bench table and turned it round.

"Detective Sergeant. This is a standard scene residency declaration, and I need the answer in your own hand before I go in that room, because whatever you tell me now, somebody else will read it at some point in a worse mood than mine."

It was a form. It was, as far as he could see, a completely reasonable form: name, business address, whether the officer assigned to the investigation resides at the premises, if so, in what capacity, and for how long.

Elias read it twice. Then he picked up the pen and wrote, in the box marked *resides at premises*, *Yes — second floor, above own premises, shop and flat, eleven years (on this site since I was twenty-five)*, and in the box marked *nature of interest*, *None. Tenant at will. No financial interest in the room above.* And then, in the free line at the bottom, because it was true and because he had decided at four in the morning that he was not going to spend the rest of this case being the man who hid the thing that mattered, he wrote: *Assigned to this investigation by proximity, not by application. Discovered the report. I live in the building and I am the nearest Sequence Desk sergeant available at 01:52. Any party may treat this as a conflict and should.*

He signed it and pushed it back across the table.

Abasi read it twice, which told him she had expected something smaller.

"Thank you," she said. "That was the right answer and it is going to cost you."

"That," said Nadia, "is the first accurate thing anyone has said this morning."

What Abasi wanted next was the physical scene, and she was thorough about it, and Elias had the two officers walk her through it while he listened from the corridor because he had already decided he was not going into that room again.

And it cost him the case, which he only understood later and which he wrote down in his own notebook that night in a single line he did not show anybody. He could have gone back in. The door was where it had been and the sill was exactly where Mara had lifted from it and the residue on it had not finished settling, and there is one thing in this city a man can do that no instrument can do, which is stand in an unstable room for nine seconds and be told the order of it. He thought about that for as long as it took Abasi to photograph the desk and the chair and the mug, and then he went and stood at the top of the stairs instead, because a second reading would have been worth nothing in a file and would have cost him the first one, and because a plate with a policeman's handprint in its history is a plate a competent defence takes out of the case in four months' time and never has to explain.

The physical scene said one thing. The chair was over. The mug was out, and it was not broken, and it had gone a distance of about two metres, which a mug does when a table is struck rather than when a table is knocked. There was a handprint on the desk that was four fingers and no thumb, and it was pressed hard, and it had been dragged. The bed had not been sat on. The window was shut, and its own small latch, which nobody had ever once seen a tenant use, was on.

"There was a struggle," Dace said. "There's a struggle and then there's no struggle. Nothing's knocked over except the chair, and the chair's knocked over in a direction. People knocking things over in a fight knock them over everywhere."

"And the takeout," said Abasi.

"The takeout is the takeout," Elias said. "Or it isn't. Ask the depot what she's been doing for four years."

The times were the worst of it.

Mrs. Achterberg was certain she had heard a woman speaking on the top landing somewhere around half past eleven, and she was worried about that, because Tamsin Ro's voice was unmistakable. Dace's neighbour, awake with a fever, was certain the noise from the building came at twenty past twelve, and had heard a bus horn with it, which put the noise after the last bus had come in. Otto Vahl had been awake and had heard nothing at all, which put the door's own opening somewhere he could not help with.

The Orra depot put the last inbound of the night shift at ten past six, and the previous one at eleven minutes past midnight, and its horn is a two-tone that can be heard for four blocks.

"A woman speaking on the landing at half eleven," said Abasi, writing, "and a horn at twenty past twelve. Those are fifty minutes apart and they are both true and they do not go together."

"They don't have to go together," Elias said. "But they do have to both be residue-compatible, and half past eleven is the better part of an hour after she left for work. Somebody was on that landing at half past eleven, and she was not."

He had the feeling of a hand on a door and no way to open it. He wanted very badly, the way he wanted it on nights when the old file was open on the table in his own apartment, to say the next sentence out loud, because the next sentence was in the file and the file had been the only true thing in his life for twelve years.

*No release was recorded at the threshold.*

He said it. Not to anybody, exactly. He said it under his breath the way a man reads a line, and Wren Abasi, who was writing and who had the ears for it that a person writes for a living, stopped writing.

"Where is that from, Sergeant?"

Elias's mouth had gone dry.

"An older file," he said.

"Which file?"

"A file I have at home. I have not opened it. It is not this case and it is not in this building, and I have not put a hand on it since I came out here at two o'clock this morning, and if you want it you are going to have to ask me for it in a form I can answer, because the moment I open that box I stop being a person investigating a room and start being a person with a motive, and I would rather not be that man in this corridor."

Abasi looked at him for three full seconds.

"Thank you," she said. "That is the second useful thing you have said to me, and I am going to be very careful with it." She wrote something on the form, tore off the bottom, and gave it to Nadia. "That is a note that you have declared a prior interest and a document you have not produced, and that I have asked and you have declined. If anyone in this case ever wants to say you were compromised, that paper says you said so first."

Nadia read it and pocketed it without looking at Elias, which was the correct thing to do and which he noticed anyway.

Mara Quill came in at eleven past nine carrying a hard case and a satchel and the flat, unhurried expression of a woman who had been awake since six and would be awake until four.

She was thirty-five and she ran the small independent residue laboratory attached to Saint Orra General, which was a phrase that meant she had four instruments, two of them older than the department she worked beside, and a waiting list. She had met Elias three times, disliked two of them, and had been polite on the third.

She listened to Abasi for ninety seconds, asked four questions, and did not once look at the door.

Then she said, "I am going to lift the threshold now, in front of both of you, and I am going to say out loud what I do at each step, because if I do not, then in four months' time somebody will say that I was alone in a room with a frame and a policeman who has already told us he can see things."

"Recorded," said Nadia, and held up her own phone.

Mara set a salt-glass plate on a folded cloth on the corridor floor. It was about the size of a paperback and it was not transparent, exactly. It had the look of very old ice.

"I am lifting residue from the sill, the jamb, and the underside of the door leaf, in that order," she said. "I am lifting nothing from the desk. It is not mine to lift and it is not part of the question yet."

"Elias," said Abasi.

"I am not going to touch it," Elias said. "I said I would not and I will not."

"Recorded," said Nadia.

It took nine minutes. Mara worked with a small vacuum hood and a glass rod and an expression so completely without performance that Elias found it difficult to look at directly, because there was nothing in it to answer. When the third lift was on the plate and set, she wrote the date and the time and the address on a paper sleeve, and signed the corner of the sleeve, and turned the sleeve so the signature faced outward.

"Two witnesses have observed the lift," she said. "I am going to want one of them to countersign, and it cannot be you, Detective."

"I know."

"You may hold the sleeve."

Abasi signed it, and dated it, and did not ask to see the plate.

He looked at the plate. He looked at the plate the way he looked at a car that had been in an accident and had been put back on the road, which was the most dangerous way available to him to look at anything.

He did not reach for it. He put his hands in his pockets instead, which was the first genuinely difficult thing he had done all day, and the difficulty was visible, and Mara saw it and said nothing about it, which was the second genuinely difficult thing she had ever done for him.

They came out into the street. The rain had stopped and left the whole district shining in a way that made the sodium lamps look like an argument. Nadia put a hand on Elias's arm for about a second and then took it off again, which is how Nadia says *not now*.

"I'm going to take her up," Mara said, tilting her head at Abasi. "And I'm going to take the plate. Not to the department. To Saint Orra. The department's cold store has been at nineteen degrees since March and everything on this plate will be water in a week, and I would rather be the person who can be asked why than the person who has to answer for a puddle."

"There is a request for certification on this case," Abasi said.

"There is a request," Mara agreed, "and there is not a finding. Those are different objects and I would like the difference to survive the morning."

Then, on the third step down from the room, Mara stopped and turned back and looked at the door the way she had looked at plates for six years — not at the door, exactly, but at a very small part of the edge of it, near where the jamb met the sill, in the part where a line of paint had been disturbed by two drills and two fillings.

"Say that again," she said, to nobody.

"Say what again?"

"You said her working stopped at the threshold. You have said it four times today and each time you stopped dead, which is what people do when they are about to say the next part." She was not looking at Elias. She was looking at the door. "Her working stopped at the threshold. There is something on this side of the threshold."

Elias felt the old cold in the back of his neck that he had got used to expecting and never got used to.

"How much is something?"

"Thin," Mara said. "Thin, and — give me a second."

She got out a lens that was not a lens, and she crouched, and she took perhaps twelve seconds, and then she stood up and looked at Elias with an expression that for the first time in three meetings contained something other than professional reserve.

"It does not match her," she said. "It does not match the door. I would bet this plate and my own hands that whatever that is, neither she nor anybody who licensed that frame made it."

"Then what is it?"

"I do not know," said Mara Quill. "That is not modesty. I genuinely do not know, and I have a very specific reason to suspect that I will not know for about nine hours." She put the plate into the hard case and closed it and took the satchel. "I am taking it to my laboratory and I am measuring it, and I will call you before I do anything else at all, including eat."

She went. Abasi went up the stairs with two Ward Works officers to take the rest of the room properly. And Elias Venn stood in the wet street outside his own building in a district where three thousand people had noticed a cordon and eleven thousand had not, with four minutes of his own life missing and a signed piece of paper in a police pocket stating that he lived here, and the discovery sitting in a hard case on a bench that was not his.

The threshold had two orders in it. One of them belonged to a night-bus dispatcher who had fed a cat on Thursdays, and had boiled her kettle dry on the sill on her way out, and had gone to work on time for four years.

The other one had no owner that he could find, and it had been put there by somebody who had wanted the room to say something else.
