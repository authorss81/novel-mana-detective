# Chapter 0037 — Bay Six

Saturday evening Elias Venn sat in the back room with the tail end of the laboratory still in him and one number in his notebook that he could not account for: *WL-44/09-07?*, written down with a question mark against it because Jules Ardent had half-remembered it from a delivery docket he had seen in August and had not been sure of the last two figures. Beside it he wrote: *Rent paid monthly cash. Water metered. Somebody keeps a book.* The Cut was not one dungeon. It was a legal maze owned in fragments, and every fragment had a keeper with a book. Find the keeper and you find the door without breaking it.

Felix Harrow kept the rent book for the bays off Cutwater Passage in his own hand, in a back room above a chandler's with a kettle that took fourteen minutes and a door he locked behind him. He was fifty-eight, had let bays by the month for twenty years, and had buried one tenant and evicted two, and he told Elias Venn so on Sunday morning at half past nine while Mara Quill stood with her case in both hands and Jules Ardent stood with his bench book in his pocket and his hands where they could be seen.

"Bay Six," said Harrow. "Low Cut, off Cutwater Passage. Two rooms. Rent paid monthly in cash, always on the second, always in the same hand on the stub. Water meter read quarterly. Lamp mine, kettle theirs, door with a Yale I fitted myself." He opened the rent book on the counter without letting it leave his hands. "I will not open a door without the tenant's word or an officer. That is not suspicion. That is twenty years."

Elias nodded. He had expected this and had brought the only things that could lawfully move it: a member of the public's asking, an officer's decision, and two people's competence.

Paul Rennick arrived at ten with his camera in its case and his file note from Friday in his pocket. He read Harrow's terms, wrote his number on Harrow's form, and signed under it while Harrow signed above.

"Read and photograph in place," said Rennick. "Nothing seized, nothing cleared, nothing pulled."

Harrow locked the rent book away, took the Yale key for Bay 6 off its nail, and led them down.

The Cut on a Sunday morning was a lived-in legal space, not a monster's lair. Water ran in the channel beside Cutwater Passage. A lamp burned over a doorway where a woman mended nets. A boy carried bread. The vault doors stood in a row with numbers painted above them, rent paid, water metered, doors closed by people who closed them behind them. Bay 6 stood at the end with its number fresh and its Yale oiled.

Harrow knocked twice, called the tenant's name, and opened it with Rennick watching every move.

The workshop was two rooms with a bench, a vice, a fixture bolted to the bench, swarf in the tray, stock on the shelf, a kettle on a ring, a lamp over the bench, a corkboard with invoices, a worklist on a nail, a daybook on the bench. It smelled of oil and hot metal and tea. The bench was worn in the middle where hands had worked. The fixture held a length of channel section with a seat half cut. Everything about it said ordinary skilled work done by people who believed it was legitimate.

Jules went first to the bench with his hands behind his back and read the channel in the order he reads all metal: width, wall, lip, seat.

"Same profile," he said quietly. "Finer than mine. Same as the stockroom carrier."

Mara put her case down on the bench and did not open it.

"I am going to say what I will not do in this room," she said, with Harrow listening and nobody in a hurry. "There is a channel in a fixture with a seat half cut in it, and there is a bench in it, and if I put a plate against that surface and take a figure, then the first number anybody has out of this bay is mine, and it will be argued with for years by people who were not in the room. I have had that argument twice. So this morning I take no figure. Rennick photographs the bench where it stands and I initial the margin as the person who declined to measure, so that the declining is on the page with the room."

Rennick wrote it on his file note, which is how a thing like that stays a fact instead of becoming an argument, and photographed the bench, the fixture, the half-cut seat and the length of channel in one frame, camera moved and nothing moved, with Harrow present and Jules Ardent as second witness in the margin. Then Mara closed the case again and put her hands in her coat pockets. Elias stood with his hands on the bench edge where Harrow could see them and wrote nothing.

The worklist hung on a nail by the bench. Rennick photographed it in place, camera moved, objects not moved, Harrow present. Then, with permission, Elias copied it from the photograph into his notebook at the bench, with the time, 11:34.

*WORKLIST WL-44/09-07, 2 Oct 2029. 12 channels, seat per DRG HW-CS-03 Issue B. 18 credits each. Delivery carrier bag, shoulder.*

The material on the shelf stood in plain sight: forty sheets of four-tenth foil, lengths of galvanised conduit, channel section in bundles. The callout on the drawing — *galvanised channel, foil 0.4* — sat on the shelf as stock any ironmonger would sell. The tolerance nobody writes on a small seat sat in the fixture as a cut no drawing dimensioned. The price sat on the worklist as eighteen credits. The delivery sat on the worklist as a bag on a shoulder. Two men could sit at that bench, take that work, and believe every word of it, and one of them could be telling the truth about why he was there.

The corkboard held the paper that made the room human. A supplier invoice for channel, cash, collected by courier, headed *Sable Supply* with a licence class and a premises on the face of it: *Class SUPPLY. Premises 2nd floor, Carrow Lane.* Orin Sable, on paper, at a distance, as a trading name and a class on a supplier invoice — not caught, not identified by face, not the person who drew anything or taught anybody anything. Nobody touched it. Rennick photographed it where it hung.

The commissioning paper sat in a tray under the bench: a typed order, dated 28 September, on Municipal Works Contracts Office paper, signed with a name and a title.

*Ordered: 12 ward continuity units, seat per drawing to be confirmed. Scheduler E. Caul.*

Emmett Caul, fifty-two, works scheduler at the Tanager Street contracts office, the man who scheduled approved contractors against the fault log. A job, a standing, a reason: keep reported faults down, keep the district's premium defensible, keep the wards running. Not a villain. Not Orin. Not the hand that made anything. An intermediary with a job, on paper, with a reason anybody could read without being told what it meant.

Felix Harrow watched all of it with his arms folded and his Yale key in his hand.

The two bench workers stood where they had stood since the room was opened, saying little, working when asked to show and standing back when asked to stand back. Elias watched them without sorting them. Kester Vane kept the bench clean, wiped the vice after every showing, asked Harrow after his wife by name, and handled the channel with the care of a man who believes it keeps wards running. Donal Pryce did the hours, took the money, kept his eyes on the floor, and answered questions with the shortest true sentence there was. The room needed both of them to run.

"They pay on the second," said Harrow. "Always cash. Always polite. One of them sincere — keeps the bench clean, asks after my wife. The other paid — does the hours, takes the money, goes home. Do not make those two identical, gentlemen. They are not."

Rennick wrote his file note on the bench: *Attended Bay 6, 21 Oct, looked, photographed in place, nothing seized.* Harrow countersigned the attendance. The worklist stayed on its nail. The daybook stayed closed on the bench — the tenant's book, not yet opened, not yet asked for, the line on its fourteenth page still unspoken.

Niko Senn's name came up once, because Elias put it there to keep it out: *Niko Senn is not in this room and will not be brought into it. His name is not on any paper here.*

They came up out of the Cut into Sunday noon with the lamp still burning over the net-mender's door and the boy coming back with empty hands. Harrow locked Bay 6 behind them, put the key back on its nail, and wrote the attendance in his rent book in his own hand.

Rennick stopped on the steps with his camera in its case and capped the pen, because that was the end of his three. Then he looked at Elias.

"Two things more, and they are not for the file," he said. "One: the order on that tray is from a scheduler who keeps his own book. If you want him, you want him on paper, through the office, not through a door. Two: the book on the bench stays closed until the tenant says otherwise. I photographed the worklist because the worklist hung on a nail. The daybook sits on a bench. Those are different things, and you know it."

"I know," said Elias. "Tenant's word or nothing."

Harrow nodded once, slowly, which is what a man does when a stranger gets something right about his tenants. "I will knock on his door tonight and ask him whether anybody may photograph one page of his book where it lies. I will not tell him who, and I will not promise him an answer, and I will not bring four of you to a door he has not opened."

"That is the whole of what we are asking," said Elias.

"The room stays," said Rennick on the steps. "Located, not dismantled. Nothing sealed, nothing cleared, nothing catalogued, and nothing moved that is not moved by the man whose it is. If anybody is going to pull a bay apart in this city they will do it with a schedule and an officer who has one, and nobody on this step has either."

Elias walked back with Mara and Jules, none of them speaking much. He had found where the frames were built. He understood the method: bench, swarf, fixture, stock, worklist, delivery, price, belief. The graded series on the clamp, in the bench room, on the stockroom carrier led here: a title block, then a register, then a bay. The room was still there behind them in the dark with its kettle and its lamp, and it would still be there on Tuesday when the charges were read.

At the shop he wrote the day out full with the times — *Bay 6 located 21 Oct 10:30–12:10, looked, photographed, nothing moved* — and put the notebook on the shelf. The daybook in Bay 6 sat closed on its bench with the line on its fourteenth page unopened. Tomorrow they would go back for it if the tenant's word came back, and if it did not, it would stay shut, and a book nobody has opened is still a book that is there.
