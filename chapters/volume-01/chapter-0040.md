# Chapter 0040 — Standing Open

Tuesday evening Elias Venn sat in the back room with the fortnight's costs spread in front of him on the bench, and counted them the way a man counts what is actually left in the tin: the censure on the shelf, the Factors letter in the drawer, the charge sheets in Rennick's file, the daybook photograph in 441-KR with its original in Bay 6, the register behind its counter, the four sheets on their wall. He wrote until ten, slept badly, and woke on Wednesday to rain on the yard and the cat on his feet.

Felix Harrow would not reopen Bay 6 on Wednesday morning without the tenant, and the tenant was at the Sequence Desk answering bail, so Elias Venn stood outside the vault door at nine o'clock with Mara Quill and Jules Ardent and looked at a closed door, which was the whole of the point.

The number above it was fresh. The Yale was oiled. From inside came no sound — no bench, no kettle, no lamp. The worklist hung on its nail in there. The daybook sat closed on the bench with page fourteen in it, photographed but not seized, original staying where it was. Twelve channels at eighteen credits. A seat cut to a drawing. An account line with an insurer's name in it, in a bench hand, on the third of October, next to a frame. Nobody had sealed anything. Nobody had cleared anything. And nothing had been catalogued either, because cataloguing takes a schedule and an officer with a schedule, and as of Wednesday morning in this city there was neither for that bay.

"The room stays," said Harrow, with the key on its nail upstairs and his rent book in his hand. "Located, not dismantled. That was the agreement. It stands open in the dark when I lock up, and it stands open in the dark when I open. I have two other tenants whose doors I have not opened this year and I am not going to start with the one I have just written four names into my own book about."

Rennick, who had come to log the attendance and nothing else, wrote it: *Bay 6 viewed 24 Oct 09:00, door closed, no entry, no seal, no clearance.* Harrow countersigned. The photograph of page fourteen sat in 441-KR. The original sat in Bay 6. That was the thread, and it was still only a thread.

Jules put his hand flat on the vault door and took it off again without trying the Yale.

"I have been in and out of rooms in this city for nineteen years," he said. "Half of them I should not have been in. This is the first door I have ever wanted to stand in front of and not be able to open, and I find I am not sorry about it, and I do not entirely know what to do with that."

"Write it down and ask somebody in a month," said Mara. "You will still not be able to open it, and you will have a sentence about it that is yours instead of mine."

They walked up out of the Cut into a Wednesday that smelled of rain and bread. Jules went to Pell Street to sit in a room with his charge sheet and his clearance and his bench book, all three in front of him. Mara went to Saint Orra Reach with two logs to file and two witnesses to sign them. Rennick went to Morrow Hill to put the viewing note in 441-KR and to be told what a Saturday costs a custody chain. Elias walked to Kettle Rows alone, because Wednesday was for counting.

He stopped on the way at the market for bread and oil, writing each down because a man with no accounts writes everything down twice. The stallholders knew him. The fish pitch where Mavis Brannigan had traded for eleven years stood shuttered. The tea woman asked after the board in the clinic. He answered truly — the pages are holding, the heating is on now, nobody has put a finger on them — and walked on with the city moving Wednesday around him: porters, nurses, children, a bus with rain on its windows.

---

The shop's books did not balance. He laid them out on the back bench in the morning light — till book, Factors withdrawal letter, censure, the two receipts from Fallow Street, the rent book in Otto's hand — and did the arithmetic out loud so that Otto could hear it and correct him.

"Eleven days of takings," said Elias. "Four repair jobs logged and paid. Rent to the first. Twenty-nine credits in the till when I opened, and by the end of this week I am spending it instead of earning with it, because a man who pays cash for parts has to have the cash in front of him before he can take the job."

"Cash," said Otto. "Repairs logged. Rent paid to the first. Cat fed."

"Cat fed," said Elias, and wrote it: *24 Oct, shop with no accounts, file closed, censure filed, rent paid, cat fed.*

The drawer that had been wedged shut for four days and then left wedged shut out of habit opened clean, and inside it the carbon book sat in date order with a bulldog clip behind it, twenty years of fuse wire and elements and flex, and it was now a book that nobody would ever ask to see again. He shut it. He did not take it out and he did not throw it out, because a man does not decide on a Wednesday that a thing is rubbish, and because in November or in the spring somebody might come into that shop and want to buy something, and the two of them would want to see how a trade account is kept.

Niko Senn came at eleven, on his own, with his hands in his pockets and his aunt's cake tin under his arm. Whether he had sat down in a room at the back of Concord Hall on Thursday morning was not a question Elias asked, because a man decides that for himself and finds out by other means.

"Mr Venn," said Niko Senn. "I am not asking about the sixteenth. I am telling you it is still not finished and still has my name on it as well as hers. I wanted you to hear me say it in this shop."

"I hear you," said Elias. "The card stays in your pocket. The date stays in yours. I am not making either into a route."

"I know what a route is," said Niko Senn, without heat. "I have been one twice. That is why I am standing in a repair shop saying a sentence out loud instead of putting it in a letter, because a letter ends up on somebody's desk and this goes in your book and your book is on a shelf in your own shop."

He put the cake tin on the counter, nodded once, and went out into Kettle Rows with his hands in his pockets. Wen Senn's cakes sat on the counter with the lid on and a bit of paper wedged under the rim with the date on it in the old woman's hand, because Wen Senn put a date on everything she baked and had done since before the counter was hers, and Elias put the tin on the shelf beside the urn and did not open it until he had written *Niko Senn, 24 Oct, date still his, card still his* in the notebook and closed it.

At noon the telephone rang and it was Dr Okafor. She said the ninety-odd pages were holding now that the heating had come on, and that the leak over the dispensary had been reported three times and fixed once, and that she had begun, without deciding to, writing the number of every person who came in to look at the board in the night porter's book, in her own column, on her own line, because it had turned out that eleven different people in a fortnight had stood in front of four sheets of paper and not one of them had been asked to sign anything. They talked for a while about ordinary things — the waiting-room chairs, the porter's book, whether the heating could be left on an hour earlier in the morning — because ordinary things were what the board was for, and because she had rung about a leak and he had four jobs in the book before two.

Then she asked after the shop's books, and he told her truly: no accounts, cash henceforth, rent paid to the first, and a Factors counter two streets away that had known his name since he was twenty-five. She was quiet for a moment.

"The clinic pays its suppliers late in winter and early in spring," she said. "Everybody in Kettle Rows knows how to do cash. If you ever need a kettle element for a ward cooker and you cannot get one, telephone me, and I will telephone you back saying no."

"Yes," said Elias. "Thank you. I will not abuse it."

"You will not," said Dr Okafor. "That is why I am offering it."

At one Nadia rang to say the carrying of paper to Rennick was minuted and closed, and that this was not the last thing. They did not speak long. A favour recorded is a favour that can be counted, and both of them knew somebody would count it. She asked whether he had slept, and he said four hours, and she said that was not enough, and he said he knew. Then she said something she had clearly thought about before dialling.

"The review I signed in September is going to be looked at again at some point by somebody who was not in the car park," she said. "When it is, I am going to write down that on the seventeenth of October an inspector carried one side of paper to an officer at my request, and that the officer decided, and that neither of those two things was in her own hands. I am telling you now so that you cannot tell me afterwards that you did not know."

"I did not know," said Elias.

"Good," said Nadia, and rang off.

He spent the afternoon moving the carrier bag of the 406-page production from the bench under the window to the shelf above it, which took him four minutes and made him feel better than anything else he had done since Friday. The shelf now held, in one row and in the order he had decided on without meaning to: the folio, the stamped copy of CS-3, the notebook, the notice about the breach, the censure, the Factors letter, the two Fallow Street receipts, and the bag. The register stood behind its counter on Cutter Street. The daybook stood in its bay off Cutwater Passage. The four sheets lay on a wall in Marshall Street. The photograph of them lay in a file on Morrow Hill, which is not a copy. The receipt stub lay in his wallet with the counterfoil in Nadia Kest's book. Marguerite Osei's note lay in his inside pocket, unanswered, because an answer is correspondence and correspondence has a file on it, and because a woman who wrote *I am refusing a person* deserves a reply written by somebody who understands that she can tell the difference.

Aegis Mutual was in that notebook now, once, in the middle of a copied line, and he did not write it out again. He had not said the name out loud in that bay, and he was not going to start saying it out loud in a repair shop over soup. A risk office that prices off reported faults, a Lantern Compact member, a payer's name in a bench hand beside a frame and two named wards: a customer, a book, an account, and nothing else. Why an office like that would be paying eighteen credits a channel to have seats cut to a drawing instead of buying continuity units from a licensed supplier was a question he had ruled a line under on the inside cover of the notebook and left sitting there, unanswered, with a second line under it saying he would not ask it out loud before somebody in that building had been charged and had had a lawyer and had said whatever he was going to say.

At six he fed the cat, who wanted the desk as well as the dish and got both. At seven he ate soup standing up. At eight he logged the fortnight in four lines rather than one, because one line had begun to look like an advertisement for itself:

*15–24 Oct. Register refused at a counter, read in place on the Friday by an officer. Sixteen Sept asked twice, twice in books, twice not meeting. Bay 6 found and left standing.*

*Reading taken 14:10–14:16, cost to 18:40, route, reported by the officer, led out by handrail.*

*Charges read Tuesday. Censure signed. Factors account withdrawn after twenty years. One clearance, in two books, on measurement.*

*Room still open in the dark. Insurer's name in a ledger line, carried and not shouted about.*

Then he put the pen down, went to the front bench, and stood for a while with Otto while the ceiling made its noise and stopped.

"A workshop standing open in the dark in the Cut," said Otto. "A ledger with an insurer's name in it. A man charged and a man cleared. A repair shop with no accounts."

"That is the fortnight," said Elias.

Otto pulled the blanket off the radio bench and folded it over the chair back, and the cat followed him to the front and sat in the window on the ledge where the urn gave off its heat.

Elias slept upstairs from ten with the window open on the yard, the shop below him with its drawer that opened clean and its books that did not balance, the bay below the city with its bench and its book, and the city above with its wards flickering once and holding.
