# Chapter 0048 — The Second Room

Felix Harrow was standing in six inches of water in the yard of the bays off Cutwater Passage at ten past six in the morning with a fire officer's line in one hand, and he was not satisfied with it, and he said so to two strangers on a landing and then again to a detective sergeant at seven.

"The lamp is off," he said. "The ring was cold. That is a stone floor and a stone wall and twenty years of my keeping and the fire is in the back room under the stock rack and not at the lamp, and I have been letting this stair since 1974 and I have had one death in it and that was a natural one and I did not have to call anybody about the natural one."

The fire officer was a woman of about fifty called Dalian Oyelaran and she had been there since a quarter past five, and she was not unkind about it and she was not going to put anything in a report she could not stand behind.

"I have written cause undetermined," she said. "I have written the seat of it as the floor under the stock rack and the heat pattern out from there, and I have written that the lamp was cold and the ring was cold, and I have written that the water came from a bucket and a hose and a hydrant on Cutwater Passage and not from a riser, and that is the whole of what I know and I have been in this job for twenty-two years and undetermined is not me being lazy."

"Then it is undetermined," said Rennick.

"It is undetermined," said Dalian Oyelaran, "and I would like it in your schedule as undetermined, and not as anything a man has decided, because the day somebody decides it, this becomes a story about a fire, and it was a room with a lamp in it."

---

Bay 6 was two rooms and a kettle and it is one room now.

The front room had its bench and its vice and its fixture and its corkboard and its lamp and its kettle and a smell of oil that the water had not got to, because oil does not wash. The stock rack along the back wall was a black iron thing with the shelf burnt off it in two places, and behind the rack the back room's door had gone, and through the gap you could see about four feet of a floor and the black lower half of a wall.

The worklist was not on its nail. There was a nail and a pale rectangle on the plaster where a wire used to be and nothing hanging from it.

The daybook was not on the bench. There was a wet shape on the bench the size of a daybook and a spine that had not entirely gone, and under the shape the wood was black.

The supplier invoice was not on the corkboard. Eleven invoice pins and eight invoices, and the ninth space had a pin in it and a corner of paper that had not burnt, which was enough for two letters and part of a class, and Rennick photographed it where it hung with the rest of it, camera moved, objects not moved, and then put the last pin in a bag of his own because it was loose in a burnt hole and it was going to fall.

Jules Ardent came down at half past eight because Felix Harrow had telephoned him at seven and because a bench is a bench, and he stood in the doorway of the front room for about a minute and a half, and then said:

"I am not going to look at that bench and tell you what I think."

"Jules—"

"You were cleared on measurement on the twenty-third of October, and the clearance said a machinist's eye comparison in my own bench book, and that is the only sentence about that bench that is worth anything and it is in a room on the second floor in a file. If I stand in here and say *that is a fixture I have not seen the like of* then in about two years a lawyer will stand up and read it out and ask me whether I was paid, and whether I was cleared, and whether I have slept since." He looked at the black rack. "I have a charge and a clearance and a die block with the Registry and I am not putting a third thing in my mouth this month. The room is burnt. That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I am saying it to you and not to him, and if he writes it down he can write it as a man who declined."

---

Detective Inspector Nadia Kest had done the only thing that made it possible, on the Wednesday night, from a corridor in a hospital with a notice on the door behind her: she wrote a schedule.

It was two sides. It had a number on it and a time on it and her name at the bottom of it, and it said what it was for in a single sentence, which was *recovery and cataloguing of items in a rented workshop, it is not a search of the premises, and the removal of any article is the act of the keeper of the letting and is recorded by the officer and not authorised by this document.* She had taken it to Rennick at half past nine on the Wednesday night and he had gone through it line by line and put his own initials beside hers on every line, and had said, "This is the schedule you said would be needed and I said in October that there was not one. I was talking about October. There is one now, and you have made it, and I have initialed it, and neither of us is to be pleased about how."

The item list ran to a page and a half and Harrow read it standing in the wet, twice, in the two rooms, because he wanted to know before anybody went into the back one, and then he said, "You will take his tools."

"Only the ones on the list."

"You will take some of his tools," said Felix Harrow, "because a tool is on a list and the man who wrote the list has never been in this room. I am not saying that to be difficult. I am saying it because I have kept that rent book in my own hand for twenty years and I will be asked about it, and I would like a piece of paper for the tools that are not evidence, with my name on it and a number on it, because in six months somebody will come and say we took a man's vice."

Rennick wrote him one. It was a receipt, it had a schedule number on it, it said *items not seized and not logged as evidence, released to the keeper of the letting*, and it named the vice, the hand drill, the bench vice jaws, a kettle, and two lengths of conduit, and it said the room had been cleared of police property and that the keeper held the residue. Harrow read it, and countersigned it, and gave it back, and put it inside his coat, and then said:

"Mr Rennick. I have let that bay to two men since June and both of them are charged with you, and one of them asked after my wife every single morning for fourteen months, and I am not going to say one word about either of them today. But I am going to tell you what the room was. It was a room where two men made twelve pieces of brass for a hospital and were paid for them and thought they were doing a real trade, and it was the only trade in this city I have ever let that was honest about being new, and somebody has burnt it, and I am sixty-one and I am going to scrub it out and let it again, and if I never let it again that is my business and not yours."

---

They brought the footlocker out from under the bench at ten past nine, and it had not burnt, because it was under the bench and the bench is heavier than its own drawing.

It was a footlocker with a hasp and no lock, and inside it there were eleven things, and Detective Sergeant Paul Rennick catalogued each of them on the loading step of the yard in the rain with a camera, a torch, a set of scales from the van, a printed schedule with a number in the corner, and a witness who was Felix Harrow because a landlord is a witness to a room and cannot be moved off a step.

He photographed the footlocker open where it stood on the step with all eleven in the frame, camera moved, objects not moved. Then he photographed each one in place, in the order they came out, and lifted it, and logged it, and put a numbered tag on it that was tied with a piece of string, and put it in a crate, and sealed the crate, and wrote the seal number on the schedule and on the crate and on his own book.

Every line was the same shape, and the shape was the point: *item number, description, where found, by whom, seal, store.* And under every line, on all eleven, the same four words: **not identified to a person.**

Four of them were like this.

*Item 1. Wristwatch, ladies', stainless, wrist strap missing, crystal scratched. Stopped. In a cardboard wristwatch box, lid off. Found in the footlocker, base, under the folded photograph.*

*Item 2. Hair clip, blue plastic, child's, one of a pair. Found loose in the footlocker, not in a box and not wrapped.*

*Item 3. Spectacles, one lens, in a case, case cracked across the hinge. Found in the footlocker, top tray.*

*Item 4. Tin, japanned, with buttons in it, and inside the lid a name scratched into the metal with the edge of something, about two inches long, and eleven letters, the first one a capital.* **Not matched.** *Found in the footlocker, on top of the tin of screws.*

And seven more, counted, weighed, tagged and sealed: a bridle strap rotted at the buckle; a brass door key on a fob stamped with a street number; a ring of nine keys with no labels and a tin tag; a folded photograph, wet through, of about twenty men outside a factory gate; a brass dog's name tag; a tortoiseshell comb, cracked; a child's left shoe, size about two, with a buckle.

"It is eleven," said Rennick. "It is not thirteen and it is not nine. It is eleven objects and none of them is a name yet, and the work of this schedule is to make them names, and that work has not been done."

Then he added the two lines that made the page what it was, and read them out before he wrote them.

"Item 12. One length of channel section, split-ash seat, recovered 30 October 2029 at 14:47 from treatment room two, Kettle Rows Community Clinic, on the plate of M. Quill, log 1201, unseized and not in this room. Cross-referenced."

"Item 13. One walking stick, ash shaft, licensed threshold bracket, sleeve removed." He wrote it slowly. "Not recovered. Last seen at 14:52 on 30 October 2029 in the possession of an unidentified male leaving the premises of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic. Direction of travel not established."

---

At nine twenty, standing in four inches of water in a yard a hundred and forty feet below the city — it had been six inches deep at ten past six, and nobody in that yard could say when it had started going down — Elias Venn found out how long it had been since the water stopped coming in.

He had been looking at the black rack for about four minutes and he had no idea, and then he had forty-two hours and forty-two minutes, all at once, in the middle of it, in the middle of a sentence somebody was saying, with no warning at all and nobody announcing it. It came back the way it had come back on the twelfth of October in a corridor and the way it had come back on the twenty-second of October on a shop step in Kettle Rows: not gradually, and not as a memory.

He said, out loud, to nobody, "The fire's out."

Felix Harrow turned round. "Since when."

"Since about twenty to nine," said Elias Venn, and then stopped, because he had no way on this earth of knowing that, and because he had just found out. "I can't tell you that. I have been unable to tell how long anything has been going on since twenty to three yesterday afternoon and it has just come back in this yard, and I am telling you I have just found out, and not that I know."

Rennick wrote it on the schedule's continuation sheet with the time against it, which was 09:20, and wrote it the way Nadia Kest had taught him to write a thing he had not witnessed, which is with the time on it, and with the word *reported* on it, and with his own estimate left out of it: *duration perception reported absent by the member of the public from 14:38 on 30 Oct 2029; returned 09:20 on 1 Nov 2029, in the yard, unannounced; the member of the public states he cannot give an account of the intervening period and has not estimated one.*

"You are the third person in this case who has come back from something in the middle of a sentence," said Rennick, "and I have written all three of them down, and not one of them could say afterwards what the missing part was for."

---

They emptied the room out completely, and it took the rest of the morning and it is the part of the day nobody has ever asked him about, because it is four men in overalls and a yard and a barrow and a landlord with a rent book.

The bench, the vice, the fixture, the foil on the shelf in eleven bundles, the channel in lengths, the swarf tray, the lamp, the kettle, the corkboard and its pins, the bare nail, the daybook's wet shape. The corkboard went on the barrow whole because it was nailed to the wall and it was cheaper to take the wall than the board. Everything went into the crate store at the yard on Fenwick Street on a schedule, and the schedule came back signed at both ends, and the front room of Bay 6 was a room with a floor and four walls and a light fitting with no bulb in it by one o'clock in the afternoon, and Felix Harrow came down with a scrubbing brush and a bucket and started on the wall behind where the bench had been.

At ten forty, on the step outside, Rennick read out of his own file the photograph he had taken in that room on the twenty-second of October, with the daybook flat against the page in front of it so the ink stood up out of its own shadow.

"There is one page," he said. "There was one line on it and I photographed it in place on the twenty-second of October at two twenty-eight in the afternoon, camera moved, objects not moved, original staying where it was. The original is ash this morning. This photograph is the only copy in the world of that line and it is the reason anybody can say what it said, and I have never in six years been glad of a photograph the way I am glad of that one."

He did not read the line out loud. He wrote it into the schedule's continuation sheet, in his own hand, and then he did read that sentence out loud, because he had written it and it needed saying: *original document destroyed; photographic record held by the officer on 441-KR; no other copy exists; the premises is a registered one in a city with a register.*

Then he produced the other thing, which Nadia Kest had sent over by hand at nine that morning in a sealed envelope with a receipt, and which Ahmed had picked up off the clinic gate on Wednesday morning when he went up to put the closure notice on the door.

A collection docket. Small, thick, printed on a machine that was not the agency's machine, and folded in three.

*Sable Supply. Class SUPPLY. Premises: 2nd floor, Carrow Lane. Collection, self-drive: 14:52, 30 Oct 2029. Depot: Northgate. Booked to: call-off, 6 days.*

"14:52," said Nadia Kest, from the top of the step. "That is the minute Ahmed timed the gate for and nobody in this case has ever given me the minute before."

Rennick read it twice, and then put it on the crate with the tags and logged it, and said the thing that goes in a schedule when there is a document and not a name.

"I am recording the premises because it is on a piece of paper, and I am recording the depot because it is on a piece of paper, and it is north of the ring road, and I am not recording a person, because there is not a person on it. If somebody later puts a name to that address I will be glad to look like a fool in a schedule, and if nobody ever does, then a man came out of a gate in Marshall Street at four minutes to three on a Tuesday afternoon and there is one more document in this city with his route on it and not his name."

Elias Venn stood in a yard in the rain with a document in a book that said a trading name and a class and a registered premises, and made himself say the thing out loud that he had been not-saying for a fortnight, in a lane and in a notebook and in a bed on a Wednesday.

"I am not writing a name against that address," he said. "And I want it written down that I said so at 10:52 this morning with a landlord, a sergeant and an inspector standing on it, because in about four years somebody is going to come and ask me why there was no name on it, and the answer is that there was no name on it and I wanted it on the page that I noticed."

"Noted," said Rennick, and wrote it down, which is the third time a document in this case has been kept from saying more than it says by a man with a pencil.

Elias Venn went up out of the Cut at half past one into a Thursday afternoon in Kettle Rows with rain in his hair and a duration he had got back in a yard, and stopped on Marshall Street outside a closed clinic with a notice on the door and a sheet of polythene over a board, and stood there a while, and then went home and put the door up and logged the day.

*Thu 1 Nov. Bay 6, from 06:10. Front room cleared, back room burnt, daybook gone, worklist gone. The only copy of page 14 is a photograph and I have never in my life been glad of a photograph and I am now.*

*Harrow's rent book is the only paper left that ties that bay to two named men, and he will have it at home tonight in his own coat.*

*Eleven objects, and the work of the schedule is to make them names, and it has not been done. A tin with a name scratched in the lid, eleven letters, first one capital. Not matched.*

*Duration back 09:20, in the yard, unannounced. Forty-two hours and forty-two minutes, which is the gap and not the point.*

*The docket says 14:52 and a depot north of the ring road and a trading name and a class and a premises. No person on it. I said out loud at 10:52 that I am not writing a name against that address and Rennick wrote it down. That is a thread now and I could not have made it into a road in four days.*

He got back to the Cut at half past four because there was nothing else to do with the rest of a Thursday and because Felix Harrow had said he would be in there until it was dark.

The front room of Bay 6 was a room with a floor and four walls and a light fitting with no bulb in it, and the back room was a doorway with four feet of black floor behind it, and the crate store on Fenwick Street had the schedule against it with two signatures on it, and the whole of what a room is for had been put on a barrow in an afternoon by four men in overalls and a landlord with a rent book.

Felix Harrow was on his knees at the back wall with a scrubbing brush and a bucket, working at the black, and he had a radio on the shelf behind him at low volume and it was a set about the size of a car battery.

"That is a Ward Works set," Elias Venn said.

"It is a set I bought in 1979 and have never had anybody in for," said Felix Harrow, without stopping. "Twenty-eight watts and it pulls a set of lights and it has never once worked, and I have told that to four men tonight and three of them wrote it down." He moved the brush along a join in the plaster and the black came off it in a wet grey roll, and behind the black it was a wall, and it had been a wall for a hundred years. "That is a workshop," he said. "That is what it was, and it is going to be a workshop again by Christmas, and I have got a man coming on the eleventh of December who does picture framing, and I shall want the floor mopped twice and I shall want him to know that two men were taken out of here by an officer in October and that one of them asked after my wife."

He said it to the wall, and the radio played to the empty room, and Elias Venn stood in the doorway of a bay a hundred and forty feet under the city with a duration he had got back in a yard six hours earlier and nothing at all to do with it.
