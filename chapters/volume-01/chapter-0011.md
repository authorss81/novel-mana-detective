# Chapter 0011 — Until Tuesday

He woke at twenty to seven on Saturday morning in a chair beside his own bed, with the shop's day book open on his knees, and the first thing he did was try to account for the previous day and the first thing he found was that he could not account for the previous day at all, only the parts of it other people had put in writing.

Saturday the twenty-second of September. Yesterday was the twenty-first and yesterday was a Friday and yesterday he had read a sheet of paper at a counter without touching it and had refused a countersignature at twenty to five and had cut a latch plate and put nine inches of galvanised steel in both his hands and had lost forty minutes somewhere between a yard on Marshall Street and a car.

The day book said the date. The shop clock three feet from his shoulder said seven minutes to seven. Both of those were the kind of record that cannot be argued with, and neither of them told him anything about his own Friday.

He had been on the pavement outside the paint works from ten past two until about a quarter to three, sitting on a kerb, and then he had walked to Marshall Street with his legs not doing what he had asked of them, and then at four he had gone back into the dispensary stockroom and stood in a doorway and taken a photograph of two objects that were lying where a man had left them, and then at four eleven he had sent Ahmed a message.

After that he had gone home. He had not gone upstairs first. He had sat on his own stairs for a while.

---

His phone was on the bench where he had left it and it had eleven messages on it, which was three more than he had ever had on a Saturday in his life.

The first was Nadia's, at 04:11, the two lines about a photograph.

The second was at 07:16, from a number he had never had, and it was a photograph of himself.

He looked at it for a while. It was taken from about ten feet away and slightly below, which meant the man had been on the other side of the yard wall or standing in the gap between the paint bins and the wall, and the angle told him that and the light told him the time: the sky behind him was the colour it goes about forty minutes before a proper sunrise in the last week of September, and the sodium lamps along Marshall Street were still on, and the wet on the paint bins had not dried, which meant the rain had stopped less than twenty minutes before.

He was standing on the third bin. There was blood from his nose down the front of his shirt and his right hand was out at his side with the fingers still curled, and his left hand was up near his mouth. He looked, he thought, like a man who had been doing something in the dark on purpose.

Underneath the photograph, from the same number, there was a second image. A screenshot.

It was a group conversation. He had seen that name before — it was the name of a residents' group for the block, four hundred and some people, mostly old, mostly awake at this hour because of the ward light, and he was in it because Halvard Reisz had put him in it in June when the stair light was replaced.

The photograph had been put into it at 04:52, by a woman whose display name was a car emoji, with no caption at all.

Below that it was forty-one replies long by seven o'clock in the morning. He read them the way he read everything, which was too fast and then again.

*That's the one off Kettle Rows.*
*That's the police one who lives under her.*
*Someone tell me that's not the police.*
*He let her in. He was in the building, everyone knows he was in the building, he came out at two in the morning.*
*My grandson goes to that clinic. If that's him then I want to know why the lights went out on Friday night at my mother's.*
*Report it. There's a form.*
*There's always a form.*

And then, at 06:58, five minutes before he had woken up in the chair, a message from a name that was a man's first name and nothing else, and it was the only one in the forty-one that he read twice.

*Whoever sent you that photo, the man in it was in a building with nine people in it all night. Ask him what time the lights went out. Then ask him why the clinic's lights are on now and his street's weren't.*

He sat with his hands on his knees.

Somebody had sent him his own face, and somebody else in that building had spent the night noticing that the ward light over the dispensary counter came back up at four o'clock in the morning and that the light on the Kettle Rows street did not, and had done the only sensible thing a tired person can do, which is to work out that those two facts were in the same person's hands.

He had not eaten since Monday. He had a cup of cold coffee on the bench that he had made at ten past six and would not drink, which was the oldest habit he had.

---

Otto Vahl had the shop shutter half up and the radio on the bench behind him going through a carrier hiss it had been going through since the spring, and Tamsin Ro's cat was asleep in the office chair with its front end in a paint tin.

"Eight o'clock," Otto said. "You were out till half three and then you sat on my step at twenty to four in the morning looking at the wall."

"You were awake."

"I was up at two because that thing on the ceiling has been making a noise like a wasp since Wednesday." Otto turned the volume down. "Whatever you did, the cat slept through it."

Elias got the shutter up the rest of the way by hand, which took him eleven minutes and would have taken him four in June, and then he made a list on the back of an invoice because the day book was open on his knees at home with the wrong page in it. Four things. He wrote them in the small bad hand.

*Stockroom door cut at the latch plate, one hinge, cannot be secured. Fire officer, first slot offered: Tuesday, ten o'clock. That is the honest answer and the desk has stopped offering me anything else.*
*Nine minutes off the ward line, 23:54 to about ten past midnight, every circuit but the dispensary's. Dispensary's back on a new length of conduit at four, hung by J. Ardent, unpaid, unsigned.*
*Two objects photographed where they lay, one frame, from the doorway, at 04:00. Nothing picked up. Nothing seized. A. speaks the date and the time and his own name onto the recording.*
*Ward light, stair, second landing, late by four seconds, then normal. Third one this month.*

The fourth line was the one that had been bothering him since ten to two on Monday and that he had been putting off in three different directions for a week, and it took him about ninety seconds to admit that he had been putting it off because he did not want it in writing, and that wanting it not in writing was the actual fault.

*Stair ward, second landing. Ballast replaced by me in spring. Contractor who owns the certificate has not signed off. Light runs about a second and a half late and has done since March.*

He put a line under it.

*If anybody asks whether the wards in that room failed, the answer is in this book and it is not the same answer as the one in the paper. Nobody is going to ask me. I am going to have to say it.*

That was a sentence he had written and was not going to say out loud to anybody, and he looked at it, and then he did not scrub it off, because a thing you can wipe off is a thing you have not written down yet.

---

Half past seven on Marshall Street. The dispensary counter made the sound a room makes when it has decided to be a room again, and the ward light above it was on and steady, which was new, and which took him a second to work out the reason for: a new length of galvanised conduit, nine inches longer than the old one, hung at the ceiling by two brass saddle clips that had come out of a piece of evidence.

Ahmed was on the desk with a thermos and the Saturday paper and a plate of something that had been fried at some point this week.

"You came in at four," he said.

"I did."

"I went home at ten past four and I have been thinking about it since." Ahmed turned the paper over. "I have been thinking about a thing and I would like to put it somewhere other than my head."

"Go on."

"The night clerk. The one of seventy-one. I keep coming back to her, and I have decided I am not going to stop coming back to her, and I would like that written down as a thing about me rather than a thing about her." He took a pull from the thermos. "I have worked nights for nine years. I have let men into that building who should not have been in it. You do not stop them. You are seventy-one and he has a vest and a document and he is very certain, and there is no version of that Thursday night where she is a villain. I have been trying to make her one since ten to two this morning because it would be easier, and she is not one."

"She isn't."

"That is what I have decided. If it turns out she was, I would like somebody to tell me and I will carry that too." Ahmed looked up. "I have also decided that you are going to want the corridor and I am going to walk you down it, so that you are not the first one through with a camera today."

---

The corridor was cold and the north door was off its frame at one corner and standing eight inches open into the room, and the room was unusable.

That was the plain fact and nobody in the building was pretending otherwise. The dispensary stockroom was a brick box with a steel door, and on Wednesday night a nine-inch length of galvanised conduit had been taken off the wall over the door head, and the door had been opened by cutting the latch plate, and the two halves of the conduit and the foil bonded inside it were in two rooms behind a hospital linen office under a woman's own name.

What was left was shelving, a steel bench, a cold room, and a patch of bare brick about a foot square at the height of a man's shoulder with two sets of screw holes in it and nothing else.

"There is a notice on the frame," Ahmed said. "Dr Okafor put it up at half past seven yesterday morning before she went home. It says the room is out of use and do not store anything in it, and the fire officer has rung twice, and he says Tuesday, and I say Tuesday, and she says Wednesday, and that is the whole of the argument we are going to have for three days."

"Until Tuesday."

"Until Tuesday," said Ahmed.

On the bench twenty feet away, where it had been since ten past nine on Wednesday night, a tin of surgical staples was still holding down a single sheet of transfer paper, face up, and it had been photographed where it lay at four o'clock that morning by a man standing in a doorway who had not touched it, and it was in nobody's file, and everybody who had walked past it since had thought it was a piece of paper.

"It is going to go," Elias said, more to himself than to Ahmed. "Not today. It will go."

"Yes," said Ahmed. "I have been thinking about that too, and I have decided I am not the man who moves it, because the last time a thing moved in this building without somebody saying so, nine people were standing in the dark."

---

Jules Ardent came at nine with a spirit level and a roll of tape and no invoice, and he was on his knees on a stepladder in the cold room when Elias came in to say good morning, and he did not get down.

"Three weeks," he said, without turning round. "Three weeks it has been ticking. I put a shim under the bracket in the spring and it did not help and I did not write it down, because writing it down makes it a fault instead of a thing I knew about."

"Write it down."

"I have." He tapped the tape measure against the ladder. "I have written down every tick in that building since Wednesday night, with the times, and I have given a copy to Dr Okafor and a copy to the fire officer's office and one in my own bench book, and there are three copies of it in this city and one of them is not mine. That is the whole of my contribution and I would like it noted that I am on an escalator at four o'clock this afternoon and I have been since Wednesday."

"Anything I pay for—"

"You are not paying me for it and you are not putting my name on it and we have already had that conversation in a stockroom." He came down two rungs. "I used the clips. That is all. I took two clips out of nine inches of conduit that came off a wall in a room with a working in it, and I used them on a new length, and that is the only reason anybody can account for them and I would like that written down somewhere before somebody comes and asks me where a pair of brass saddles went."

Elias wrote it down on a fresh page of the Sequence Desk day book, in the small bad hand, with the time on it, which was a thing he had been forbidden to do in a room with a bench in it and was now doing on a Saturday morning in his own shop because nobody had told him to stop.

---

Niko Senn was awake at half past nine, sitting on the edge of his bed in room two with the blanket folded, and he had a rash on the inside of his forearms that had gone from red to a colour that Dr Okafor did not like.

"Two things," Niko Senn said, before Elias got the chair out from under the door. "One, I heard somebody in the corridor at about six this morning telling a lad it's the police one who killed her. He wasn't saying it to me. He was saying it where the corridor does that."

"Yes."

"Are you going to do something about it?"

"I'm going to give a photograph of it to a police officer with a file number," Elias said, "and I am going to be honest with you about what that is worth, which is that it goes in a file and the file does not go in a room. There is a woman outside that building right now who is more afraid of you than of me, and she has been since Wednesday, and it is not fair to her and it is not fair to you and neither of those things is going to stop this week."

Niko Senn looked at the window.

"Two," he said. "There's a woman come round last night with a form about the ward light and she was very nice. She asked me if I wanted to report it. I said no. But I said it about four seconds after she asked, and I did not know why I said it that quick."

Elias did not write that down. He wrote the time of it down, in his own notebook, and no words at all next to the time, which was a compromise he made with himself and which was not good enough and which he would do again.

---

Mara Quill came in at ten with a plate of food and put it in front of him on the counter where the transfer order had been read on Friday, and did not sit down, and did not discuss it.

Elias ate all of it. It was eggs and it was too much salt and it was the first thing he had put in his mouth since Monday morning that he had not had to force, and he noticed that the man who had put it there had done it at precisely the moment in the morning at which a person who has not eaten for four days stops being able to make decisions, and that she had known that, and that she was not going to explain it.

"You are going to the magistrate at eight," he said. "You told me last night at about half three in a car."

"I told you on Friday at twenty-three forty-one on a telephone, before I did the thing, which was the part I was actually worried about." She took her coat off. "I am going to walk into a room with a man I have annoyed on a telephone at half past midnight and he is going to tell me in person what he thinks about it, and he is entitled to, and I have decided that the correct response to that is to bring the file and to let him."

"Do you want me there."

"No," said Mara Quill. "You would do it honestly and that is precisely the problem, and in about a year you will understand why I said no, and today you will take it as me not wanting you in a room with a man in a wig."

He took it as that.

At half past eleven his phone rang and it was Nadia, and she was in a car and she sounded like a person who had been in a building for two hours and had come out into fresh air.

"The entry is papered," she said. "I have a stamped sheet with the application time and the entry time on it and a note in the margin, and the note has my name under it. He read me the note. It says the interval between the warrant and the entry is to be recorded, and it does not say I exceeded it, and it also does not say I did not, and he has written it that way on purpose, and he told me he had written it that way on purpose."

"Twenty-three forty-one to twenty-three fifty-four."

"Thirteen minutes, which is the same thirteen minutes that your frame came off the wall in." She let that sit. "I am coming back in at one. I want you at the board at one. Do not go anywhere before you have written on the board where you are going, because that is the condition and I have had a morning where I would very much have liked it to be somebody else's."

"The condition is the condition."

"Write it where I can see it." A pause. "And Elias. Before one. There is a thing I have had in my own file since Thursday that I am going to hand you in front of a witness, and I am not going to explain it on a telephone, and I want you to understand that I have been holding it for four days and that I have not been holding it off you. I have been holding it off the department."

That was the whole of the call. It took two minutes and a half, which was the shortest conversation the two of them had had since Monday.

Elias put the photograph on the counter in front of her at twenty to one, the original, the one from the number he had never had, with the screenshot of the forty-one replies printed underneath it, and he gave her the number in full and the time of the first caption and the name of the group and the fact that forty-one people had written under a photograph of him by seven o'clock in the morning.

"Item," he said. "Received on Saturday morning at sixteen minutes past seven by the assigned detective from a number not in the case address book. Photograph of the assigned detective taken at the paint works during the working. Circulated to four hundred and some residents of his own block within one hour. I did not trace the sender. I have not asked anybody to trace the sender. I am recording it as an item because it exists and because a file that does not have it in it does not have the case in it."

Nadia Kest read the caption line, and then read the second one, and then looked up at him over the top of the phone.

"*He let her in,*" she read.

"Yes."

"That is the sentence that is going to cost this case money, and it is not going to cost it a conviction, and it is not going to cost it a witness, and it is going to cost Halvard Reisz his certificate and it is going to cost that clinic nine people a morning." She put the phone down. "I am going to do something about it at eleven o'clock on Monday and by then it will be three days old and there is a version of me that will tell you that three days is the same as one day and there is not."

Elias wrote the four lines up in his own day book, and then wrote a fifth under them, and then put the day book on the shelf behind the bench where it lives, and hung the shutter the rest of the way, and did not wash his hands, which was a habit he had started on Monday and which was, as of Saturday, apparently permanent.

It was twenty past two. He had until Tuesday.
