# Chapter 0039 — What a Charge Does

Jules Ardent did not sleep on Monday night. He sat in the scullery at 9 Pell Street with the bench book open, the charge that had not yet been read hanging over him, the clearance that had been measured but not yet spoken sitting beside it. At seven on Tuesday morning he walked to Kettle Rows, knocked on the shop door, and asked Elias Venn to walk with him as far as the Sequence Desk, not inside, just as far as the steps.

"I would rather go in with somebody who told me the truth about which notice," he said.

They walked together through a Tuesday that smelled of rain and baking. Jules talked about the bench room he could not open, the die block in the Registry, the two runs in August and September with a carrier bag on his shoulder for money he needed. He did not excuse himself. He stated it as a man states a measurement: exactly, without softening, because softening would make it worthless.

At twenty past eight they were on the second landing at Morrow Hill with the stair door open behind them and nobody on it, and Jules stopped and put his hand flat on the rail.

"You told me a drawing exists," he said. "Reference only, and you stuck to the reference. Now tell me the rest of it. Because at half past nine somebody is going to read a piece of paper in that room with my name at the top of it, and I would rather have had it from you at twenty past eight than find out at twenty past nine."

Elias took the notebook out, did not open it, and said it in his own words instead, which is what he had promised in a shop doorway on Friday: *I will tell you in the corridor if it is going to be read in a room with an officer in it.*

"Fenwick and Marsh, twelve Cutter Street. The register says the sheet was drawn on the twenty-eighth by two initials, T.B., checked by two more, and issued on the thirtieth. An earlier issue went out on the twenty-fourth, drawn on the twenty-second, with the depth marked *to be confirmed*. Six days between them, and the number that fills it was certified on the twenty-seventh by a woman whose name is on her own plates. No more than that. Then on Saturday, at ten o'clock, a woman whose name is on her own plates put a plate of her own against a paper copy of that sheet and took a number off it, and you read the seat on the paper copy and said it was finer than anything you ever cut, inside four thousandths, and wrote three lines in your own book with the date on them, and on Sunday you read a channel in metal in a bay in the Cut and said it was the same profile and finer still. She initialled the margin. Two books, two hands, and both of them say the same thing."

"That is all of it?"

"That is all of it. I have not seen a clearance and I cannot promise you one, and if there is not one you will be charged with building a frame you did not build, and I will be standing in that room when they say so."

Jules did not say anything for a moment. The stair door swung on a draught and the noise of the building came up through the shaft.

"Four thousandths," he said. "Eleven figures on my clamp. I have never been able to hold four thousandths on a seat that size and I have been chasing it for nineteen years."

"No," said Elias. "And it is written down, with your name and the date on it, in a book you wrote and a log she wrote, and neither of those can be got at by asking me nicely."

"Then that is what I will have," said Jules. He took his hand off the rail. "If it is not in there I will come and find you, and you will not enjoy the conversation."

"You will not have to," said Elias.

They went down the last flight together, and at the bottom Jules made himself stand in the corridor outside the charge room doors two minutes early with his hands at his sides where everybody could see them.

The charge room at the Sequence Desk smelled of floor polish and paper on Tuesday morning, and Elias Venn sat in the public chairs at nine o'clock with his hands on his knees because he had no standing to sit anywhere else.

Jules Ardent sat on the bench with Kester Vane and Donal Pryce. The notice was still on his door at nine o'clock, wired to the handle with its seal showing, and the bench room was still shut, the die block still in the Tool Registry, the uncollected second set still frozen inside the maker's enquiry. He wore his good coat. His hands were clean. He had not slept.

The duty sergeant read the sheets once, as documents, without commentary, in the voice of a person reading a thing that is not hers to explain.

*Kester Vane, 34, Bay 6 Low Cut: charged with assembling unregistered working frames, twelve channels, worklist WL-44/09-07.*

*Donal Pryce, 41, Bay 6 Low Cut: charged with assembling unregistered working frames, twelve channels, worklist WL-44/09-07.*

Donal Pryce cried, once, into his hands, and stopped. Kester Vane stared at the wall. The room wrote it down.

Then the second paper, and the third.

*Jules Ardent, 29, 9 Pell Street: charged with carrying other people's work through The Cut for payment — two runs, 28 August and 11 September, carrier bag, shoulder — contrary to the ward works schedule.*

Silence. Then:

*On measurement: cleared of building the frame. Workshop seat finer than clamp JA-4417 — the comparison is a machinist's eye comparison, written in the maker's own bench book and not disputed by anybody in this room. Figures of M. Quill cited: her logs of 20 and 22 October. Maker's enquiry to remain open on the die block; practice notice lifted.*

At the back of the room a telephone went and a constable wrote a note and took it out to the stairs to be read to somebody, and came back in and put the telephone where it had been. Nobody announced anything. Elias watched Jules instead, and saw the man's shoulders come down about half an inch, and then go tight again, because a charge was still on the table and one line had been lifted off it.

Elias stood in the room while it was read, three paces behind the bench with his hands visible and no right to be any nearer. The clearance was the first thing anybody had given Jules Ardent since August, and it arrived as a measurement, not a kindness — one line on a sheet, about a seat in brass, read out in a room that smelled of floor polish, by a person who had never met him.

Jules did not move for a long moment. Then he nodded once, to nobody, exactly as he had nodded in the fourth chair of a waiting room on the tenth.

Nobody charged Elias Venn with anything. Nobody charged Orin Sable with anything. The supplier invoice stayed pinned to a corkboard in a bay in the Cut. The man in the very good coat stayed out of the room. That was how the arithmetic came out, and the desk recorded it by not writing it down.

Afterwards, in the corridor, there was a trolley of tea going past and nobody taking any. Kester Vane sat on a bench with his sheet in his lap, staring at nothing. Donal Pryce stood by the window with his hands in his pockets, crying quietly and not hiding it. A constable waited with forms. Life went on being life while paper changed it.

Jules stood with his charge sheet in one hand and his clearance in the other and said the only true thing about either of them.

"A charge is rent," he said. "Two runs for money I needed. The clearance is the bench. Finer than mine means not mine. I can live with carrying. I could not have lived with building."

Kester Vane came past with his sheet and did not look at anybody. Donal Pryce's wife arrived with a coat over her arm and took her husband's sleeve, and he put his hand over hers and let her lead him, and the two of them went down the stairs without speaking to the man who had told him, at twenty past eight that morning, something true about a measurement, and had then stood three paces behind the bench and said nothing at all while it was read. Charge rooms empty like that: quickly, without ceremony, with lives changed inside envelopes.

Elias walked with Jules as far as the steps, no further, because a member of the public does not escort a charged man.

"I will come to the door before any notice," said Elias. "As agreed."

"I know," said Jules. "You have so far."

The departmental censure arrived at noon, by hand, at the shop, while Otto Vahl stood at the front bench with a chassis open and the cat asleep on the blanket.

*Departmental censure, Sequence Desk: E. Venn, formerly D/Sgt, breach of filed prohibition 29 Sep, third examination 22 Oct Bay 6, with written consent M. Quill and witness D/Sgt Rennick. File closed against him. Not a formal reprimand.*

Elias signed for it, because a man signs for what is his. The cover note said the file was closed against him — the retention review, the breach log, the censure in one envelope — and that a formal reprimand, if any, belonged to a different process on a different day, with a hearing he had not been given and a clean certificate he had not been offered. That was still in front of him, somewhere, on somebody's desk. It was not this.

He put the censure on the shelf above the bench with the notice about the breach, on top of the folio, where he could see it. Niko Senn was named in none of the charge sheets, and Elias checked twice to be sure, because a young man of twenty-four with a withdrawal in a file must not appear in a charge room by accident.

He sat for a while in the back room with the censure in front of him, reading it as he had read the practice note on the eighth: slowly, without moving, until the words stopped being words and became a fact. Third examination. Written consent. Witness. File closed against him. Not a formal reprimand. This was the trade standing going: the department saying it was better off without him on the file, in writing, with a reference number, and a Factors account that had been in a drawer under a till for twenty years about to go with it.

Otto came through with tea in two mugs and set one down without a word. They drank standing up, because there was work to go back to. The cat slept on the radio bench. The ceiling ticked.

"You still mend kettles," said Otto at last.

"I still mend kettles," said Elias.

At two he walked to Cutter Street Factors with the monthly statement in his pocket and the trade book under his arm, because a withdrawal is a thing a man goes and stands in front of.

The Factors occupied a narrow office above an ironmonger's with a counter worn smooth by twenty years of elbows. Elias had stood at that counter since he was twenty-five, repairing radiators for a man who took cash, then for himself. The clerk who served him had changed twice; the book had not. He laid the trade book on the counter with both hands visible, which was the shape of the thing he had not laid on Draper's counter, and said he had come about the letter.

The clerk was kind, which was worse. She read the letter out before she handed it over. Competent people do painful things that way, face to face, with the date on the paper and her own initials in the margin of the file copy.

*Venn Repairs trade account, twenty years: withdrawn, connected certified record, account pending. Cash for parts henceforth. No reflection on workmanship.*

"Do you want to tell me what a connected certified record is?" said Elias. "Because I have never heard those three words used together by a person and a shop."

"No," said the clerk. "I have not got it in front of me and I am not going to guess at it in a shop. What I have got in front of me is a withdrawal, dated today, on a ground, with no reflection on workmanship, and I am required to give you the paper and take your acknowledgement and say that the arrears are clear. Are they?"

"Clear," said Elias. "I balanced the book on Friday."

"Arrears clear." She stamped the copy, initialled it, and turned the book round to face him, which she had never had to do. "Mr Venn. Twenty years. I want to say one thing and then I will stop talking, because I am a clerk and this is not my decision. I have closed four accounts this month. Two of them were people who were angry. You are the first one who brought the book in under his arm and asked me to read the paragraph out to you."

"I have a rule," said Elias. "A man who has been told something true gets it read at him, wherever he is standing, and he signs for it in the room."

"That is a better rule than most," said the clerk, and gave him the book back.

Twenty years of fuse wire and elements, gone in one paragraph. The lawful income that paid the rent on the rooms above the shop. He signed the book back, took the letter in his hand, and walked out with the book under his arm into a street that had rain on it, past the ironmonger's where he had bought channel conduit in 2019 and never once asked what it went into. He walked back to Kettle Rows with nothing in his pockets that could buy channel section and nothing that ever had.

Otto looked at the letter once, folded it, and put it in the drawer that no longer stuck.

"Cash," said Otto. "We have done cash before."

"We have," said Elias, and logged the day out full: *charges read 23 Oct 09:00–11:30; censure signed noon; Factors withdrawn 14:00, arrears clear, no reflection on workmanship.* Then he wrote the costs underneath, because a fortnight of this is paid for in sleep and in money and in standing, and a man who writes it down pays less of it in the dark: *Jules charged, cleared in part. Vane and Pryce charged. My file closed, my censure filed, my shop accounts gone.*

He slept that night above the shop he could barely afford to keep, with the censure on the shelf and the cat on his feet, and the ceiling making its wasp noise and then stopping.
