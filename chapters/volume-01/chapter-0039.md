# Chapter 0039 — What a Charge Does

Jules Ardent did not sleep on Monday night. He sat in the scullery at 9 Pell Street with the bench book open, the charge that had not yet been read hanging over him, the clearance that had been measured but not yet spoken sitting beside it. At seven on Tuesday morning he walked to Kettle Rows, knocked on the shop door, and asked Elias Venn to walk with him as far as the Sequence Desk, not inside, just as far as the steps.

"I would rather go in with somebody who told me the truth about which notice," he said.

They walked together through a Tuesday that smelled of rain and baking. Jules talked about the bench room he could not open, the die block in the Registry, the two runs in August and September with a carrier bag on his shoulder for money he needed. He did not excuse himself. He stated it the way a man states a measurement: exactly, without softening, because softening would make it worthless.

The charge room at the Sequence Desk smelled of floor polish and paper on Tuesday morning, and Elias Venn sat in the public chairs at nine o'clock with his hands on his knees because he had no standing to sit anywhere else.

Jules Ardent sat on the bench with Kester Vane and Donal Pryce. The notice was gone from his door — taken down that morning by the practice office, folded, handed to him — and the bench room was still shut, the die block still in the Tool Registry, the uncollected second set still frozen inside the maker's enquiry. He wore his good coat. His hands were clean. He had not slept.

The duty sergeant read the sheets once, as documents, without commentary, because an institutional beat arrives as a document or it is not a beat.

*Kester Vane, 34, Bay 6 Low Cut: charged with assembling unregistered working frames, twelve channels, worklist WL-44/09-07.*

*Donal Pryce, 41, Bay 6 Low Cut: charged with assembling unregistered working frames, twelve channels, worklist WL-44/09-07.*

Donal Pryce cried, once, into his hands, and stopped. Kester Vane stared at the wall. The room wrote it down.

Then the second paper, and the third.

*Jules Ardent, 29, 9 Pell Street: charged with carrying other people's work through The Cut for payment — two runs, 28 August and 11 September, carrier bag, shoulder — contrary to the ward works schedule.*

Silence. Then:

*On measurement: cleared of building the frame. Workshop seats finer than clamp JA-4417 by logged margin. Figures of M. Quill cited, log 1147 and 20 October log. Maker's enquiry to remain open on the die block; practice notice lifted.*

Elias had told him the drawing and the measurement before the clearance was read, as promised, standing with him in the corridor at half past eight with the bench book between them. He stood in the room while it was read, as promised, three paces behind the bench with his hands visible. The clearance was the first thing anybody in this volume had given Jules Ardent, and it came as a measurement, not a kindness.

Jules did not move for a long moment. Then he nodded once, to nobody, the way he had nodded in the fourth chair of a waiting room on the tenth.

Nobody charged Elias Venn with anything. Nobody charged Orin Sable with anything. The supplier invoice stayed pinned to a corkboard in a bay in the Cut. The man in the very good coat stayed out of the room. That was the volume's arithmetic, and the desk wrote it down by not writing it.

Afterwards, in the corridor, Jules stood with his charge sheet in his hand and his clearance in the other, and said the only true thing about either.

The corridor smelled of polish and tea from a trolley. Kester Vane sat on a bench with his sheet in his lap, staring at nothing. Donal Pryce stood with his wife's coat round his shoulders, crying quietly and not hiding it. A constable waited with forms. Life went on being life while paper changed it.

"A charge is rent," said Jules. "Two runs for money I needed. The clearance is the bench. Finer than mine means not mine. I can live with carrying. I could not have lived with building."

Kester Vane came past with his sheet and did not look at anybody. Donal Pryce's wife arrived with a coat and took his arm. The room emptied the way charge rooms empty: quickly, without ceremony, with lives changed inside envelopes.

Elias walked with Jules as far as the steps, no further, because a member of the public does not escort a charged man.

"I will come to the door before any notice," said Elias. "As agreed."

"I know," said Jules. "You have so far."

The departmental censure arrived at noon, by hand, at the shop, while Otto Vahl stood at the front bench with a chassis open and the cat asleep on the blanket.

*Departmental censure, Sequence Desk: E. Venn, formerly D/Sgt, breach of filed prohibition 29 Sep, third examination 22 Oct Bay 6, with written consent M. Quill and witness D/Sgt Rennick. File closed against him. Not a formal reprimand.*

Elias signed for it, because a man signs for what is his. The cover note said the file was closed against him — the retention review, the breach log, the censure in one envelope — and that a formal reprimand, if any, belonged to another process. Arc E's reprimand and clean certificate stayed where they were: unissued, unpromised, untouched.

He put the censure on the shelf above the bench with the notice about the breach, on top of the folio, where he could see it. Niko Senn was named in none of the charge sheets, and Elias checked twice to be sure, because a young man of twenty-four with a withdrawal in a file must not appear in a charge room by accident.

He sat for a while in the back room with the censure in front of him, reading it the way he had read the practice note on the eighth: slowly, without moving, until the words stopped being words and became a fact. Third examination. Written consent. Witness. File closed against him. Not a formal reprimand — that belonged to Arc E, to the crowded clinic, to the clean certificate that would go out over him. This was the trade standing going: the department saying it was better off without him on the file, in writing, with a reference number.

Otto came through with tea in two mugs and set one down without a word. They drank standing up, the way men drink when there is work to go back to. The cat slept on the radio bench. The ceiling ticked.

"You still mend kettles," said Otto at last.

"I still mend kettles," said Elias.

At two he walked to Cutter Street Factors with the monthly statement in his pocket and the trade book under his arm, because a man whose accounts are being withdrawn does it in person.

The Factors occupied a narrow office above an ironmonger's with a counter worn smooth by twenty years of elbows. Elias had stood at that counter since he was twenty-five, repairing radiators for a man who took cash, then for himself. The clerk who served him had changed twice; the book had not. He laid the trade book on the counter with both hands visible, the way he had laid nothing on Draper's counter, and said he had come about the letter.

The clerk was kind, which was worse. She read the letter out before she handed it over, the way competent people do painful things: face to face, without euphemism.

*Venn Repairs trade account, twenty years: withdrawn, connected certified record, account pending. Cash for parts henceforth. No reflection on workmanship.*

Twenty years of fuse wire and elements, gone in one paragraph. The lawful income that paid the rent on the rooms above the shop. He took the letter in his hand — shown leaving, the book under his arm, the street outside with rain on it — and walked back to Kettle Rows with nothing in his pockets that could buy channel section and nothing that ever had.

Otto looked at the letter once, folded it, and put it in the drawer that no longer stuck.

"Cash," said Otto. "We have done cash before."

"We have," said Elias, and logged the day out full: *charges read 23 Oct 09:00–11:30; censure signed noon; Factors withdrawn 14:00.* Then he wrote the costs underneath, because the volume counts who pays in sleep, money and standing: *Jules charged, cleared in part. Vane and Pryce charged. My file closed, my censure filed, my shop accounts gone.*

He slept that night above the shop he could barely afford to keep, with the censure on the shelf and the cat on his feet, and the ceiling making its wasp noise and then stopping.
