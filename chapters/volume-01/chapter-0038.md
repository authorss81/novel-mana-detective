# Chapter 0038 — The First Ledger

Sunday evening the telephone rang twice. First Harrow, to say he had walked to Halloway Street on his own time and got the tenant's word for Monday: Kester Vane would let one page be photographed where it lies and would not open a drawer, would not hand over the book, and would not have his bench cleared. Then Mara, to say the conditions were the laboratory wall's conditions and the figure came first. Elias wrote both down with the times, laid out his coat, and slept badly with the window open.

They went back to Bay 6 on Monday morning with the tenant's word, which had cost a landlord an evening walk to do it. Kester Vane, thirty-four, bench worker, stood in the doorway of his own workshop at ten o'clock with his arms folded while an officer with a camera, a woman with a case, a mechanic with a bench book, a stranger in a wet coat and his own landlord went past him into the two rooms that were his whole living.

Donal Pryce, forty-one, stood at the bench with swarf on his sleeves. He did not speak. Harrow stood by the door with the Yale key. Rennick stood by the bench with the camera.

Mara Quill laid her conditions on the bench out loud, because the wall behind her already said them: her figure first, in her log, under her number, before anything of his. Jules Ardent stood with his bench book ready. Elias Venn stood back with his hands visible and the prohibition sentence in his mouth.

"I am going to say it out loud," he said, to the room, "because it was filed on the twenty-ninth of September and I am about to breach it a third time. The assigned detective will not examine any physical object in this matter."

"The consent is mine," said Mara. "For this object, this bench channel, this afternoon, because my figure is in somebody's drawing and I will not have it used without me present. The breach is yours. The witness is Rennick. The figure comes first."

Then they were there four hours, and the four hours were mostly waiting. Vane worked, because a man who has said that a page may be photographed goes back to his work while it is being decided. Pryce made two teas and took them to the corner and drank his standing up, looking at the floor the whole time, and put the cup in the sink and washed it. Mara sat on the upturned crate by the door with her case between her boots and did not open it, and twice she said the same thing out loud, to nobody, that a number taken in a hurry is a number somebody else owns by Friday. Jules read the fixture from the doorway, then stopped reading it, and stood with his hands in his pockets looking at the walls like a man waiting for a bus he does not want to get. Harrow sat on the bench with the rent book shut on his knee and did not go out, because a landlord who leaves a room he has opened is a landlord who has to open it again.

She held her threshold plate against the bench channel's seat — her facility, plate against a surface, measurement taken — and read the figure twice before she wrote it down, because a number that comes out of an object in a locked bay is a number people will want to argue with for years, and she would rather argue with it in her own log than in a room. She logged it with the time, 14:02, Jules initialling the margin. The pen line was in the seat where the draughtsman had changed pens. No tool in the world puts a pen line into brass.

Rennick wrote the time down. "14:02. Figure logged. Proceed if you are proceeding."

He wrote a second time under it, 14:10, and drew a line under that, because from that second the prohibition filed on the twenty-ninth of September was broken, and the officer was the one writing it down where the officer's own file could not pretend otherwise. Then he looked up and waited.

"Before I do that," said Elias, to the room, "there is a person this is not going to touch. Niko Senn, twenty-four, working nights, one night off in seven. He has a date in September in his own pocket and a card in his coat he has refused off me three times, and I am not reading him in this room and I am not asking him to carry one gram of it. If anybody in this bay has a copy of his name on paper, I would like to hear it now, in front of Rennick, and not later."

For a moment nobody said anything. Harrow said *no* once, to the floor. Vane said, *nothing here.* Pryce said nothing at all, and Elias had not expected him to, and did not write it down as agreement.

Elias put his hand on the bench channel, once, flat, where Mara showed him, with his name already on the breach beside her consent. It was six minutes. The order came without a picture of the room, without furniture, without faces. Order and anchor, as the Margin always gives it, never motive, never identity.

> **MARGIN — The order is the order of a worklist. The seat was cut to a picture.**

Everything in that channel was made after a sentence. The sentence came first, on an afternoon, at a desk, giving a depth. Then a man drew it. Then a seat was cut to the picture. Then a piece was folded into it. Then a stamp was cut. Then the whole of it went out of a door in a carrier bag on somebody's shoulder. There was no man at the end of it, only a worklist number and a price.

He came off it with his hands shaking and sat on the bench stool with his head down. Mara closed her log. Jules put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

"Time," said Rennick, and wrote it: 14:16.

"The order of a worklist," said Elias. "Seat cut to a picture. No man at the end."

Mara nodded. "That changes the decision. We photograph the book. We do not seize it."

Kester Vane stood in the doorway of his own workshop with his arms folded, watching six people decide about his book. He was thirty-four, with oil under his nails and a burn scar on his left wrist, and he had paid his rent on the second of every month for fourteen months. Elias looked at him and saw what Harrow had told them on the steps: a man who kept a clean bench and asked after the landlord's wife, doing work he believed kept wards running.

"Mr Vane," said Elias. "Your book stays on your bench. One page photographed where it lies, with the worklist beside it, camera moved and nothing moved. Your word covers the photograph and nothing else."

Vane nodded once. "Page fourteen. Third of October. Bench hand is mine. Account line is what the scheduler wrote on the order. I copied it."

Donal Pryce said nothing from the bench. His sleeves had swarf on them. His wife would arrive on Tuesday with a coat. Elias thought about what a charge would do to a lease like this one — rent first, then the tools, then the bench — and then stopped thinking about it, because they were still two men in a room with a kettle and a lamp, and the book was still theirs, and nothing that had happened that afternoon had taken it off them.

The daybook sat on the bench where it had sat yesterday, closed, with its spine cracked and its pages thumbed. Harrow watched from the door with his arms folded. Rennick opened it with a folder of his own, went to page fourteen, flattened the page against the one in front of it so the ink stood up out of its own shadow, and photographed it in place with the worklist in frame beside it — camera moved, objects not moved, original staying where it was.

Then, with permission, Elias read the line where it lay, without touching it, and copied it from the photograph afterwards at the bench, with the time, 14:28.

*Page 14, 3 Oct 2029, bench hand: 12 ch. seat per drg, a/c Aegis Mutual, continuity schedule Kettle/Halloway, 216 cr.*

The name sat in the book in a hand, on a date, next to a frame, in front of six people who had to decide what to do about it and two men who worked in the bay and had to live in it afterwards, and nobody in that room said the name out loud, and it has not been said out loud in any room since. It was a customer line, an account line, a payer's name in a workshop book — defensible on its own terms, a risk office pricing off reported faults, a Lantern Compact member insuring what the city asked it to insure. What was new was the sitting-next-to: a frame, a date, a bench hand, twelve channels, and an insurer's account for the continuity of two named wards, all in one line in one man's own handwriting.

Rennick closed the book with the folder, wrote his file note — *Daybook p.14 photographed in place 22 Oct, original stays Bay 6* — and had Harrow countersign. The original stayed on the bench. The photograph went into 441-KR. That was the thread, not the resolution.

Then the cost came, and it came as a place.

Elias stood to go and could not find the stairs. He had come down them twice — Sunday with Mara and Jules, Monday just now behind Harrow — and the route would not come. The passage turned. The vault doors repeated. He stood in Cutwater Passage with his hand on the wall, knowing the way in the way you know a word on the tip of the tongue, and not finding it.

Jules took his arm without a word and walked him up by the handrail, counting the steps aloud as he would count threads: "Twelve. Landing. Eight. Air." Mara walked behind with the case, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that would make a route come back. It took twenty minutes to do a five-minute walk.

At the top, in daylight, Rennick wrote in his own book: *14:10 to 18:40, officer notes member of the public unable reliably to re-find twice-taken route; assisted out by J. Ardent.* Elias reported it himself at half past six in the shop, sitting on the stool with tea he could not taste: the route came back at 18:40 while he was still sitting on the step, all at once — four turns off Marshall Street, past the clinic, the gate, the lamp over the second landing, a walk he has done four times a week for two years and could not have found at two o'clock that afternoon. It came back the way the order had come back in a corridor at ten to ten on the tenth, in the middle of a sentence, with nobody announcing it.

He sat for a while afterwards with Jules on the shop step, neither of them speaking, the street going home around them. Jules's hand was still on his arm from the stairs, and neither of them moved to take it away. The cost sat between them like a third person on a step: logged as a duration, honoured to the minute, four hours and thirty minutes of not knowing the way out of a passage he had walked twice. It was not a headache and it was not blood, and neither of them had been waiting for blood. Somebody else had had to get him out, and somebody else had.

Nadia Kest was not there and had not asked to be. She minuted it in her own book as *reported by the officer*, with the two times Rennick gave her, and refused to put a figure of her own beside it, because she was not going to write four hours and thirty minutes of a man's mind into a book of her own on the word of two other people and call the result an observation.

The breach went to the department that night in Mara's hand, sealed, worse than anything he would have written. The censure would follow. That was the price of the third touch, and it was written down before anybody slept.
