# Chapter 0038 — The First Ledger

Sunday evening the telephone rang twice. First Harrow, to say he had walked to Halloway Street and got the tenant's word for a photograph in place on Monday: Kester Vane said yes to a photograph and no to a seizure, and Donal Pryce said nothing, which Harrow took as yes to the same. Then Mara, to say the conditions were the laboratory wall's conditions and the figure came first. Elias wrote both down with the times, laid out his coat, and slept badly with the window open.

They went back to Bay 6 on Monday morning with the tenant's word, which Felix Harrow had got on Sunday evening by walking to a flat in Halloway Street and asking. The tenant was Kester Vane, thirty-four, bench worker, who said yes to a photograph in place and no to a seizure, and who stood in the doorway of his own workshop on Monday at ten o'clock with his arms folded while an officer, a mage, a mechanic and a member of the public filed past him into the two rooms that were his whole living.

Donal Pryce, forty-one, stood at the bench with swarf on his sleeves. He did not speak. Harrow stood by the door with the Yale key. Rennick stood by the bench with the camera.

Mara Quill laid her conditions on the bench in her voice, not on paper, because the laboratory wall already said them: her figure first, in her log, under her number, before anything of his. Jules Ardent stood with his bench book ready. Elias Venn stood back with his hands visible and the prohibition sentence in his mouth.

"I am going to say it out loud," he said, to the room, "because it was filed on the twenty-ninth of September and I am about to breach it a third time. The assigned detective will not examine any physical object in this matter."

"The consent is mine," said Mara. "For this object, this bench channel, this afternoon, because my figure is in somebody's drawing and I will not have it used without me present. The breach is yours. The witness is Rennick. The figure comes first."

She held her threshold plate against the bench channel's seat — her facility, plate against a surface, measurement taken — and logged the figure with the time, 14:02, Jules initialling the margin. The seat measured what the stockroom carrier measured, to inside four thousandths. The pen line sat in it where the draughtsman had changed pens. No tool in the world puts a pen line into brass.

Rennick wrote the time down. "14:02. Figure logged. Proceed if you are proceeding."

Elias put his hand on the bench channel, once, flat, where Mara showed him, with his name already on the breach beside her consent.

It was six seconds. The order came without a picture of the room, without furniture, without faces. Order and anchor, the way the Margin always gives it, never motive, never identity.

> **MARGIN — The order is the order of a worklist. The seat was cut to a picture.**

Everything in that channel was made after a sentence. The sentence came first, on an afternoon, at a desk, giving a depth. Then a man drew it. Then a seat was cut to the picture. Then a piece was folded into it. Then a stamp was cut. Then the whole of it went out of a door in a carrier bag on somebody's shoulder. There was no man at the end of it, only a worklist number and a price.

He came off it with his hands shaking and sat on the bench stool with his head down. Mara closed her log. Jules put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

"Time," said Rennick, and wrote it: 14:16.

"The order of a worklist," said Elias. "Seat cut to a picture. No man at the end."

Mara nodded. "That changes the decision. We photograph the book. We do not seize it."

Kester Vane stood in the doorway of his own workshop with his arms folded, watching five people decide about his book. He was thirty-four, with oil under his nails and a burn scar on his left wrist, and he had paid his rent on the second of every month for fourteen months. Elias looked at him and saw what Harrow had told them on the steps: a man who kept a clean bench and asked after the landlord's wife, doing work he believed kept wards running.

"Mr Vane," said Elias. "Your book stays on your bench. One page photographed where it lies, with the worklist beside it, camera moved and nothing moved. Your word covers the photograph and nothing else."

Vane nodded once. "Page fourteen. Third of October. Bench hand is mine. Account line is what the scheduler wrote on the order. I copied it."

Donal Pryce said nothing from the bench. His sleeves had swarf on them. His wife would arrive on Tuesday with a coat. The batch would charge them both, and the charge would do to their lives what charges do: rent, tools, bench. But on Monday afternoon they were still two men in a room with a kettle and a lamp, and the book was still theirs.

The daybook sat on the bench where it had sat yesterday, closed, with its spine cracked and its pages thumbed. Kester Vane nodded once. Harrow watched. Rennick opened it with the bone folder Harrow lent him, turned to page fourteen without lifting it, and photographed it in place with the worklist in frame beside it — camera moved, objects not moved, original staying where it was.

Then, with permission, Elias read the line where it lay, without touching it, and copied it from the photograph afterwards at the bench, with the time, 14:28.

*Page 14, 3 Oct 2029, bench hand: 12 ch. seat per drg, a/c Aegis Mutual, continuity schedule Kettle/Halloway, 216 cr.*

The name sat in the book in a hand, on a date, next to a frame, in front of five people who had to decide what to do about it. Nobody said it aloud. It was a customer line, an account line, a payer's name in a workshop book — defensible on its own terms, a risk office pricing off reported faults, a Lantern Compact member insuring what the city asked it to insure. What was new was the sitting-next-to. The batch let the reader do the rest.

Rennick closed the book with the folder, wrote his file note — *Daybook p.14 photographed in place 22 Oct, original stays Bay 6* — and had Harrow countersign. The original stayed on the bench. The photograph went into 441-KR. That was the thread, not the resolution.

Then the cost came, the way costs come in this volume: not as a symptom but as a place.

Elias stood to go and could not find the stairs. He had come down them twice — Sunday with Mara and Jules, Monday just now behind Harrow — and the route would not come. The passage turned. The vault doors repeated. He stood in Cutwater Passage with his hand on the wall, knowing the way in the way you know a word on the tip of the tongue, and not finding it.

Jules took his arm without a word and walked him up by the handrail, counting the steps aloud the way a mechanic counts threads: "Twelve. Landing. Eight. Air." Mara walked behind with the case, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that would make a route come back. It took twenty minutes to do a five-minute walk.

At the top, in daylight, Rennick wrote in his own book: *14:10 to 18:40, officer notes member of the public unable reliably to re-find twice-taken route; assisted out by J. Ardent.* Elias reported it himself at half past six in the shop, sitting on the stool with tea he could not taste: the route came back at 18:40 on the corner of Cutwater Passage, all at once, the way the order had come back in a corridor at ten to ten on the tenth.

He sat for a while afterwards with Jules on the shop step, neither of them speaking, the street going home around them. Jules's hand was still on his arm from the stairs, and neither of them moved to take it away. The cost sat between them the way costs sit in this volume: logged as a duration, honoured to the minute, not the order of a day, not the nosebleed, not the ear. Four hours and thirty minutes of not knowing the way out of a passage he had walked twice. Somebody else had to get him out, and somebody else had.

Nadia Kest, who had come at Mara's asking to witness the reporting, wrote *reported by the officer* under it, because she was not there and would not put an unsourceable phrase in her own book.

Niko Senn was named once in the room, by Elias, before the hand went down: a person he would not read and would not ask to carry anything, with a date still in his pocket and an appointment kept or not by his own decision.

The breach went to the department that night in Mara's hand, sealed, worse than anything he would have written. The censure would follow. That was the price of the third touch, and it was written down before anybody slept.
