# Chapter 0044 — An Old Man Agrees

They stood in the lane outside number three, Tanners Row, from ten to five to ten, in the rain, and Mara Quill said the thing she had come outside to say.

"His consent names the object," she said. "It does not name anything else. Not a working, not a sequence, not a release, not a reason. He has agreed to let three people look at a walking stick and he has not agreed to anything about what is inside it, and if we go in there and treat those as the same permission we will take something off that man that he never agreed to give, and we will take it off him in his own kitchen with the kettle on the table."

"Then we go back and get it again," said Jules Ardent.

"From whom? He is seventy-eight and he has asked three times this week whether he has already had his injection. I am not going to hand a man of seventy-eight a second piece of paper to sign on a Sunday morning because I did not write the first one properly." She pulled her coat up. "So we take what he has actually given us, we do only what the paper says, and if it turns out we need more than the paper says then we stop and we come back on Monday with a different piece of paper."

"And the sentence," said Elias.

"Line two of the sheet." She looked at him. "We say it to him in his own words back to us, and if he cannot say it then we do not go in."

"Whose object, and who has said it is theirs."

"Whose object, and who has said it is theirs." Mara Quill took a breath. "And what we are doing to it we tell him as *we are going to look at the fitting at the bottom of your walking stick, because a man has been at your door, and we are going to tell you what we find, and we are going to put it back before we leave*."

"That is a sentence a man could say in a corridor."

"That is the test," she said, "and it took us eleven minutes in the laboratory and I am not going to discuss it in a lane."

---

Alfred Neve had written it himself on the back of a prescription, in biro, on Saturday morning after they had telephoned, which was the first thing that went right and which nobody had expected.

> *I am Alfred Neve of 3 Tanners Row. Three people may look at my walking stick. They may take it off me. They will put it back.*

*Mr Venn wrote the date on it and I wrote my name next to his*, said Neve, when he read the line over Elias's shoulder, *and he asked whether I wanted the time on it, and I said no, because if it is not a time then it is a permission and if it is a time then it is a record, and I am seventy-eight and I have had four records this year and every one of them was somebody else's.*

He made four cups of tea, and put the stick on the table, and gave them the chair by the window because it was the one with the good light.

Jules Ardent worked for eleven minutes without saying anything, and this is what he did.

He read the stick. Not the ash — the fittings. The collar, the stamp in the brass, the rivet, the ferrule's own geometry, the way the collar is supposed to sit on the end of a shaft this diameter and the way this one sat. He said the number on the collar out loud twice and neither of them interrupted. Then he asked for a cloth, and put the stick in his lap, and put the point of a cold chisel against the rivet head, and tapped it, and it moved, and he put the chisel down and asked Mr Neve whether he was going to be allowed to take the ferrule off.

"You are on a Sunday," said Neve. "You are not a police inspector."

"No," said Jules. "I am a mechanic who has been charged with carrying other people's work and cleared of building one of them, and I have a maker's enquiry open on my die block, and if I take this off and I put it back you will never know it was off. If I have your word I take it off."

"You have my word," said Neve, "and I would rather you did it at the table than in the yard, because I would like to watch."

It came off in about four minutes and it came off whole, and the ash shaft under it was a socket with a small licensed bracket in it, seated and riveted and live, and the bracket was working — Neve put the ferrule back on the slab in the yard again and the stick stood up, and the ash under the ferrule was warm, which Jules said was not surprising and which Neve said was the first thing he had ever been frightened of and had never mentioned to anybody.

Then Jules lifted the sleeve out with his fingers.

It was four inches of channel section. It was a length of ash, split and dressed, that had been shaped into a sleeve and seated over the bracket and bonded to it, so that the two of them made one assembly, and where the sleeve sat against the bracket there was a channel cut into it with a seat in the bottom of the channel about as deep as a thumb-nail is wide.

"That," said Jules Ardent, "is not a component."

"No," said Mara Quill.

"It has no maker's pair on it." He turned it in the light. "I have said this to Mr Venn twice now and I will say it to you: a licensed maker grades his own sets. You cut a run, you cut the pair, first through fourth, the cut is the grade, it is how a maker numbers his own work, and Bramwell puts it on everything he stamps. There is no pair on this because this is not a run. Somebody made this one at a time, for one stick, and did not grade it, because there was nothing to grade it against."

"And there is one more thing," said Jules, "and it is a small thing and it is the one that has been sitting at the back of my head since Wednesday." He held the sleeve up so that the light came through the end of the channel. "On the twenty-first of October, in Bay 6, with Rennick's camera in the room and the worklist on its nail, I counted eleven finished sleeves in a tray on the shelf behind the vice. Eleven. The worklist says twelve and the bench book's line says twelve at two hundred and sixteen credits, and I did not count them again on the Monday because on the Monday Rennick had a camera in that bay and a man was not going to be counting things."

"So one left between the Sunday and the Monday."

"Or on the Monday. Or on the Tuesday. It is in Tanners Row in a pocket and it was not on the twenty-first and I did not say so on the twenty-first because I did not think it mattered, and I have thought about nothing else since Wednesday." He put the sleeve down on the table very gently, next to a cup of tea, in a man's kitchen. "That is on me and I am putting it on the record by saying it out loud to you, Mr Neve, in your own house, with your wife dead nine years."

Elias Venn felt the prohibition arrive in him the way it arrived now, which was as a sentence he had already said twice, and he said it.

"Mr Neve. I am going to say it out loud because I have said it twice in this case and both times somebody wrote it down. The assigned detective will not examine any physical object in this matter. I will not put a hand on that sleeve or that stick or that bracket, and I will not put a hand in this house, and the woman taking the figure is not police and has no licence in this city and is doing it because she has done it eleven thousand times and because she is the only person in the room who can write a number down properly."

Neve looked at him for a while.

"You are all right in that chair," he said, "and I would like you to know I can see it."

---

Mara Quill took the figure at fourteen minutes past eleven, in the kitchen, with Alfred Neve in the room and Jules Ardent as her second witness, and she held her plate against the seat in the sleeve and read it twice before she wrote anything, and then she logged it in her own book, under her own number, with the time on it and with the owner's name in the ownership line and the address.

**Log 1198. 28 Oct 2029, 11:14. Sleeve, 4 in. channel section, split-ash seat, seated on licensed bracket (collar no. read aloud to owner). Seat depth 2.604 mm. Second witness: J. Ardent. Owner present throughout. No contact by D/Sgt Venn. Measured by plate against surface; no reading taken; no other figure in this matter today.**

"Two point six oh four," she said. "That is my number."

"It is a number," said Jules.

"It is a number," said Mara Quill, "that I certified in pencil in nine minutes in a warm room in Concord Hall on the twenty-seventh of September, and a draughtsman wrote on a drawing on the twenty-eighth, and a man in a bay in The Cut cut twelve seats to it, and one of them is in a walking stick in a kitchen in Tanners Row, and I would like everybody in this room to understand that I have never once seen it in a stick and I am not going to be able to un-see it."

Then she closed the book and said the other thing, and this is the part that mattered, and it was the thing she had not got a sentence for on Saturday and got one for anyway.

"Mr Neve. I am going to tell you what the sleeve is, because you have been extremely decent about all of this and I am not going to be decent back."

"Please."

"A bracket like the one in your stick is a working. It holds your step. It does that by binding itself to the step, addressing itself to it, holding it, and being shut off. Those are four things and a man in a kitchen did them on Friday afternoon, and they are yours, and you did not do them, and they are properly done, and the collar on it is stamped with a number a man in a city office can look up in a drawer in about four minutes." She was not looking at Elias Venn and she was not looking at Jules Ardent. "The sleeve in it is a different thing. It was put there by somebody who wanted a working to look complete. It does not hold anything. It carries a release — a fourth act — which was not performed, and which somebody made somewhere else, and brought here, and sat in that channel, and the whole point of the release is that if it ever goes off, the record of the working will show four acts and a man will have done four things, and three of the four are yours, and the three are real, and that is why it can be done at all. A stranger cannot make a man look like he performed something he did not perform. They can only do it to a man who has already performed something, in the same material, in the same place, for the same reason, and the work on your walking stick is real and the sleeve is sitting on top of it like a lid."

Alfred Neve looked at the sleeve on the table next to his tea.

"And the three things that are mine," he said. "Are they still in there?"

"Yes."

"Will they come out?"

"No," said Mara Quill. "Not unless somebody takes the metal away, and I am not authorised to take anything away and there is no officer in this kitchen."

"Then put it back," said Alfred Neve.

So Jules Ardent put the bracket into the socket, and the sleeve over the bracket, and the ferrule over the sleeve, and put the rivet back in with the punch and the drift, and Alfred Neve held the stick in his hand in his own kitchen and put the tip on the concrete slab in the yard and took his hand off the top of it, and the stick stood there on its own in the rain.

---

They put it back at twenty past twelve and Elias Venn had not touched one object in that house.

"Mr Neve," he said, at the door. "Before I go. Do you know why it is a working clinic and a session and a Tuesday?"

"I am afraid you are going to tell me."

"I am not going to tell you, because I have not got it, and I have got a piece of a list and a man coming at a quarter to three on Tuesday. I am going to tell you what I do know, and then I am going to go and work it out, and if I get it wrong you are going to be the first person I ring and I am not going to pretend to you that I will not."

"That is fair," said Neve. "I will be in. I have been in twice before and I have never once been late."

Jules Ardent said it in the lane, at the gate, with his bag over his shoulder, in one sentence, and it was the sentence the whole of the next three days was built out of.

"You cannot build a frame that makes a man look like he did something," he said, "unless the man has already done something, in the same place, in the same material, for the same reason. That is the only handle anybody has ever had on this. Every one of these is a man's own working with a lie sitting on the top of it. That is not a technique anybody invented for a crime. That is a technique somebody learned by reading a form."

Mara Quill agreed with him, which she did not often do about sentences, and then she said the sentence that had been sitting in her since Friday.

"It wants a record," she said. "Not a person. A record. A release that is carried can only be certified against something that is written down at the time, and there is one building in this city where a room full of people are written down at the time, several times a day, by a woman who has done it for nine years, on a list." She put her hand up before Elias spoke. "It is a clinic. It is a session. It is a Tuesday. And it is the third slot, and the third injection of the afternoon is due at fourteen thirty-eight, and there is a man booked into the same room at fourteen forty-five to look at a piece of brass, and I am not going to say his name out loud in a lane in Tanners Row."

"Niko Senn is not the man in slot three," said Elias.

"Niko Senn is the reason that we are going to have to do this on a Tuesday afternoon in a room with forty-one people in it and not in an empty workshop on a Sunday," said Mara Quill. "Because if a person has already been given an order, then the person cannot be used to say which of two orders is true, and we have known that since the fourth of October, and it is now the only fact in this case that everybody in every building is agreed on, including the two people it is worst for."

They walked back up Tanners Row in the rain, past number fourteen, where Mrs Achterberg was not at her window, and at the top of the lane Mara Quill stopped under the lamp and would not go on until she had said the thing she had been holding since the kitchen.

"If it is any comfort to you," she said, "it is not a mystery. There is no man in a room somewhere who knows which of the two orders is the real one, and there is not going to be one, and there is not going to be a machine. I have been eleven years in a laboratory behind a linen office and the best I have ever managed is a plate and a pencil and a man in overalls with a barrow, and the best anybody in this city has ever managed is a form with a printed definition on it and a box, and the box says that where a working has a turn and no release of its own, the record is to be treated as though one was performed. That is the whole of it. That has been the whole of it since the ninth of October, and every one of us has spent a month behaving as though it were a harder question than it is."

"And the hard part," said Elias Venn.

"The hard part is Tuesday," said Mara Quill, "at fourteen thirty-eight, in front of forty-one people, with a piece of metal four inches away from a man's knee, and three other people, and no time, and a sheet of five lines with four of them done."

Jules Ardent stood in the rain at the top of a lane in Tanners Row and looked at the two of them and then at his own hands, which were a mechanic's hands and had been for nineteen years.

"Right," he said. "Nine seconds. Monday, then."
