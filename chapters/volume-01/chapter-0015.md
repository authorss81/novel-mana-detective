# Chapter 0015 — Halloway Street

The transfer order had been presented at the dispensary counter of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic at six o'clock on Monday morning by a courier who had a signature on a pad and did not come past the counter, and Dr Okafor had read it standing up and had given the courier a folded sheet in reply, and the courier had taken it away.

By nine on Tuesday morning it had been processed, and the way it had been processed was this.

Non-attendance on the part of a patient booked to a continuing-treatment schedule is treated as discharge. The account closes the patient's file. The account does not transfer the patient. The account does not send anybody. The account writes *discharge* in a column and a date beside it, and the eleven days of treatment that Niko Senn had never received, the course with a name, the sequence box with four printed lines in it, all of it goes into a closed file at an address on Verge Row that cannot book a patient into the only building he has been able to sleep in since Wednesday.

Niko Senn had been discharged. He was, in the only system that mattered to the people who had been trying to use him, no longer a patient at all.

"Heard," said Niko Senn, from the bed, when Elias told him. "That's good, isn't it?"

"It is good for you and I want to explain why both halves of that."

"Go on."

"You are out of their file. Nobody can transfer you out of a place you are not in. That is not a rumour, it is how the paper works, and it happened at six o'clock yesterday morning while I was in a room on Morrow Hill being told that you do not exist in a document." He put the folder down. "And in about nine days, when a caseworker somewhere asks why a twenty-four-year-old laundry worker has no treatment on his record and no registration at a clinic and no address that resolves, the answer will be a column with a word in it, and the word is discharge, and nobody will be able to find out where you went or who you were."

"So it fixes me and then it loses me."

"It fixes you and then it loses you," said Elias, "and I have been sitting with that for a day and a half and I have decided I am not going to let it happen quietly. Inspector Kest is going to write something into the Kettle Rows file this afternoon that says a named person was under a clinical hold on a named date and that the discharge was recorded by a third party while the hold stood. It will not stop the discharge. It will make the hole in it have a shape."

Niko Senn looked at the window for a while.

"You've been in a room all day," he said.

"Since ten o'clock this morning, yes."

"And you lost."

"The Registry issued its own attestation over my refusal. It took forty minutes. I got one document attached to it." Elias said it without anything in his voice at all, because he had found on Sunday that the flat delivery was the only delivery that did not make him sound like a man in a bar. "The certificate says a working was self-directed and closed inside the dwelling and no third party needs looking at. It is not a lie. Every word of it is true, and it is the most dangerous thing anybody has put in a file about this case, and it went out this morning anyway, because I did not sign it, and my not signing it is the only reason it took forty minutes instead of five."

"So you're further out than you were."

"Considerably," said Elias.

Niko Senn pulled the blanket up. Then he said: "There's another one."

"What?"

"Somebody in the corridor. Yesterday, and again this morning." He was looking at the ceiling now. "A man of about forty telling two lads about the one off Kettle Rows. And he wasn't saying it to me. He was saying it where the corridor does that. And then one of them said, what about the other one, and he said, there's another one, in Halloway, the woman who had the flicker, she's gone."

He stopped.

"I did not ask him," said Niko Senn. "I want that on whatever you write down. I did not ask him a single question about her and I have been sitting here for an hour trying to work out why I thought about her for a second before I thought about the register."

Elias wrote the time down in his own notebook and no words at all beside it, and the second time this week he had made that compromise with himself he got slightly better at it and slightly worse at tolerating it.

---

Kirsten Brannigan telephoned the Kettle Rows station at eight minutes past ten on Tuesday morning, having not slept, and gave her name and her mother's name and her mother's address and said that her mother had been found on the floor of her own cold room and that she was not going to be able to say anything else to anybody today, and then she asked a question that made the officer on the desk put the phone down for a moment.

She asked whether the police would need to come and take the door off its hinges.

Elias got there at half past eleven with Mara Quill, who had come because Elias had rung her and not because anybody had asked her to, and who had brought a plate in a case and a hood on a long handle and had spent the drive doing the thing she did in a car, which was to say out loud what she was going to do and what she was not going to do and who would be present for it.

Halloway Street is four streets back from the market and it is a working street with a market's economics on it: a shuttered betting shop, a place that sells one thing, a lock-up with a roller that has not worked since the spring, and above the lock-up a cold room with a green door and a refrigeration unit on the roof that had been running when nobody was paying for it.

Mavis Brannigan had been fifty-seven and had had the fish end of one pitch for eleven years and had got it from her mother, and she was on the floor between the cold room's chest freezer and the door, and the chest freezer's door was open, and the unit on the roof was running, and the room was four degrees, which killed the possibility of a great many ordinary explanations in about nine seconds.

Her daughter was sitting on an upturned crate in the yard in a coat over pyjamas and would not come into the room.

Kirsten Brannigan was twenty-nine and had a business and had bought a thing in August to make her life easier, and she was going to say it, and she was going to say it without being asked, and that fact turned out to be the most useful thing anybody gave this case all week.

"I bought a unit," she said, in the yard. "For the cold room. In August. I want to say that before anybody asks me anything, because I know how it looks, and my mother would have been—" She stopped and started again somewhere else. "There was a man. I found him through a card the landlord's agent had in the letting file. I did not look him up properly. I want to say that as well. I did not look him up properly and I paid him three hundred and forty credits and I put it on the account, and he came at night, and he did it in about two hours, and he gave me a sheet with a diagram on it."

"Describe the sheet."

"It had a plan of the room and a line on the ceiling and a number of hours and a tick box, and it said which line and which corner." Kirsten Brannigan looked at the green door. "And I asked him what the tick box was for and he said that was the survey for the warranty and I believed him, because I wanted to, and I have been going over that for fourteen hours and I cannot find one single thing I did wrong."

"You did nothing wrong," said Mara Quill.

"That is what I would like written down," said Kirsten Brannigan, "by somebody, on something, so that I have it, because in about a week somebody is going to tell a trade paper that my mother was killed by a fault in a refrigeration unit that I paid to have installed."

"The fault is not in the unit," said Elias. "I want to be careful about how I say that. I have a measurement of the residue in that room and I have not got an answer yet, and I am not going to stand in your mother's cold room and give you a conclusion at half past eleven on a Tuesday morning. What I will tell you is that the light on your stair has been reporting, and that a thing that is reported eleven times and never looked at is not a thing anybody has failed to look at. It is a thing somebody has arranged not to look at."

"Ma's diary," said Kirsten Brannigan.

---

The diary was in a drawer in the office of the lock-up and it was a hardback appointment book from a supplier in a town two hundred miles away, and every page from the second week of July to the Friday before she died had a date on it and a short line in it, in pencil, in a small upright hand.

*Tue 8 — stair light again, went off about half 7 while I was in. Rang it. Man came Wed, said the fuse, did not have a fuse, went off again Thurs as I was locking up.*
*Sat 19 — same. Told them again. Girl on the line said it's on a schedule, don't ring, it goes.*
*Wed 2 Sept — third time this month. Man came, was very nice, gave me his card, said he'd come back next week.*

Fifty-three entries. Nine of them ended with the word *resolved*.

Elias read them in the office, standing up, with the book on the desk and his hands in his pockets, and he read them twice, and on the second pass he put his finger on the ones that said *resolved*.

"Eleven reports in six weeks," he said. "Nine marked resolved on the day they were reported. That's not a fault going unfixed. That's a fault going *answered*."

Kirsten Brannigan sat down on the crate. "He came every time. He came and he was lovely and he told me the equipment was fine and he gave me a card and he said put the card in the window for the next man, because they'd no use for the log."

"That's the sentence," said Mara Quill. "That is the sentence that makes this a network and not a madman, and I want it in my own hand, in my own notebook, before anybody else has a conversation with this woman."

There were two other things on the desk and neither of them was evidence.

The first was a sheaf of cold-room servicing invoices going back four years, on which a line in March read *fuse replacement, stair lighting, no fault found*, in a trade's handwriting, with a plate number in the corner.

The second was a schedule of ward fittings attached to the lease of the lock-up, which is a page of ordinary commercial paper that a landlord and a tenant both sign, and which nobody reads, and which had a **sequence box** on it in the bottom right, with four printed lines in it, where the printed instruction above the box described a three-act standard transfer of fixtures.

Elias asked for it to be put flat on the desk, and then he stood back from it with his hands visible at his sides, the way he had stood back from a counter on Friday morning, and he read.

Four acts. The first three were bind, move, settle. The fourth was the shape of a release.

He counted them twice and he did not lean over it, and he noticed that he had brought nothing to that desk except the ability to count four printed lines, and that in a room in Kettle Rows on Friday he had worked out what happens to a man who lets those two things run together inside his head in front of a registrar.

"Four," he said, out loud. "I can count four and that is the whole of what I have brought to this piece of paper, and on Friday I was in a room with nine people in it and a document that had four acts in a box on it, and I have got one more of them now, and it is a different kind of document in a different building in a different medium, and the box is the same box."

"What does that mean," said Kirsten Brannigan.

"It means that somebody is putting the same four lines on every document that is allowed to carry a sequence," said Elias, "and that this one is a lease, and that your landlord signed it, and that the box was printed on it, and that a form is printed in ten thousand copies and there is no way on earth to find out whose copy is in this room."

Mara Quill did not look at the paper.

"I have two plates and no register," she said. "I have four figures off a threshold in a room in Kettle Rows and two breaks in a piece of brass in an outer ward that I have never seen. I am going to say the sentence that you all need to hear and that nobody in this building should be able to say without me saying it first."

"Mara—"

"No." She was looking at Kirsten Brannigan. "Say it. Because you will say it in a fortnight if I do not, and you will say it in a tribunal, and you will say it with a plate in your hand as though the plate had done something. No instrument in this city found anything unusual in the room over the deceased. That is true. That is true this morning, and it will be true in a year, because I hold no Concord registration and my plates are not on a register, and the only thing standing between that sentence and this case is a piece of paper with my figures on it in my own name."

"And if you get a registration?" said Elias.

"Then it is a different woman with a different standing and I will have earned it and I will not have earned it in a cold room in Halloway Street on a Tuesday," said Mara Quill, "and that is not a modest thing to say and I am not being modest."

She asked Kirsten Brannigan to come into the cold room and stand at the door and not touch anything, and she said out loud what she was about to do at each step, in the order she was about to do it, because she had learned in a corridor on Marshall Street on Monday morning that a thing done correctly and not described is a thing a person can spend a year being told did not happen.

Kirsten Brannigan stood at the green door for twenty-two minutes with her arms crossed and watched a woman in a coat put a sheet of old-looking glass against her mother's cold-room floor and a door frame and a ceiling, and at no point did anyone ask her to move, and at the end of it Mara Quill wrote the date, the time, the address and her own name on a paper sleeve, and turned it round, and asked Kirsten Brannigan to sign it as the person present.

Nobody spoke on the way back to the van. Then Mara said, "I am going to say one useful thing and I am going to say it now while I still believe it. The material I take out of that room will say whether an act was made in it or made somewhere else and carried in. It will not say who. It will not say why. And the reason it will not say why is not modesty and it is not a gap I will close next week. If it is a man in a smock it will be a man in a smock. If it is a man in a grey work jacket with a caliper it will be that, and they are not the same man, and there are four men in this case who are not the same man, and I have watched four days go by in which somebody could have merged them and would have got away with it, and I would like this one to keep four."

On the board in the Sequence Desk office, in Elias Venn's own hand, under Friday's date: *Halloway St — consent given by K. Brannigan, resident, on her own initiative. Lift performed by M. Quill, unaccompanied by police. Two documents read by me from a distance, one of them a lease schedule.*

The nine blank boxes on the board, and then the last one on Monday, and then nothing, and then a woman's name on Monday and then two on Tuesday, which was how he knew it had gone on without him.

The telephone rang at four in the afternoon.

It was Nadia, and she was not in a car.

"Elias, the Registry's information office has issued a draft line for the Halloway Street death. Not published. Drafted, circulated internally at about two o'clock this afternoon, ten hours after the body was found and three hours after Mara took a plate out of the room."

"What does it say?"

"It says *a refrigeration fault at a commercial premises in Halloway Street*. It says it is a draft for internal clearance and it is not for release until after the attending officer's statement." She let a second go by. "They have written the line for the case before they have the statement. That is the same as the sheet of the nineteenth and it is the same as the certificate of Wednesday, and I have put all three dates in a row on a piece of paper and I do not know yet what the row is for."
