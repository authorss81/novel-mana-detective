# Chapter 0007 — The Mechanic's Hands

Rafi Ilori had been on the second floor of Saint Orra Reach since Tuesday night, in a family-observation room, being extremely patient with, and he had run out of being helpful about nine hours before he ran out of talking.

By ten to six on Thursday morning he had given Nadia Kest his route, which was the two hundred metres between the fish end and the bread end of the Kettle Rows night market, walked about ninety times between ten at night and four in the morning, nine years of it; and the man on the top-floor landing of 14 Kettle Rows at about ten past twelve, going up, carrying a canvas roll, whistling.

And then, at ten to six, with his hands around a cup of tea he had not asked for, he started again.

"There's a thing I didn't say Wednesday," he said, "because it sounded stupid, and I thought if I said it twice you'd think I was making it up to help. But the roll, man. The roll is the thing." Nadia put her pen down, which was how she told people to keep going. "Brass comes to the market in rolls. A man who buys shim and brass in a market is a man making something. And I've seen him in the hardware aisle at the Cutter Street depot on a Wednesday morning at about eleven, in daylight, looking at somebody's bench."

"Description."

"Tall-ish. Not tall. Very neat. The smock is the sort of thing a man wears to a job he has not got." Rafi turned the cup round. "And here's the part I couldn't get out from under all night. He asked me if the twelve o'clock went up Tanager Street or down. And I said down, because the twelve goes down. And he said *right*, and he went up Tanager Street."

Nadia took it down, and then added in the margin: *witness correcting own earlier account after sleep — treat as two accounts, one corroborated.*

"Why does that bother you?" she said.

"Because it means he was checking." Rafi looked up. "The bus is the only thing in that building runs on a schedule everybody knows. He wasn't lost, Inspector. He was counting."

Down the corridor a door opened and closed, and in a laboratory four doors away a man was lying face down on a table with a plate in his hands, and Nadia thought about the twelve o'clock going down, and about a man going up.

---

Thursday was the day nothing happened, and Nadia had a rule about days like that, which was that they were not really days like that, they were days on which something was being assembled somewhere out of sight of everybody in the building.

She spent it on paper. She read the whole of Elias's file from the top, including the four days of it that were written in a hand that got worse toward the end of the afternoon and included three different accounts of the same four minutes. She read Mara Quill's numbers, which had come in from Saint Orra at nine-fifteen on Wednesday night under a cover note that said, in full, *First comparison, plate versus swab 14, order consistent, direction consistent, and I will not say more in writing than I can defend under cross-examination. M.Q.*

She read the Registry's draft certificate, which she now had in print, because a draft certificate is a public document the moment it exists, and she read it twice and then a third time with a pen.

It ran to two and a half pages. It was well made. It was, in fact, exemplary: it named the premises, the deceased, the classification of the working, the standard against which the classification had been checked, the absence of any third-party address, and the conclusion, which was that the working was self-directed, single-subject, and closed within the dwelling, and that no further inquiry was required into the conduct of any third party.

Not one word in it was a lie. Every sentence in it would be true, and every sentence in it would be a reason not to look at anything else, and Nadia had read a document like that once before in her career, in a different building, in a different case, and it had been signed by a man who was later made a magistrate.

She was reading it a fourth time when a routing slip came in with the tray, and the routing slip had a name in the top corner that she had spent nine years not thinking about, and which read: *A. Orr, D/Chief — for attention re: cert. schedule, Morrow Hill.*

The slip did not ask her to do anything. It said that Deputy Chief Orr would be grateful for a summary of the sequence position in the Morrow Street matter by close of Friday. It was signed by an assistant who had been an assistant for eleven years and who put the trays down gently.

Nadia read it twice, put it in the tray, and then took it out of the tray and put it in her own file, and did not tell Elias, and was going to tell him on Friday when it was no longer useful to keep.

---

She found Jules Ardent at four in the afternoon in a transit maintenance shed at the edge of the Orra interchange, on the evening shift, changing the clamp rail on an escalator in a way that he clearly did several times a week and which he was doing, she noted, with a chalk line and a small spirit level and a great deal of swearing.

He was twenty-nine and he had cut hands and he did not stop working when she came in.

"You're the other one," he said, to the escalator.

"Detective Inspector Kest."

"You're the one who signed the paper." He set the rail down. "It's in the bench book. You wrote 'transferred not seized' and I thought that was the most decent thing anybody had written down about me in about four years, and then you sent it out to Verge Row on a courier and I nearly didn't sign my own bench the next morning."

"You could have refused to hand it over."

"I could have. I did think about it at four in the morning on Wednesday with a torch in my hand, and I did it anyway, and I drove it out myself at half eleven at night because the courier had the wrong idea about which street." He wiped his hands. "It's on the bench at the address on the card. It is on the bench and the card is on the bench, and the man whose name is on the card is not at the address, and there is a woman there who is not a nurse who told me to leave it and not come back and asked me whether I was Concord, and I said no, and she said that was a problem for her and not for me."

Nadia wrote *returned to issuing account by witness, 23:30 Wed* on her pad and did not say anything about it for a moment, because a man who had driven a piece of evidence back to the people who had stolen it in order to keep a promise was not a man who was going to be easy for her to handle, and she had spent nineteen years learning that the hard ones are the honest ones.

"Jules Ardent," she said. "I am going to ask you where Niko Senn is."

He picked up the spirit level.

"I don't know a Niko Senn."

"You lent a man a brass clamp on the seventeenth, and a night-bus porter saw somebody go up Tanager Street at ten past twelve on the Monday with a canvas roll, and a witness in a hospital this morning has given me a name and a description, and I have got a detective sergeant who can see the order of a working and who has put his hand on this case, and I am going to have to put him off it. Do you know how that works?"

"No."

"It works badly." She closed the pad. "It works badly for everybody, and it works worst for the people in the room it is being done to. But that is going to happen in about six weeks unless something changes, and the something that changes it is usually a man in a workshop who was there at the wrong time. So I am asking you now, while it is still a question."

He put the level down properly, which was a mechanic's version of sitting down.

"If I tell you," he said, "you'll take me to Concord."

"Yes."

"And they'll put a notice on the front of it. That's what they do. There's a form. *Unregistered practice — premises closed under clinical practice order.* And the notice goes on the door, and everybody on that street sees it, and the people who were coming there for sleeping tablets stop coming, and the ones who are still coming get frightened, and the whole thing is finished in about six weeks and it is finished for them and not for the people doing the fixing."

"Who is the clinic, Jules?"

"That's not the question."

"That is exactly the question, and I am the only person in this city who is going to ask it in a way that does not answer itself."

"No." He picked up the chalk. "You go and ask the Concord. You will get a name and an address and a file number and you will be very happy, and the address will be on Verge Row, and the Verge Row address is where the man who is not a nurse told me to leave a brass clamp. So the notice would go on a building where there is nothing. That is what they want. That is what it is *for*."

Nadia stopped writing.

"Who wants it?"

"I don't know who wants anything. I know a piece of paper wants it." He said it without any bitterness at all. "Ask me the real question."

"The real question," said Nadia, "is where Niko Senn is, and you know, and you have moved him, and you did it because you worked out, correctly, that an account with a card can book a person into a room and can serve a person's body out of it, and that there is exactly one address in this city that such an account cannot reach."

Jules Ardent put the chalk down and wiped his hands for a long time, on a rag, that did not need it.

"The Kettle Rows Community Clinic," he said. "Niko Senn has been at Dr Okafor's since Wednesday afternoon. She's known about this since Saturday. I went to her and I did not ask, I told her, because I knew what she'd do with a man who asked her to take a frightened boy on no paperwork."

"Sana Okafor?"

"Dr Okafor." He nodded. "And here is the thing you need to write down, because you will want it and you will want to be right about it: the account cannot book into that address. Not a patient, not a treatment, not a bed. It cannot write a name on that clinic's door because that clinic is not on the account's list of places it is allowed to reach, and that is not a law, that is a line on a form in a filing cabinet in an office on Verge Row, and I have read that form. I read it in a waiting room."

"And can the account reach a *transfer* out of it?"

"It reached the records in about ninety minutes," Jules said. "Which is the thing none of you have understood. Nobody has to walk into that building. They just merge the patient's file with the account's file, and the account's file has its own address on it, and then the document says the patient was under the care of the account at an address the account controls. And the clinic's name is on it as a *referral*, and the referral makes it legal on its face, and nobody at that address ever has to agree to anything." He looked at her. "They did it to somebody else first. There is a man on that account's books who has been coming to Verge Row for a year and who does not know he has been on a treatment course."

Nadia put that down in her own hand and read it back to him, and made him confirm it, and dated it, and initialled it, and then did the thing she had come to do.

"Stand up," she said.

---

They did it in the back of Elias's own shop, at the back table, under the day-bulb, with the shutter down and the extractor fan going, because the extractor fan meant nobody on the street could hear and because Nadia had decided, on the way, that if she was going to do this then she was going to do it in the room where the conflict of interest lived.

Elias came out from behind the bench with a dead night-bus depot light in his hand that he had been repairing for three days for a man who had not collected it, and Nadia watched his face when he saw who was sitting at his table, and she watched him take about a second and a half to decide not to make it a joke.

"Sit down, both of you," she said. "I am going to do three things and then I am going to be at the hospital until nine."

She did the first thing. She had a canvas roll on the bench, and she unrolled it, and there were ten tools in it in a piece of oilcloth: a scribe, a pair of nippers, three drivers, a stone, a small file, a pair of tongs, an old hacksaw, and a fifth clamp — JA-4416, from the run.

"These came out of a lock-up on Chalk Street that was entered on the eighteenth of September with a warrant obtained by a detective who has since retired to Cornwall," she said. "He carried other people's tools for six years. He kept a list. He has brought me the list."

Jules Ardent put both hands flat on the bench.

"I did the repairs," he said. "I did the repairs on the ninth of September, and on the eleventh, and on the fifteenth, on a false clinic account, at a flat above a shuttered laundrette in Chalk Street, and I did not ask what the workings were for, and I told myself that a repair is a repair and a ward doesn't care who owns the room it is in. That is the sentence. That is exactly the sentence I used on myself for six years. Record it."

Nadia recorded it, word for word.

"The second thing," she said. "Elias. Jules. This is not a reconciliation. I am not standing here telling you two men to be friends. I am standing here telling you two men that you are both in this room, and one of you has been living upstairs from the crime scene for four days and has admitted it in a file, and the other one has just admitted to six years of carrying work he did not understand, and both of those admissions were made for the same reason, which is that a file is a place where somebody has to write something down and it turns out that the people who can be trusted to write it down are the two people who were in the room."

"That is a speech," Elias said.

"It is a booking summary," said Nadia, "and I am going to finish it, because if I stop now it is a speech."

She took the pad off her knee.

"Detective Sergeant Venn stays on this investigation. That is decided and I have decided it, and it is on the routing slip you will both see on Monday, along with the word *retained* and a signature. He stays on it on one condition, and I am making the condition in this room, in front of a civilian who has just confessed to a documentable offence, because there is nothing on earth that stops a man arguing with me and everything on earth that stops him arguing with you."

She looked at Elias.

"You do not work alone again. Not once. Not for an hour. Not to check a bench, not to walk a landing, not to follow a repair number, and not to go into a room that anybody else is going into. If you go somewhere, you say where on the board in the office, and if the board is empty you wait. Do you want to argue with that now or in front of him?"

"No," said Elias.

"Say it properly."

"I don't argue with it," Elias said, "in front of him."

Nadia turned to Jules.

"Mr Ardent, I am now going to do the ordinary police thing, and I want to warn you that it is going to feel like nothing at all and it is not. I am not going to charge you with anything tonight, because you have not been arrested and you have not been interviewed under caution, and I am not going to put you in a car while there is a frightened man in a clinic who has been moved by you. Instead I am going to take a full voluntary statement from you about the six years, tonight, at the hospital, with a solicitor of your choosing and a copy, and I am going to give that statement to the Crown in the morning, in writing, with the repairs, the dates, and the account names on it. Which means that in about nine days you will not be a man who might be charged. You will be a man who has already given everything, and when somebody eventually does bring this up, you will be a witness, and your name will be on a document that cannot be taken away from you by anybody with a stamp."

Jules Ardent looked at the bench for a while.

"You could have taken me tonight," he said.

"I could have taken you Monday, before any of this, and I would have got a conviction out of you by Wednesday, because you would have been frightened and you would have said whatever you were asked. And in a year there would be a man in a cell who felt like he had done something terrible, and a clinic on Verge Row with no door on it, and no more of either." Nadia closed the pad. "I am not in the business of collecting that."

Elias Venn had not said anything for some time. Then he got up, went round the bench, and put a cup of cold coffee in front of Jules Ardent, and it was a stupid thing to do and he did it anyway.

"I used you," he said.

Jules looked at the cup.

"On Tuesday I walked into your shop with a number off a witness's statement and I worked you like a ledger," Elias said. "I came out with a clean route and a feeling in my chest like a man who has found something, and what I had actually found was a person who had done a bad thing for eighty credits and who was going to lose his certificate for it. I should have come and asked you first. I came and took."

Jules Ardent picked the coffee up, looked at it, and put it down again.

"It's cold," he said.

"Yes," said Elias. "It is."

---

They were still standing there, not happily, when Nadia's phone went, and she read two lines of it and then handed the phone to Elias because it was addressed to him.

It was a notification from the Proof Registry's document system, which notified anybody in the address book of a new filing, and the title line said:

*ACCOUNT 44-9 (REHABILITATION) — TRANSFER OF PLACE OF RESIDENCE UNDER CONTINUING TREATMENT, PATIENT N. SENN, S/12, TO ACCOINT PREMISES. SIGNATURE REQUIRED. ISSUE 06:00 FRI.*

"Tomorrow morning," said Nadia.

"And they merged the files on Wednesday afternoon," Jules said. "I watched them do it. A woman in a white coat and a man with a lanyard. They put a form into a scanner and a line came out with a clinic on it and a street on it, and the street was the clinic, and neither of them looked at the street."

"Which clinic?" Elias said.

"They printed a referral line," Jules said. "Not for Verge Row. For Kettle Rows. The Kettle Rows Community Clinic, Dr Okafor, and the line said *continuing care on discharge from the account's premises*, and the date on the discharge was the twelfth of the month, and the twelfth of the month is the date the account says Niko Senn completed a course of treatment he has never had."

Jules Ardent picked up the cold coffee again and drank it, grimacing, because it was the only thing on the table.

"Then they've written your clinic into their account without asking you," Nadia said. "Which means the order is legal on its face. Which means in about nine hours somebody is going to arrive with a piece of paper and take a man out of a building he is safe in, and the piece of paper will say he is being moved for his own good."

"Which means tonight," said Elias, "somebody has to tell Dr Okafor, and somebody has to be standing in her corridor at six in the morning."

"Yes," said Nadia. "And I cannot, because I am going to be in a room taking a voluntary statement from a mechanic until eleven, and if I leave it the Crown can use the delay."

"I will go," said Jules Ardent.

Elias and Nadia looked at each other across a bench with a dead bus light on it.

"No," said Nadia. "Mr Ardent, you are not walking into a police matter in a district where one person has died and you have a warrant-free confession on file and no solicitor. You will be at Saint Orra at eight with a solicitor of your choosing. You will not go near Kettle Rows until this is resolved, and if you go near Kettle Rows I will find out, and I will not enjoy finding out."

"Yes, Inspector."

"Then go home." She held the door for him, and at the last second she said, "Mr Ardent. The repairs. The ones on the ninth, the eleventh, and the twenty-fourth. Were any of them for a frame?"

Jules Ardent stopped in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

"Not a frame," he said. "A pattern. Somebody brought me a pattern in a sealed envelope and paid me to cut a first set from it, and it was a design for a device that holds a sequence in a channel, and it came with a stamp device and a drawing of the stamp, and the stamp was a pair — two marks, one open square over one filled square, cut as a pair so a maker can tell a first cut from a third. And the drawing said *deprecated practice, retained for existing frames only*." He shifted the roll under his arm. "I cut two sets. The second one I never picked up. And the first one is the clamp you sent to a laboratory in a bag, and I did not know then and I know now that there were more, because you told me what was in it, and it was not what I made."

"Then who did the second set?"

"I don't know." He said it flatly. "It was going to be collected. It was never collected. It has been sitting in my bench room since the seventeenth, which is why I know it is not the second one in the street."

Elias Venn stood in his own shop and understood that the piece of evidence that had just left his hands in a courier's van on Wednesday night had not gone to a hiding place.

It had gone home, to a workshop, to a bench, and was sitting there waiting to be picked up by whoever had sent the sealed envelope.
