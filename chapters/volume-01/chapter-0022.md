# Chapter 0022 — Witness Services

The forms counter at Morrow Hill sells three things to anybody who asks, and on Tuesday morning the second of them was on a low table by the radiator with a wire rack on it and a card in a card holder above it, and the card said **Publications — 3 credits. No receipt required under 40.**

Elias Venn had been in the building twice in six years and both times had gone upstairs. He had bought the concordance of sequence-box codes on the Saturday before last, for four credits, in a plastic sleeve, and had read it on a bench by a radiator. He was now standing in the same corridor at ten past nine in the morning with a leaflet in his inside pocket and three credits in his hand, and the person who had sold him the concordance was on the till.

"The blue ones," she said. "Take the blue ones, they're the current printing, and if anybody's told you there's an older one with something different in it, they're confusing the practice notes, and the practice notes are not public and if you ask for them I'll have to ask you to leave."

"I want the witness training one."

That stopped her for about a second and a half, which is a long time at a counter.

"That one's in the blue rack," she said. "Part One, Part Two, Part Three. Part Three's the one people come in for." She reached down and put a book on the counter, and it was about the size of a telephone directory and about four times as thick, and it had a Concord crest on the front and a spine that said **WITNESS TRAINING COMPENDIUM, PART THREE: SEQUENCE SUPPORT AND RECALL** in gold, and a printed slip in the front saying *Fourth printing, revised 2026.*

"Third credit," she said. "And the modules inside it are numbered and the page numbers run per module, so if somebody quotes you a page, ask them which module, because half the mistakes I see in this building come from a page number without one."

Elias Venn carried it to a bench by the radiator, because that is where he had read the last one and because his left side was gone and the noise in that corridor was not going to help him, and he sat down and opened it at the contents page and found that there were eleven modules and that Module 4 was called *Grounding and Anchor* and was nineteen pages long.

He read it twice. It took him fifty minutes.

The Concord trains witnesses. That was on page three, in the ordinary way a piece of public information is stated, and it said that the Civic Concord held that a working could not be made legible in a court without an account from a person, and that the Authority therefore trained persons to give accounts, and that it published its training so that any person might see what it taught and so that a person might be taught by somebody the Authority had examined.

*No training in this Compendium may be delivered to a person who has not been informed, in advance and in writing, of the purpose for which the order of an account is being established.*

Elias Venn read that sentence four times and then put his thumb under it and read it a fifth time with his thumb there, and what he felt about it was not satisfaction and it was not horror. It was the specific feeling of a man who has just been handed a thing he wanted by an institution he does not trust, and who has noticed, in the four seconds it took him to notice, that wanting it is the reason he cannot look at it properly.

Page eleven set out the exercise. It was called *the anchor exercise* and the manual was extremely careful about the word it did not use, and the reason it was careful was that the manual had been revised four times.

*The practitioner places a physical object with which the person has had recent contact on the table between them, and asks the person to hold it. The practitioner then names a sequence — an event, a movement, a sound — and asks the person to place it in order. The practitioner does not supply the order. Where the person cannot supply an order, the practitioner records that the person cannot supply an order, and the record stands. **A supplied order is not an account and may not be recorded as one.***

That sentence was in a box, and under the box, in the same printing, was a second box.

*Where a person has been exposed to a working, the anchor exercise may be conducted with the exposure as the anchor. The exposure is then the thing the person holds, and the order asked for is the order the person perceived. Care must be taken to distinguish the order perceived from the order expected, and the manual does not describe a method of distinguishing them, because no such method is established.*

Elias Venn sat on a bench in a corridor on the ground floor of Concord Hall for about four minutes and did not turn a page, and what he was aware of was that the reading had taken him somewhere he had not intended to go, and that he was going to have to sit with it, and that nobody in this building was going to help him do that because the sentence about no established method was not a warning. It was a note about a research position. It was the ninth page of a book that anybody could buy for three credits.

He found, on a bench by a radiator, in the third printing of a public curriculum, four lines that were printed on a sheet of paper in a young man's aunt's front room in the outer wards.

*Hold the order you actually perceived. If you cannot hold it, say so. An uncertain account recorded as uncertain is worth more than a complete one you cannot stand behind. Do not be helped into an order you did not see.*

Do not be helped into an order you did not see.

He wrote the whole box out in the small bad hand in the back of his own notebook with the module and the page and the printing against it, and he wrote the date on it, and he put the book in the wire rack on the table by the radiator because it was public property and he had paid for it and it belonged on that table, and then he went and found out where the office was.

---

Witness Services is at the back of the ground floor, past the certificate office, down a corridor with a window that looks at a wall about four feet away, and there is no sign on the door at eye level. There is a sign, higher up, and it says **WITNESS SERVICES (SEQUENCE SUPPORT) — INFORMATION AND APPOINTMENTS**, and beneath it, in a smaller type that has clearly been there a long time and has never been updated:

*Holding an account of an event is not a matter of effort. If you cannot hold the order, say so, and the record of that is worth more than a guess.*

Elias Venn stood in that corridor and looked at a sign that said almost word for word what the leaflet said, and understood that the leaflet was not written for a person at all. It was written for a counter. It was the counter's own discipline, printed small, on a sheet of hospital paper, and left in a launderette-adjacent front room by a man with a clipboard who was not lying and was not even improvising.

There was a counter with a woman behind it who was about thirty-five and had a clipboard and a good manner, and beside the counter a wire rack with about forty leaflets in it, and on the wall behind her a board with a list of practitioner names and numbers on it under a heading that read **MODULE 4 CERTIFIED — GROUNDING AND ANCHOR**, and the list ran to about thirty names and it was, as far as he could see from four feet away, the moral equivalent of a driving test pass list.

"Can I help you?" she said.

"I'm not here about myself. I'm a police officer and I've got a file, and I'd like to know what your intake is, because I've got a name in it and he's twenty-four and he's on a board on Thursday and I'd like to know what you hold."

That was a lot more than he had planned to say in a corridor, and he heard himself say it, and she reached for a form and then stopped.

"Who's the file," she said.

"Niko Senn."

And it was the woman's face that did it, not anything she said. She did not become cagey, which he had been ready for, and she did not become helpful in a way that cost her, which he had been more ready for. Her face went to the face of a person who has been handed a piece of paper that is a job.

"Give me a date of birth and I'll tell you what I can tell you," she said.

"Nineteenth of July, 2005."

She went away down the corridor. She was gone about four minutes. He stood at the counter and did not read the practitioner board, and he could have read the practitioner board from four feet away, and he did not, and he was going to be asked about that later by himself.

She came back with a folder and did not open it on the counter.

"Eleventh of September," she said. "He came in on his own on the eleventh. He came back on the twenty-fourth and the twenty-ninth, both booked, both two hours, and he's booked again for Thursday morning at half past eleven, which I have had to move because of a board in your district, and I don't know why a board moves my appointments but it does."

"How was he referred?"

"Card." She said it in a particular way, and it was the same way Wen Senn had said *neat*. "He had a card in his hand. He had it in his hand before he came in and he showed it to me at the counter and he said, *a man gave me this.* And I wrote the number down and that's in the folder, and the number is the referral and I am not going to read you the number because you haven't got a warrant and a witness-support file is not a police record."

"Ma'am—"

"It's a witness-support file." She said it patiently and it was the exact sentence Mara Quill had used in a different building ten days ago about a plate. "If you want anything out of it you make an application through a certifying office, and if the account is open in a case then you get it through the case, and I would honestly rather you got it through the case because I have been doing this job for four years and I have watched two offices go into those files and neither of them left anything in them that helped anybody."

"Is the account open in a case?"

"It's open on an address." She closed the folder, which she had not opened. "I am not going to say the address either, because you have not said it, and if I said it and it went in a police file I would be the woman who gave it to you and I have seen what happens to that woman. It happens to her about six months later and it happens through somebody else's letter."

"I'll say the address."

"Then say it after you've thought about Thursday, because you will be able to say it on Thursday and you will not be able to take it back, and if you tell me the address now I will have to tell you whether the account is open on it, and then you will have it, and then you will use it, and then the next young man who comes in here with a card in his hand is going to find out what happened to the one before him."

Elias Venn stood in a corridor four feet from a wall and understood that a woman behind a counter had just told him, correctly, and without any ill will whatever, that the most useful thing she could give him was a refusal.

"That's fair," he said. "Then tell me one thing that isn't in a file."

"What?"

"The referral. What happens to a card number when a person comes to you with one."

She thought about it for a moment, and it was a real question and not a tactic, because he had phrased it as one.

"It gets opened," she said. "If a card refers somebody to sequence support, then a card has been used to buy sequence support, and there is a file. Not his file. A file on the referrer, and it has got a name on it and a registration number if he's got one, and the file says he holds a certificate for Module 4 and it says he has referred this many people." She stopped. "That's the bit people don't like. Because it's true that we've trained about nine hundred people in this city and there's no list of who they are, and it's also true that we know, on any given day, who has sent us somebody this month, because a referral has to be countable, or the service doesn't work."

"Why countable?"

"Because if it isn't countable, nobody can be told." She put the folder away under the counter. "And that's the whole job, sir. That's the entire job. Telling people who sent them here."

Elias Venn stood in that corridor for another minute, and what he thought about, and could not have said aloud if he had been asked, was that there was a card in the pocket of a man of twenty-four in the outer wards with a number on it, and a man in a smock who had taken forty credits four times for a hand flat on a table, and a file in a cabinet behind this counter with a name at the top of it and a number beside it, and that if the number on the card and the number in the file were the same number then a great deal of what had happened in a room in Kettle Rows had a *source* in it, in a way that had nothing to do with a plate or a seal or a Ward Works vest.

He did not have the number. He had refused it on Sunday, in a corridor, on Marshall Street, at about half past four in the afternoon, and he had refused it for a reason that had been correct, and he had said on the page in his own words that he was not going to be the reason a man of twenty-four became a man who gave a card to the police.

He said none of that to the woman at the counter, because none of it was any of her business, and he thanked her, and he asked her one last thing, which was whether the Thursday half-past-eleven appointment would still be in her book if the person did not turn up.

"It stays in the book," she said. "It always stays in the book."

---

He got as far as the end of the corridor and then he stopped, because on the wall opposite the Witness Services door, at the height of a man's shoulder, there was a notice, and he had walked past it four times that morning without reading it, and he read it now because it had a seal on it that he recognised.

**PROOF REGISTRY — WITNESS SERVICES (SEQUENCE SUPPORT)**
**Practice note W-7. Issued annually. Current printing on the rack.**

*Residents may be advised that a sequence-support session is not a hearing, is free, and is not an admission of anything. A person who has attended may withdraw the account at any time by completing Form W-1. **A withdrawn account is superseded and remains on file.** Withdrawal is recorded in the ordinary way and does not by itself impugn either account.*

Elias Venn read the last sentence of that paragraph three times, on his feet, in a corridor, with a book under his arm that had cost him three credits.

*Does not by itself impugn either account.*

It was careful. It was correct. It was, as far as he could tell, the work of somebody who had thought very hard about not being unfair to a frightened person, and it had one property that he turned over four times on the walk back to the car and could not find a way round, which was that it drew no line at all. It said only that the record of the withdrawal would be kept and that nobody would be blamed. It said nothing whatsoever about which account was the account, and the machine did not need it to. A file with a new account in it and an old one marked superseded is a file that answers to the new one, because the new one is the one a certificate is made from, and the old one is a thing that happened to somebody.

He wrote the paragraph out in the back of the notebook with the practice note number against it, and then he wrote underneath it, in the small bad hand, three things he was not going to be able to unwrite:

*Practice note W-7. Withdrawal does not impugn either account.*
*A card refers to a service. A referral is countable. Somebody is told who sent a person here.*
*Nobody in the Concord is hiding this. That is what the twelve pages and the leaflet and the free rack and the four pounds at the counter are for.*

Then he went out past the certificate office and up the stairs and out into the daylight on Morrow Hill at a quarter to eleven in the morning, and stood on the step, and took the leaflet out of his coat and read the box on it one more time.

*Do not be helped into an order you did not see.*

Somebody had written that, and it was right, and it was printed in a public building on a public document, and it had been handed to a man in a launderette on a Sunday night by a woman with a clipboard who had not lied to him, and by Tuesday morning it was in four places in a man's life and he had a form and a booked hour for Thursday at half past eleven which the counter would not let go of whether he came or not.
