# Chapter 0024 — The Board

Wen Senn had never been in a room with a radiator in it that worked, and she had brought a shopping bag with a hat in it, and she had put the bag on the floor and then picked it up again and then put it down, and she told Elias Venn about the hat at some length on the way in.

"It's for the back of my head," she said. "I don't know what it's for. I get through a door and there's a draught."

---

The building was a ward authority office with a public hall on the ground floor, and the room was up a stairs with a fan heater in it that had a smell of dust coming out of it, and there were eight chairs in a row against the far wall and four of them had people in them at a quarter to ten in the morning on Thursday the fourth of October.

Elias counted the room twice, because he had been counting rooms for a fortnight and could not stop. Eight chairs, four people in them, and a table at the front with three chairs behind it and a man in a grey suit at the end one who had a folder and a glass of water he had not drunk.

By ten past nine it was seven in the gallery and one at the table, and by ten to ten Jules Ardent had made it eight, and came in with his coat over his arm, and sat at the end of the row with his knees at ninety degrees and read the fire notice on the wall behind him, which was a thing that took him about forty seconds and told him nothing, and which Elias Venn understood perfectly and did not comment on.

Nadia Kest was already there. She was in the second seat from the back, in her own coat, with a folder she did not open.

"You're not on this," she said, when he sat down beside her.

"No."

"You're the assigned detective on 441-KR and you're not on this, and I have been in eleven of these as an inspector and I have never once been on one, and I did not know that was true until this morning, and I would like you to sit here and notice it properly, because I think it is going to matter in about a year."

"You want to know why the case is there at all."

"I want you to notice that there is a building eleven minutes from your front door in which your word cannot be entered, and that it has been true since the day a woman died two floors above you, and that you found out about it today in a room with a heater in it." She put her hands in her lap. "That's all. Notice it."

---

Sana Okafor came in at four minutes to ten with a folder under her arm and a list on a loose sheet and no coat, because she had come up from the clinic and had walked.

She sat on the left of the three chairs, squared the folder, and did not look at the gallery, and Elias understood that it was going to cost her something not to look at the gallery and that she had made the decision on the stairs.

The chair of the board was a woman of about sixty called Ada Lund who had come out of the clinical service herself and had the manner of somebody who had chaired four hundred of these and had stopped performing any part of it. On her left was a man of about fifty with a tie and a folder of his own. On her right was a young woman with a minute book and a pen, who wrote down the time the chair came in, which was 10:02.

"Mr Crowther," said the chair. "You are the registered agent for the account."

Martin Crowther said yes he was, and he said it as a man who had a difficulty and had decided to have it in front of the board rather than behind it.

"I'm here because the account is of the view that the hold should come off," he said. "I'm not here to say the hold is wrong. I'm here to say the account has a person it cannot treat and cannot discharge, and that is a position we've been put in and not one we chose, and I'd like the board to understand that when it makes its decision we're going to comply with it inside a day."

"Understood," said Ada Lund. "The board will make its decision and the account will comply. Doctor Okafor."

"The hold was filed on the twenty-first of September," said Sana Okafor, "on the ground that a transfer under a clinical hold is not a transfer, and it has been upheld twice already and it should be upheld again, and I have nothing to add to that which is not on the file, and the file has ninety-odd pages in it that this board did not have last time and had four hours' notice of."

"I've read them," said the chair.

"Everybody has read them," said Sana Okafor. "That was rather the point of putting them up."

---

The first two items took nineteen minutes and were about a treatment schedule and a dispensing record and were dealt with correctly and at speed, and then the chair came to the third item and the room changed.

"The matter of the person named here as N. Senn," said Ada Lund. "There is a clinical hold filed on the twenty-first, a discharge entered by the registered agent on the twenty-fourth, and a person before the board. Doctor Okafor, is the hold still live in your view?"

"It is."

"And is there any clinical basis for the account's discharge?"

"There is a clinical basis for a discharge in the ordinary sense. He is not ill. He is twenty-four, he is not eating, he is not sleeping, he has nobody's address on any record after the twenty-fourth, and if the transfer does not go ahead then he is a person with no treatment and no housing and no file, which is not a good outcome for him either." Sana Okafor put two fingers on the folder and left them there. "The board is not asked to decide that. The board is asked whether a hold is proper. In my view it is, and if it's lifted then I will write that I said so."

"Noted." Ada Lund looked at the young woman, who wrote it. "Mr Crowther."

"We'd like it lifted," said Crowther, "and I'll be honest about why, and the honest reason is not about him. He did not attend on the twenty-third. He was discharged on the twenty-fourth for non-attendance under the schedule, which is a term of the account's contract with the Authority and not a decision anybody took about a person. And the discharge is the problem, not the answer. The account has closed a file it is not permitted to close, and it has a person on a transfer it is not permitted to receive, and those two things cannot both stand in front of a continuity office, and the account is required to report on both of them in the same quarter."

"Ten days," said Crowther. "Ten days of a discharge the account cannot implement and a transfer the account cannot take, and every week it goes on the account reports a person it has not got. I would rather the board lifted a hold that is not mine to lift than have the account carry an entry that is not the account's either."

Somebody in the gallery made a sound. Elias Venn did not find out afterwards who it was.

"Three," said the chair. "The board records that rehabilitation account 44-9 entered a discharge against the above on the twenty-fourth of September, that a clinical hold was filed on the twenty-first and had not been discharged, and that the person concerned has attended no reviewable appointment since the twenty-first. The hold is **upheld**. It remains in force until an examination by a second clinician has been carried out and recorded, and the account is not to receive the person into its care before that examination has taken place. A copy of this minute is to be supplied to the registered agent of the account at its address on Verge Row, and to the clinical respondent, and to the person."

"Item three," said the chair, "is the item I want on the record, because it is the only one anybody in this room will read in a year, and it is the one I am least comfortable about, and I am going to say why I am going to do it anyway. Doctor Okafor has made a hold. The account has discharged a person in defiance of it. Both of those are true and only one of them is in a system. A minute that names a hold and a discharge and the fact that they happened in that order is a document that can be read by any office that has a reason to read it, and I would rather it existed than not, and I would rather it was minutes and not letters."

Nobody said anything, and the young woman wrote, and it was gone.

---

"Mr Crowther, does the account wish to be heard on the matter of the person's account of the events of the sixteenth?"

Crowther looked at his folder and then at Niko Senn.

"He can speak," he said. "He's a party. I'd rather he did and I'd rather he did in his own words than have me paraphrase him, because if I paraphrase him it becomes the account's account and the account is the last office in this city that should be holding a witness's words."

"Niko Senn," said the chair. "You have heard the items. Do you want to say anything to the board?"

"I want to say one thing and I'd like it written down in the words I say it in, because I've had one sentence of mine written down this month by somebody who wasn't there."

"That's fair. Go ahead."

And Niko Senn put his hands flat on the table in front of him, on purpose, the way a man does it when he has been taught to do it, and Elias Venn put his own hands flat on his knees and then took them off again, and said nothing.

"I work nights," said Niko Senn. "I do four nights and I'm on the bus at ten past one and I was on the landing at about that time on the sixteenth of September because I go up to hers about three times a week and we were a queue for four years, and I was never anything to her, and I went up that night the same as I've gone up a hundred times.

"And I put my hand on the frame and the door did the thing it does, and it opened, and she said something to me — she said something, and I couldn't tell you what, and I'm not going to pretend to you that I can — and then the mug went over. It went across the room and it didn't break. And then the door did the click it does. And then I was in the room."

He stopped. He was not a practised speaker and he was not frightened in the way a person is frightened in a police interview; he was frightened in the way a man is frightened in a waiting room.

"And then I was on the step and I don't remember getting to the step," he said, "and the doctor at the clinic knows because she was there at about half four in the morning, and I've told this before and I'll tell it again, and I wanted it in writing in the words I said it in."

"Thank you," said the chair.

The young woman read it back. Three beats in three words, and they were the middle three of what Elias Venn had been handed on the seventeenth of September at about seven in the evening in a family observation room at Saint Orra Reach, with the first of them left out entirely, because a woman had said something on a landing that he could not repeat and had told the room he could not repeat it. He had never written any of it down anywhere, because Mara Quill had put her hand on the door frame and said *stop*.

*Mug. Click. Room.*

He knew what the room said. He had read the room at ten to two in the morning on the seventeenth and the room said the working began after the door opened, and the residue said it, and the two incompatible things had been sitting on a bench in a laboratory ever since and had never been reconciled with a person's mouth, and here was a person's mouth, in public, in a minute, saying the wrong order first, and being believed, correctly, because a frightened man on a bus at ten past one in the morning is not a witness to a floor.

He did not say a word. He had a question — the best question he had had all day, and it was the same question he had wanted to ask on the seventeenth and had not been allowed to ask — and he kept it, because he was a member of the public and had no standing whatever in a ward authority building and had had none since the beginning.

Sana Okafor's eyes came across the room to him for about a second and a half and then went back to the folder, and he understood that she had seen him not ask it, and had understood why, and had not thanked him for it, because it was not a favour to her. It was the room.

---

It took another half hour and then the chair read out the minute and it was the ordinary shape of such things — names, dates, four items, a decision, a direction — and item 3 was three lines long and every word of it was correct.

Jules Ardent read the whole of it standing up in the row, with his finger on the back of the chair in front of him, and when he got to item 3 he said, "That's the one," to nobody, and sat down. A man in the public gallery who has no more right to a copy of that minute than the woman beside him does, and who had read it at about eleven the night before off the back of a chair in a solicitor's waiting room, which is how a practice notice works.

"It isn't a criticism of the board," said Crowther, taking his folder. "I'd like that said. I think that minute is right. I want you all to know I think that minute is right, because in about six months somebody is going to ring me and ask whether I tried to say otherwise."

"Noted," said Ada Lund, in a voice that meant it was not noted, and closed the book.

---

Wen Senn was crying in the corridor by about a quarter past eleven and was extremely angry about it and told three people so, and Niko Senn stood next to her and let her, and Jules Ardent went past them and out of the door at a walk without saying anything to anybody, which was his way of saying it.

Elias Venn waited until the aunt had gone down the stairs before he said the thing he had to say.

"You gave it in the wrong order," he said. "The mug before the click. That's the order from the seventeenth and I never wrote it down anywhere, and this afternoon it's in a minute, and I sat in that room and let it go in."

"I know."

"Do you know why I didn't stop it?"

"Because you're not allowed," said Niko Senn.

"Because I'm not allowed," said Elias Venn, "and also because the moment I stood up in that room and said *the order does not fit the residue*, I would have had to explain how I know the order of a residue, and I have never been able to, and you would have watched it happen."

He had the notebook in his hand and did not open it.

"I want to be clear with you about what I did just now, because in a fortnight's time I am going to be somebody who was in that room and said nothing, and there will be a version of that which is me protecting a case. Here it is. If I had corrected the order, the board would have had to consider whether your account was reliable. And the moment a board has to consider that, the hold becomes a question instead of an answer, and the account gets to argue it, and the next time you are in a room it is about whether you can be believed. A board that you can correct is not a board."

Niko Senn stood in the corridor of a ward authority building with a plastic bag over his shoulder and looked at him for a long moment.

"That's the first time anybody's explained to me why they didn't do something," he said. "I'm glad. I'd have been scared of you if you'd done it."

"I'm glad too," said Elias Venn, and did not say it as a joke, and did not say what he actually felt about it, which was that he had just sat in a room for eighty minutes and discovered that the mechanism he trusted most in himself worked by never being used, and that a frightened man of twenty-four had been the only person in the building who came out of it understanding the arrangement.

At the door of the public hall there was a table, and on the table there was a wire rack, and in the wire rack there were about forty leaflets.

Niko Senn picked one up, looked at the top of it, and said, "That's the one they hand you."

"That's the one they hand you everywhere," said Elias Venn.

He was standing four feet away with his hands in his pockets and a leaflet of his own in the breast of his coat, and there were about forty of them on the rack, and he knew exactly what the one in Niko Senn's hand was, and he said nothing at all.
