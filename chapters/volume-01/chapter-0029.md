# Chapter 0029 — Fig. 3

The conditions were on the bench in her handwriting, four lines long, on a piece of A4 with the top edge torn off, and she put them face up in front of him and then sat down on the other side of it and waited.

"Read it before you say anything at all, including thank you."

---

*1. A new sleeve, and a new log number, and neither of them is reused from this file.*
*2. My name on the consent and your name on the breach, on the same sheet, in the same room, in front of a witness, and the witness is not you and the witness is not me.*
*3. My figure is taken first and my figure is mine, and it goes in my log under my number before anything of yours is written anywhere.*
*4. You do not stop me and I do not stop you, and if either of us stops the other, that is also written down.*

"Is there a fifth thing," said Elias Venn.

"There is, and it is not a condition, it is a sentence." Mara Quill had her sleeves turned back. "I did not consent because you asked nicely and I could not refuse you, because if that is the reason the log is worth nothing and everybody in this building knows it. I consented because on Sunday you read me four sheets in a shop that name a number of mine. It costs me, and I will not pretend otherwise."

"Who is the witness."

"Inspector Kest, at one. She is not on the case, she has no standing to be here, and I want her here for exactly that reason. I told her on the telephone and she said, and I am quoting her, *I would not have thought of that, and I would not have been able to refuse it.*"

"You have not written down that I am not allowed to ask you to stop."

"No," said Mara Quill. "Because if I write that down then you will never put a hand on anything again. And I would like it on the record between us, not in writing, because writing is the only language this building speaks, that the reason you are about to do what you are about to do is not that a document stopped you. It is that a woman who has known you for six years looked at you and could not say the word."

---

Nadia Kest came in at one o'clock with her coat off, looked at the two of them and said, "Right."

"I am here as a witness and not as an inspector, and I would like that written down. The condition that you do not work alone is still in 441-KR. It was made in front of Jules Ardent on the twentieth of September and it does not carry my name, because I was the inspector who permitted it. I checked it at twenty to eight and did not look again." She put her handbag down. "And I am going to be unpleasant about the next half hour, because you filed a prohibition on the twenty-ninth of September and then sent me your drawing by a man from your own counter and never came through the door, and if I say nothing, in a year somebody says I watched."

"I know. Say the prohibition out loud. The sentence, not the item number."

Elias Venn said it out loud, in a laboratory, to two people.

"The assigned detective will not examine any physical object in this matter."

"Twice, and the second occasion in this room, with a written consent. The consent is Mara's and the breach is yours." Nadia Kest wrote the times down as she said them, in a book of her own. "And you will both do the thing in the order she has written down, and if either of you goes out of order I am going to say so out loud, and nobody is going to be pleasant about it."

---

She took the plate out of the case herself. The padlock was on one, and the case stood on the shelf behind her and had stood there for seventeen days and would stand there at four that afternoon, and the new sleeve went on with the date and the time and her initials, and the log number was 1147 and it was hers, and the sleeve said *log 1147* in her hand and *consent: M. Quill* in her hand and *breach: D/Sgt E. Venn* in his, and all three of those were on the same sheet because she had made them be.

Then she did her figure first.

She had his stamped copy of tab 61 on the bench. He had sent it to her on Monday by a man from the Sequence Desk counter with a note asking her to keep it, because he was not allowed into the building and would not ask her to come out of it. She did not have the original and had asked for it and been told by a clerk that it was not producible to a member of the public and that his copy was the one she could have.

"Give me the half," she said, and took it out of the sleeve herself with the tweezers, and put a glass over it, and laid the drawing's channel section over the glass and slid it about until the two outlines agreed.

"They agree. The width, the wall, the lip. And the lip is the only thing in that drawing I could not have told you about from memory, and whoever drew it knew there was a lip."

"The seat."

"The seat is where the drawing stops." She turned the sheet round so he could see. "There is no dimension on it. In a drawing, where there is no dimension, there is a note, and the note says *per description relied on* and gives a number. So the seat's depth is not on the drawing at all, and you cannot measure a drawing, because a picture can be the right size or the wrong size and it is still the same picture." She turned the sheet back. "So I did it the other way round. I measured the metal. I am going to read you the number and you are going to want to say something before I have finished, so don't."

She read it off her own log.

"Two point six hundred and four thousandths of a millimetre. And the number on the drawing, which is a number I certified in Concord Hall on the twenty-seventh of September, in pencil, in my own name, off a plate lifted in a corridor in front of two witnesses, is two point six."

Neither of them said anything for about four seconds.

"That is not the same number," said Nadia Kest.

"It is the same number," said Mara Quill, "to inside four thousandths, and four thousandths of a millimetre is inside a tolerance nobody on earth would write on a drawing, and I did not write it. Somebody read my figure off a certified copy and used it as a dimension. The drawing is right and the metal is right, and the depth of a seat in a piece of channel that came off a wall in a clinic in Kettle Rows is the depth of a threshold in a room in Kettle Rows, and it is right as far as the drawing chooses to state it, and there is no workshop in this city that could have got that number any other way than off a piece of paper."

She put the pencil down.

"And now the part I actually want." She put a second glass down on the bench and used her own hands, which she had told him about at the window on the nineteenth of September and had never once mentioned again. "Look at the seat. Not the depth. The *shape* of the seat, along its length."

It was a fine curve along the inside of the channel for about forty millimetres.

"That is not a cut," said Mara Quill.

"What is it."

"A drawing of a cut." She got closer to the glass, which she never did, because she was the instrument. "A man cutting that would be using a slitting bar or a very small mill, and the cutter is a circle, and the whole of the seat would be one radius end to end, with a witness mark where the tool entered and a burr where it left. There is not one of those in there." She tapped the glass with a knuckle. "Look at it along its length. It changes. The radius narrows about a third of the way along and changes again, and where it changes there is a thickening — about a fortieth of a millimetre, on the inside of the curve, and it is exactly the width of a pen."

Nobody said anything.

"That is a line weight," said Mara Quill. "Somebody drew that seat with a pen and changed pens once, and the place where they changed pens is in the metal, because somebody made the metal out of the drawing, and the drawing had the thickening in it. That is not a thing a man does with a tool. It is a thing that happens to metal when a person works to a picture instead of to the thing."

"There is no tool in there at all," said Elias Venn, who had not spoken for a minute. "There is a hand in there."

"And it was doing drawing office work on an afternoon," said Mara Quill, "and did not know it was going to end up in a cold room in September."

---

"And that is my figure, and it is mine, and it is in log 1147 at 13:11, and I have said the time out loud, and it is in the room."

"I have written it," said Nadia Kest.

"Then say what I have to say next, because I have to say it before you do anything." Mara Quill took her hands off the bench. "Elias. I have measured this and it is a measurement, and it is also, this afternoon, worth nothing: the metal is in my drawer, the drawing is on a bench in a tea room, and nobody has to turn up with either of them. A number with a plate and a number with a drawing is a rumour. It becomes a measurement in about four months, in a chain, and by then it will be somebody else's case and I will be sixty."

She stopped and picked the pencil up and did not use it.

"So I am going to say the one thing I have been not saying since Sunday, and then you will do whatever you are going to do, and I will not have an opinion about it. But I have read your four sheets, on Sunday, in the back of your shop with a cat on the bench. And I want to say one thing about them and then I want to sit down."

"Go on."

"Your four sheets are a list of documents." She said it without any weight, which was the only way she ever said a thing that mattered. "A list of documents gets answered with a document. Any office in this city can produce a document in answer to a list of documents, and they will do it in a fortnight, and every one of the documents will be true, and the whole of the answer will weigh nothing. You have written the most careful thing I have ever read and it has no teeth in it, and I have been trying to work out since Sunday whether that is because it is careful or because it is frightened, and I have decided it is because you have not got the one thing in it that cannot be answered with paper."

"What is that."

"He put a pen in a drawing and changed pens once," said Mara Quill, "and the seat in that channel is the shape of the line he drew, and there is no tool in the world that puts a pen line into brass. That is not a document. That is a thing in a room, and the only answer to it is the thing, and nobody in this city has got the thing." She squared the log book with two fingers, deliberately, where the witness could see her do it. "So publish that. Not the four sheets. Not a list. *That*, and the number, and the fact that it was certified in a Concord office on the twenty-seventh of September by a person who holds no licence, and let them come and find the room, and find it before somebody sits Niko Senn down in a corridor again."

"Elias." Nadia Kest had not moved and her voice was very level. "The order is the order. The consent is signed, the breach is written, the log is open and the figure is done. If you put a hand on that plate, the witness is me and I am writing the time now, and you are going to look at me and not at the glass."

He looked at her. He put his hand flat on the bench in the way a man in a smock had taught a boy to, felt how completely he knew the gesture, took it off and turned it over once and put it down on the sleeve instead, where his name already was, written before he had done it.

"I am going to ask you for one thing, and you can refuse it, and I am telling you now that I am going to do it anyway if you don't, which is not consent, and which is why I am saying it out loud in front of a witness."

"I am not going to be able to refuse it," said Mara Quill. "Put your hand down."

He put it down.

---

It was four seconds. He had been warned about four minutes and his balance on the seventeenth, about his hearing and three hours of that afternoon, about two hours of shaking and the right to handle the clamp on the nineteenth, and about his legs and forty minutes and a nosebleed on the twenty-first. Mara Quill had said at Saint Orra Reach that nobody in the world was going to believe a reading, and had been entirely right, and none of that is what it was like, because none of those had been his own hand on a piece of metal somebody had made a room in.

The order came the way it always came, without a picture of the room, and he did not see the corridor or the bench or Mara or Nadia or the case on the shelf, and there was no light and no distance and nothing at all except the thing he could not do to anybody, which is to say where a thing was made and what it was made out of.

> *The order was the order of a sentence.*
>
> *Not the order of the metal. Everything in that channel was made after the sentence. The sentence came first, on an afternoon, at a desk, in a drawing office, and it gave a depth, and the depth was a number about a floor, and then a man drew it, and then a seat was cut to a picture of what he had drawn, and then a piece was folded into it, and then a stamp was cut, and then the whole of it went out of a door in a carrier bag on somebody's shoulder.*
>
> *And the second thing was that there was no man in it at the end. He had been expecting a man. There was a sentence, and a number, and a pen that changed once, and a bag.*

Then it stopped, and it took four minutes for his hands to be his own again, and he sat down on the edge of the bench, and Nadia Kest said the time out loud, and it was 13:20, and she wrote it down, and Mara Quill put the plate into a sleeve and closed the case and put the case on the shelf, and the padlock went through, and the case was where it had been at one o'clock, and the bench was clear.

"You want to know what it took," said Mara Quill, from the other side of the room. "It took you the order of today. It has not given it back and it will not give it back this afternoon."

"How do you know that."

"Because I have watched you not know where you are four times since the seventeenth, and every one of those went away in a few minutes, and there is no version of this that does." She sat down on the stool. "Go home, Elias."

"I can't."

"Then walk. And do not drive, and do not make anybody drive you, and do not stand in the middle of a road."

---

He got as far as the door and had to sit on the step outside the linen office for a while, and got up again after a stretch of time he could not put in an order, and walked. It was half past three when somebody told him the time and it was not the time he had thought.

He did not telephone anybody, because he had no sequence to give anybody. He got on a bus without knowing which one and got off at the only stop he recognised, which was the end of Kettle Rows, went into his own shop at about four and put the bag on the bench under the window, and then could not remember doing it.

Otto Vahl found him at about six, standing at the front bench with his own bench, working at nothing.

"You've been stood in that spot since about four."

"Sorry, Otto."

"You're not here," said Otto Vahl, and went back to his radio, and did not ask a single question about it.

He found him again at about nine, on the office chair with a cup of tea he had not drunk.

"Kettle Rows Community Clinic. Okafor. She has telephoned the shop twice since six because she wants somebody to confirm you're alive and she won't say why, and I've told her twice you're alive and nothing else, and I would like to know whether I should stop telling her that." And Elias Venn said, "Tell her I'm fine," and Otto Vahl said, "That is the third time today somebody has said that to me about you, and not one of the three of you has said a word to the other two," and went to bed at ten.

Elias Venn sat in a dark shop and could not tell you, in any order at all, whether the drawing had come before the bus or after the chip shop, and the not-knowing was not a feeling and was not a symptom and was not frightening, and it was the plainest description of the world he had ever had: a set of things, with nothing on top of them saying what came first.

At about half past nine he went up to his own flat and did not sleep much, and at ten past five the next morning the telephone in the shop rang and it was Dr Okafor, and it took him forty seconds to work out that it was morning.

---

"Mr Venn. It is twenty past five in the morning and I am telephoning a shop, and you are the reason I have a shop to telephone." Dr Okafor's voice was somebody who had been awake since four. "I am going to put four sheets of paper on a wall at half past eight and I am not doing it without a sentence in front of it, and I am not writing the sentence, and you are going to have to say it in a room with nine people in it. I telephoned because I have been awake since four working out how to do that without a police officer in it, and I have failed, and the least bad version has you in it."

"I'm coming."

"You are not well."

"I know what I am not," said Elias Venn, and put his hand flat on the counter of his own shop, and could not say afterwards, in any order, what he had been doing between half past nine and ten past five.

"Doctor, I want to tell you something in a room and not on a telephone. I do not know whether I can put the four things in the order they go in, and if I cannot, I am going to read them in the order they are written on the paper, and that will be a different thing and a worse thing, and I am telling you now so that you can decide whether to take the wall down."

A pause of about three seconds.

"Get here by half past five," said Dr Okafor. "And bring the four sheets and do not fold them, because a folded sheet on a wall is a notice and a flat sheet on a wall is a document, and I have spent three weeks on that."

---

He got to the clinic at twenty past six. Sana Okafor was standing in front of the board in the waiting room with a marker, and the four sheets were not on it, and the ninety-odd pages of the maintenance book were on the left in their clear sleeves and the registrar's transfer order was on the right in its sleeve, and there was a space in the middle the size of four sheets of A4.

Mara Quill was there at a quarter to seven, for a reason that was not him, and said one sentence to him on the way past, which was the only sentence either of them said to each other all morning.

"I wrote it last night," she said. "The breach. It is in the post and it is worse than what you would have written, and I would like you to know that I read every word of it before I sealed it and I sealed it anyway."

---

At ten to ten, in the corridor, with his hand on a door frame, Elias Venn found that he could tell you what had happened.

The telephone at ten past five. The bus he did not remember getting off. Otto Vahl at six and at nine. The four conditions on a torn sheet of A4. Nadia Kest at one saying *right*. The half-metal in a sleeve. A number off a log. A curve that changes.

Twenty hours and thirty minutes, and it came back in a corridor outside a clinic at ten to ten on Wednesday morning with nobody announcing it, in the middle of a sentence about a wall.

He did not say anything about it, because there was a woman in front of a board with a marker in her hand, and because he had learned over ten days that the things he did not write down were the things that got used, and because Nadia Kest was writing in a book of her own in the corridor behind him and did not need to be told.

She finished, and closed it, and said: "13:20 to 09:50, and it came off in a corridor, and I am going to write *reported by the officer*, because I was not there and I do not want a phrase in a book of mine that I cannot source."
