# Chapter 0023 — Two Rooms, One Set of Words

The bucket was leaking and Niko Senn had folded a newspaper into the bottom of it, and when Elias Venn came through the door at ten to nine on the morning of Wednesday the third of October he was on his knees behind the counter of his aunt's launderette with his sleeves rolled and his forearms in the water.

"That's the cold feed," said Niko Senn, without turning round. "It has been doing that since August and she has told the man about it twice."

---

Wen Senn was at the clinic for a blood test at ten and had left the shop, and the keys, and a list of four things written on the back of an envelope, and had told her nephew that if he let the counter get out of hand she would be having words with him in front of the machine.

"I have been up since half past six," Niko Senn said, getting the weight out of one side of the bucket. "I do nights. I do not do them tonight, I do them tomorrow, and I have got the depot at two. That is the whole reason I am standing in a bucket instead of being at the clinic."

He had a plastic bag with a clean shirt in it, because he had stopped owning more than one clean shirt, and there was a smell of hot cotton off the machines that Elias Venn had last smelled in a corridor on Marshall Street at about four in the morning eleven days ago.

"You've come for the thing," said Niko Senn.

"I have."

"Which thing."

"Any of them." Elias took his notebook out and did not open it. "And I'm going to start by telling you that the woman at that counter has taken your Thursday morning off. You have a board at ten that day and you are a party to it, and she was not going to do that twice for the same man, and she told me on Tuesday in a corridor when I had not asked her for it." He put the notebook away. "She has put you back in on the eighteenth, same time of day, and it took her four minutes to do it and she did not have to. And I'm not going to try and talk you out of the eighteenth. You'll go."

Niko Senn wrung the bucket out into the sink and looked at him.

"You told me Monday not to read anything anybody gives me."

"I did, and I meant it, and I'd say it again."

"And I'm going anyway."

"You're going anyway." Elias Venn stood with his hands in his pockets, which was the thing he did with them when he wanted them somewhere he could account for. "Then here's what I'd say about it, and it's the same thing I said and not a different thing because you asked me to. Whatever you get handed in that room, you bring out in your own hand. You don't sign anything you haven't read standing up. You don't take anything home. And if anybody in there asks you what order you saw things in, you say *I don't know* and you let that be the end of it, because an uncertain account recorded as uncertain is worth more than a complete one you can't stand behind."

"That was on the leaflet."

"That was on the leaflet, and the leaflet is in the inside pocket of this coat, and I did not take it off you because a leaflet is a public thing and you gave it to me." He turned the notebook so the back cover faced up, where a single sheet of hospital paper was folded in half and worn soft, and on Tuesday morning at a rack on the ground floor of Concord Hall he had copied the four lines out of the box onto it in his own small bad hand and dated them underneath. "So it's in the coat and it's in here, and mine is the one with a date on it, and that is not vanity. In about a year one of the two is going to be the thing somebody has to look at."

Niko Senn looked at the folded edge of it for a second and nodded, and then he dried his hands on the front of his jeans, which is what everybody in that launderette had done since it opened.

"Right," he said. "Hardaker."

---

"He doesn't have a shopfront," said Niko Senn. "There's a tax office on the ground floor with a green sign and a door round the side of it, and a stair, and a door at the top with a smock hanging on it. Second floor. He's in most days and he books by telephone and he is not on any list I've ever seen and I have never once seen a certificate on that wall, and I asked him about it, and he said he doesn't hold one and never has and isn't going to, and I believed him, and I want you to understand that I believed him on the day."

"What's the address."

"Fallow Street. Off the Verge Row side, past the launderette that isn't yours, up the hill where the ground starts going up. It's a yellow door and there's a window that opens and a bell that has a chip in it." Niko Senn recited the whole of it like a bus route, because he had walked it four times.

Elias Venn wrote it down. He wrote it in the small bad hand in the front of the notebook, and he did not put a case number beside it, and he did not put a time beside it, and he wrote the street and the door and the day of the week and nothing else.

Niko Senn watched him do it, which he had not expected to be able to stop himself from doing.

"That's the first thing you've written down that I actually said."

"It's yours," said Elias. "You said it to me on a Wednesday morning while you were wringing out a bucket. It's in my book, and this book has no case number on it and never will have, and there is nobody in this city who can ask to see it. If you told me the time of day instead I'd have written that too, and the reason I don't is that a time of day in a notebook belonging to a detective sergeant is a thing a person ends up explaining at some point, and I have not got the two hours for that conversation this week."

Niko Senn considered it.

"That's the second time you've told me why you don't take something."

"It's the third," said Niko Senn. "And I did count, because I have been standing here with a bucket doing nothing else and there was not a great deal on offer." He put the bucket down on the tiles. "I'm not giving you the card."

"I know."

"You keep saying that like a fact about the future and not about today."

"Both," said Elias Venn. "Today, and the version of today where I am wrong about it in six months."

---

The door was yellow and the window was open and the bell had a chip in it, and the stair smelled of other people's stairs, and at the top the door stood ajar with a white smock hanging on the back of it.

Inside there were two rooms. The front one had a couch with a blanket folded over the arm of it, a table with two chairs on the same side of it, a kettle on a hotplate, four mugs, and a window onto a wall about nine feet away. On the wall over the table, at the height where a person sitting down would have to look up to read it, was a price list on a card:

*Grounding and recall. Forty credits the hour. Two hours' notice where possible. Receipt given.*

There was a shelf with a client book on it, and the spine of the client book had gone white, and there was a second shelf with about nine books on it and a mug ring on the top one, and there was a form on a clipboard on the table with a ballpoint through a chain.

Elias Venn read the form from where he stood, at arm's length, the way he read everything.

*CITIZEN INFORMATION — SEQUENCE SUPPORT. PRIVATE PRACTICE.*
*Consent of the person attending.*
*1. I will describe what I saw, in the order I saw it.*
*2. Where I cannot hold the order, I will say that I cannot hold the order, and that record is made and stands.*
*3. I am not to be helped into an order I did not see.*
*4. I may stop at any time. I may withdraw afterwards.*

He read it twice and then stood there and did not say anything at all for about four seconds, because four lines of a printed form on a clipboard in a room above a tax office were the same four lines as the box on the leaflet in the back of his notebook, and the same four lines as page eleven of a book that cost three credits on Tuesday morning.

A man came out of the back room with a tea towel over his shoulder. He was forty-six or thereabouts and he had a face that had been doing this a long time, and he did not stop and he did not become careful, and he said:

"Sit down. Do you want tea?"

"I have about twenty minutes," said Elias Venn. "I'm not here about myself. I'm a police officer and I have got a name in my notebook and I want to ask you one question."

"Then you'd better ask it before the water's boiled," said the man, "because if you say yes to the tea you're going to be here an hour, and I'd rather you didn't. I have a man at half past twelve who cannot travel, and if you stay until one I have to ring him and tell him what an inspector wants."

"Who's the name."

"Neil Hardaker. That's me." He put the tea towel over the back of a chair. "Forty credits the hour, and that's on the wall, and I don't do anything for nothing, and I don't do anything under the counter either. What is the question?"

---

"You've done a session in a client's own premises," said Elias Venn. "Twice."

Neil Hardaker looked at him for about a second and a half and then said, "Yes."

"You've answered that faster than I expected."

"It's on the wall of every office in this city that trains people to do what I do," said Hardaker. "Page eleven, module four, second box. *The session is conducted in a room the practitioner has prepared. Where a session is conducted in other premises, the practitioner records the premises, the date and the reason.* So the answer to your question is in a book, and if I sat here and worked out whether I was going to tell you, I'd be doing something a bit more interesting than being honest, and you would notice."

"Who were they?"

"I'm not going to tell you that."

"I didn't ask who they were."

"You asked who they were," said Hardaker. "You asked it in the plural and about the premises, and if I answer *two* you have asked me for the two, and there is a way for me to answer the question that isn't a lie and isn't a refusal, and you are welcome to it."

Elias Venn took the notebook out and shut it.

"Go on."

"The fourth of August and the sixteenth of September," said Hardaker. "Both times the reason on my sheet is *the person cannot travel*, because both times the person could not travel, and one of them was on a second floor with no lift and one of them was in a room at the back of a building with a corridor he could not get out of quickly. I have written down where I went and what day I went and why, in a book, in columns, because the book says to, and because in 2021 I paid three credits for the book and the least I can do is keep it."

"You've written the address?"

"I've written the address, the date and the reason, and the time I was there and the time I left, and the number of the referral if there was one, and if there wasn't one I have written *none given*, because that is information too." He picked up the tea towel again and folded it. "That's why I'm not reading you the names. The book is mine. You can have the dates because they're dates. You cannot have the names because the names are not a public document and the only person who would be harmed by me reading them out is the person whose name it is."

Elias Venn did not ask for the names. It took him about two seconds not to, and in those two seconds he heard the sentence a man of twenty-four had said in a launderette in front of a bucket, and he heard it the way you hear a word you have decided not to hum.

*If I give you the card then it wasn't worth anything, it was evidence. Then I'll be the one who made it evidence and not the one who got told to.*

"Is the card the referral?" he said.

"Somebody's, yes. A referral's how they count. If a card sends somebody to me, the card's got a number on it and no address, and the number's mine to keep and theirs to print, and I'll tell you honestly that I have never once seen the Concord's copy and I have never once asked to." He sat down on the edge of the table. "Now. You said you had a name in a notebook and you have asked me one question and got a straight answer to it, which almost never happens on a Wednesday morning, so ask me the other one."

"You did four sessions with a man called Niko Senn."

"I did."

"Four, at forty credits, in the last week of August, because he was working nights and he wasn't sleeping."

"He wasn't sleeping and he was frightened of something and he wouldn't say what," said Hardaker. "And he had a card with a number on it and no name on it, and he told me a man gave it to him, and I asked him whether he wanted to give the card to me and he said no, and I said fine, keep it, and that's the whole of what happened between us over that card. And I taught him the exercise out of module four, which is *now imagine somebody is in the room with you, and wait for a tap on the table, and when you hear the tap, breathe out and say the name*, and I did it four times, and I did not vary it, and I could not vary it if I tried, because there is nothing in it to vary."

Elias Venn was standing very still in a room that was nine feet from a wall.

"Tell me the module exactly," he said. "Any part of it that's in the room."

Hardaker reached over the couch, took the top book off the shelf, and put it on the table between them, and did not open it.

"Third printing, 2026, three credits, forms counter, Morrow Hill," he said. "And a course tutor showed me the sentences in 2021 at an evening class that the Authority runs and charges nothing for, and I have never met the man since and I don't know his name, and he told me at the start that none of it could put anything in anybody's head, and that was true, and I'd still say it's true."

---

"How do you make your living?" said Elias Venn.

"That's a different question and it's the one I actually like."

"Answer it."

"Four hundred people a year at forty credits an hour, and about a third of them come twice," said Hardaker. "I have a waiting list in November. I turn work down. I've turned down two in six years and both times it was the same thing — a woman came to me with a card that had a number on it and no name, and wanted to start inside a fortnight, and didn't want to pay me, and wanted the session to be where she already was. And I said no, because I have never been able to work out who is paying, and a person who is not paying is a person somebody else is paying for, and I have a client book with names in it because I am honest, and I am not going to have a name in that book that came in on a card."

"Have you ever been to the Concord?"

"To the forms counter, in 2021, with three credits, same as everybody."

"To Witness Services."

"To the counter at the back, once, to find out what the free service actually was before I sent anybody to it," said Hardaker. "It's better than mine. That's not a joke and it's not modesty. They have a certified practitioner sitting in a room four days a week, and the room has a door that shuts, and a person who has lost the order of something can go and be told by somebody the Authority pays for. I've sent two people there. Both of them were mine, both of them couldn't pay me, and I sent them because I could stand in a room with a person for two hours and I couldn't promise a police officer or a court or anybody that I'd got it right." He put his hand flat on the form on the clipboard, and then noticed, and took it off again, and laughed at himself once. "Bad habit. Different one to yours."

Elias Venn looked at the wall behind the table and there was a hook on it and nothing on the hook, and there was a second hook and nothing on that one either, and there was a pale rectangle on the paint where something with a wide head had hung for years. There was nothing in the room that was a tool. He noticed that the way you notice a missing tooth, and he did not ask about it, because he had no question about it that he was entitled to ask in a room he had walked into without a warrant and without a card in his hand that was not his own.

"That's the woman at the counter," he said. "On Tuesday. She was straight with me and she gave me nothing and she was right to."

"They all are," said Hardaker. "That's a service where the people in it can be relied on, which is the entire point of the Authority doing it, and I'll tell you what I think about that and then you can decide what to do with it. I think it's right. I think the police should not be the ones holding the order of things, because the police are in it, and I think a woman with a clipboard in a corridor who says *a residue on its own is not an account* is doing the single most important thing anybody in this business does. And I'd like to point out that I've been doing it in two rooms above a tax office since 2021, and nobody has ever asked me to, and I could not tell you one single person who was sent to me by somebody with a reason."

"Is he all right?" said Hardaker.

It came out of him without any preamble at all, and it changed the temperature of the room, because it was the first thing he had said that had not been carefully arranged.

"I can't tell you that."

"He's a customer of mine. He paid me. I have a duty to know."

"You have a duty to be a good man to him and you have been one." Elias Venn put his hands in his pockets. "He's twenty-four and he's on a board on Thursday morning at ten o'clock in a room in a ward authority building, and he is the best witness in this city and he is not in a file, and I am not allowed to be in that room except as a person in a chair with the public in it, and I have spent a fortnight trying to keep it that way. That's the whole of what I can give you and I'm giving you all of it."

Hardaker looked at the door and then at the window nine feet from the wall.

"He said he was frightened of something and it wasn't the room," he said. "That's the part I've thought about. He said *I'm not frightened of the room* and then he wouldn't say what he was frightened of, and I have run that over a good many times, and I still think it's a room, because the ones I've met are always a room."

"Yes," said Elias Venn. "I know. I know that. Keep it."

---

He paid him forty credits for an hour he had used forty minutes of, and asked for the receipt, and Hardaker said that nobody had ever asked for one, and then went to a shelf and got a pad and tore a slip off it and wrote on it in a small upright hand.

*Grounding and recall. One hour, part. Forty credits. N. Hardaker. No Concord registration.*

He stamped it with a thing that was not a stamp, it was an inked rubber with the letters on it worn, and tore it off and gave it over, and Elias Venn put it in his wallet, which was where it was going to live, because a man who has been handed a piece of paper with a number on it and no address on it by a man in a smock has learned something about that in the last fortnight even if it took him until Wednesday to be able to use it.

He got out into the street at about ten past twelve and the hill up to the Verge Row side was steeper than the number six front room had suggested and the air smelled of hot cotton and diesel.

He did not telephone anybody.

He stood on the pavement outside a tax office and wrote four lines in the small bad hand in the front of the notebook, and the fourth of them was a date on its own with nothing beside it at all, and he looked at it for a while, and he did not write next to it, because it was a date.

The fourth of August. The sixteenth of September.

Then he put the notebook away and got the bus, because the bus went past the clinic on Marshall Street and he had been on Marshall Street a great deal in the last fortnight and had not been in the room behind the dispensary once.
