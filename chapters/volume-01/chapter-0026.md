# Chapter 0026 — Off File

Paul Rennick had been in the building since half past seven and had read the whole of 441-KR on the corner of a desk with his coat still on, and when Elias Venn came in at eight he stood up, and offered a hand, and then took it back and wiped his hand on his trousers, which was a thing he had clearly decided to do before Elias Venn came through the door.

"Rennick. I'm taking the file."

"I know."

"They told me on Friday morning by telephone, which I think is a service to both of us." He was about forty-five and had been six years in the unit and he did not appear to have opinions about any of it, which after three weeks was a rest. "I've read it. I'll tell you now what I think and then I'll stop telling you things, because you're not on it and there's a point at which asking a man who has just been relieved questions becomes harassment."

"Go on."

"I think it's in a bad state and I think that's not your fault and I think most of it is not anybody's." Rennick squared the folder. "I've got a Registry attestation that says one thing and a schedule on the back of it that says another, and ninety-odd pages of a clinic's own maintenance book in a paper wallet, and a man's refusal of a countersignature with his reasons, and a public information sheet with the words *contained accident* on it, and about four hundred pages of somebody else's photocopying. And somewhere in the middle of it there's a young man who is the only person alive who can tell anybody what happened on that landing, and he isn't a party to my file, and I have to find out whether he wants to be, and I haven't decided today."

"He withdraws," said Elias Venn.

Rennick stopped with his hand on the folder.

"When?"

"Yesterday. About ten to nine. On a form from the office that certifies him." Elias Venn took his coat off and hung it on the back of a chair that was not his chair any more. "It came in answer to the Registry's notice that a withdrawal had to be produced. There is a bundle and it has got his order in it and there is a record in 441-KR of the times, and I am going to have to go and see him at some point, and if I have gone first I would like it noted that I have gone first."

"Noted," said Paul Rennick. "I'll put it in as a contact note with the time on it and I'll put the time in before I put the note in, because the time is the part that matters and the note is the part that gets read."

---

The retention review was a single side on a clipboard and it had been on Nadia Kest's desk since Friday afternoon, and it was the only paper in the building with a box on it that nobody had to argue about.

*REVIEW OF RETENTION — ASSIGNED OFFICER*
*File 441-KR. Reviewed by an inspector. Ground of review: [ ] conduct [ ] capability [ ] conflict of interest [x] other — connected certified record, application pending.*
*Recommendation.*

The other three boxes were empty and had never been going to be anything else, and the word *misconduct* did not appear on the page, and Elias Venn read it twice to be certain.

"She's signed it," he said.

"She signed it at five to four on Friday and gave it to Deputy Chief Aveline Orr's office at ten past four, and Orr's office logged it at twenty past four and it came into effect at forty to twelve this morning." Nadia Kest was standing at the end of the desk with her coat on, which meant she was on her way out and had stopped. "Which is the exact minute the application was served on you yesterday, and that is not a coincidence, that is a sequence, and I put them in that order myself on purpose, and I asked for the effective time to be the served time and I was not refused."

"You wrote the review that removed me."

"I signed the review that removed you. The review is an inspector's paper and I am the inspector and there are two of us in this city who can sign it and the other one is on leave until the fourteenth." She did not soften it and she did not enjoy it either, which he had expected. "Elias. I want to say the ground out loud so you have heard it from me. It is not misconduct. Nobody has said a word about your conduct since the twenty-first of September and if anybody does it will not be me. It is that a member of the public with a pending application in another matter is an interested person in this one, and the Registry knows it, and an inspector who keeps a connected record in the hands of a detective whose connection is on a form is a bad inspector. That's it. That's the whole ground and it's correct and I would have written it if you'd asked me to write it."

"Then I asked you to write it."

"You asked me to keep you on until Thursday," said Nadia Kest. "I did. It was a bad instruction and I took it anyway because Niko Senn had a board, and Niko Senn has had his board, and there is now a document about him that says he cannot hold an order, and that document is not going to be better because you are in the room."

She put the clipboard on the corner of the desk and squared it to the edge.

"The file went at forty to twelve," she said. "I signed you off and Rennick signed you on and we did it at the same desk in about ninety seconds and neither of us said anything worth putting in a minute."

---

There were two things on the desk that were not the file, and he picked them up in the wrong order.

The first was a folded A4 sheet with a delivery receipt stapled to it, and the receipt had a time on it in a box, and a name in a column, and a signature.

**SERVED BY HAND. 5 OCTOBER, 11:40.**
*Received at: 14 Kettle Rows, second floor.*
*Signature: E. Venn.*

Underneath the receipt, the answer. One paragraph, on a sheet with a Deputy Chief's office reference in the corner, in the flat language that a large office uses when it is declining something it has been asked to do twice.

*Thank you for your application for access to the certified Morrow Street record, received and logged on 30 September. The Tribunal archive has reported the sequence position of that matter to this office. The archive reports sequence positions. A form category is a publishing convention of a certifying authority and is not a sequence position, and the archive does not report publishing conventions. Your application is declined. You may not seek the same record by another application within twelve months without the written authority of the Deputy Chief.*

"Twelve months," said Elias Venn.

"Twelve months."

"That's new."

"That's not new, that's in the form, and I read it out to you in a car park a week ago and you read it and you signed it, and I have thought about it every day since and I have not been able to find a way round it, and I am not going to pretend this morning that I could."

He put the two sheets down side by side. One said he was too connected to be told, and the other said he had asked the wrong question, and both of them had been written in good faith by people who had done their jobs, and the combination of them was a man who could not find out what was in a box on a form in a report about a collapse twelve years old, and had now been told he could not ask again until October next year.

"The archive reported on Friday," he said.

"The archive reported on Friday to Deputy Chief Orr's office and the report is a document in that office and it is not going to be sent to you and it is not going to be sent to me."

"Then who read it."

"A clerk," said Nadia Kest. "On Thursday, in an archive on the far side of the hill, with a request in front of her that came from the Deputy Chief's office and not from this one, and she opened a file from 2017 and read it, and she did it in a room by herself, and it took her about a day, and nobody in this building was told she was doing it until it was over."

"And the answer to my question is in it?"

"The answer to your question is a paragraph that says the archive reports sequence positions and does not report publishing conventions, and that is not in it either — that paragraph was written for you this morning by a person who has read it and is being careful with it." She stopped. "Elias. I want to say the next part and I am not going to be able to say it nicely."

"Go on."

"I have been in this job twenty-two years," said Nadia Kest, "and on Friday a woman opened the only document in this city that could tell you what was in a box twelve years ago, and she was not you, and she was not me, and she was doing it because I wrote four hundred words on a Saturday in September that I sent myself and signed and did not copy you on, and I told you in a car which of the two halves of that was deliberate."

"You did."

"So the thing you have been waiting twelve years for is being read by somebody, and there is not one thing I can do about it, and I am the reason it is not you." She said it the way she said everything, flat, and then she said the other thing. "And I would do it again on the Saturday, and I would still be doing it on the Saturday, and you have every right to be extremely angry with me about it, and I have asked to be argued with rather than admired and I am now going to ask you to be argued with."

---

He did the arithmetic on the twenty-ninth of September at about half past nine in the morning, standing up, with the two sheets in his hand.

At about eleven o'clock on **Saturday** the twenty-ninth he had been at a wire rack on a low table by a radiator on the ground floor of Concord Hall, buying a book about sequence-box codes for four credits.

At about half past one that same afternoon he had walked into the Sequence Desk on Morrow Hill and filed a nine-item note, signed and dated, with a printed instruction along the bottom telling anybody who processed it as a request that somebody in his building had done something worse than what he had done.

And at half past one on that same Saturday afternoon, four floors and one street away, at the counter at the back of the same building he had been standing in at eleven o'clock, a woman named Kaye had read a man a line off a sheet with a clip on it and told him to hold to it.

And item eight of it, in his own hand, said that there was a certificate in a clinic's file on a public board twenty feet away, and that he had read the box on that document four times, and that he had never read the issuing-office line.

He had never read the issuing-office line. It had been in a plastic sleeve on a wall on Marshall Street. He had been twenty feet from it. It said **Witness Services (Sequence Support)**. It had cost him four minutes and it had taken him four days to get round to, and there was a man in the outer wards who had been told to hold an order that had been typed out of a telephone call Elias Venn had made in a corridor eight days before, fifty-two minutes later in the day and in the same city, by somebody who had never heard of him.

He put the two sheets down on the desk in a neat stack because he could not think what else to do with his hands, and he did not say anything about it, and he was not going to say anything about it, because saying it out loud would have been a tenth item and there were only nine.

---

"The third thing on your desk," he said, "is the out-of-hours magistrate's refusal, and you have not opened it."

"It came by hand at twenty to five on Friday afternoon and it is addressed to me and it is a refusal and there is no hurry in the world about it, so I have left it shut all weekend." She took her handbag off the back of the chair. "You should know that the week I had with it was a week of choosing not to open it, and I am telling you that so that you can decide what you think of me afterwards with the right facts."

"I have already decided what I think of you."

"That is a great comfort and it is not what I asked for." She put the envelope on the desk and pushed it across. "Read it standing up, and then you are going to tell me that I should have named the address, and I am going to tell you that I am not going to, and then we are both going to have to live with a two-hundred-word refusal from a magistrate on Vine Street."

*Hadwin Slade, out-of-hours, Vine Street. Refused. 1 October.*

*1. The application does not identify the premises for which authority is sought. The court cannot test an affidavit against an unnamed place, and the applicant declined to name it to the court as well as to this office.*

*2. The material on which the application rests is a document received by another authority on 19 September. It is not identified in the application, and the applicant has declined to identify it. It does not, on its face, connect to any person, address or thing capable of being entered.*

*3. The application is refused as presented. The applicant is advised to consider whether there is a document that speaks to the premises, and to consider that an application which names a place and is refused on the merits is a different instrument from one which does not, and is capable of being answered differently.*

*4. This refusal does not prevent a further application. It prevents a further application in the same terms.*

He read it twice.

"He wrote you a tutorial," said Elias Venn.

"He wrote me a tutorial," said Nadia Kest, "in four numbered paragraphs, at half past ten at night, to a police inspector, and he has done it before and he will do it again, and if you read the third one properly he has told you the thing that would have worked and I have not been able to work out how to get there myself, which is that the problem was never the address, it was that there is no document in the world that names the place. The annex is a piece of paper that somebody sent me. It has got a number on it and no place on it. And I have spent three weeks being angry at a magistrate when the magistrate has been completely correct and I have been the one arriving without a document."

"Then you'll go and get a document."

"There isn't one," said Nadia Kest. "There is a man in an outer ward with a bench book in it and a bench room I can't lawfully enter, and two stamps I can't compare, and a woman in a laboratory who will not put her plate on a register, and a man of twenty-four who has withdrawn his account. And every one of those is a gap I could only close by naming a place to a court, and I have declined to do that twice now, and I am not going to start doing it because a magistrate has written me a polite note."

She picked the envelope back up and put it in her handbag.

"I'm not going to name it," she said. "I want that said out loud in a building rather than in a car. I am going to go on not naming it, and the refusal is correct, and I would rather be refused nine times than hand a young man's address to a court in a form I cannot control."

---

They went down at twenty to one because the car park was the only place a conversation of that length could happen without a door, and Nadia Kest stood by the driver's side of her own car with her handbag on the roof and did not get in.

"One question," said Elias Venn, "and then I'll go and let you have your Saturday."

"Ask."

"Do you think this department is better off without me on this file."

"Yes," said Nadia Kest.

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is four minutes and I'm going to be quick because if I take longer I'll stop." She did not take longer. "Nineteen days ago you were a sergeant who had a rare faculty and a case and a file, and you spent the whole of it refusing to write down the things you weren't sure of, and every single one of those refusals made this case better and made you more dangerous to the people who did it. Since twenty to twelve this morning you have been a man with no file, no board and no standing to ask a question, and a Registry document that says his own description of an order was used to train a witness, and about four people in this city who are frightened. Do you think the department is better off without you on it? Yes. Not because of what you did. Because of what you published, and you have not published anything yet, and everything that has happened to this file for four days has happened because you filed a note with your own name on it and a printed line along the bottom."

"That is not what I did."

"It is not what you did," said Nadia Kest. "It is what you did, and the two of them are going to be the same thing for the rest of your working life, and I have watched a man do it once and it did not kill him, so I am not going to be dramatic about it with you. The second half is that I would rather this department were better off without you on this file than that you had stayed on it and got what you wanted, and those are not the same sentence, and I have not got the right words for the difference and I have wanted them since Friday night."

She took her handbag off the roof.

"That was four minutes."

"Yes."

"I am going to say one more thing and then I'm going to be extremely pleasant to you for the rest of the day, and you are not to read anything into it." She opened the car door. "When Rennick finds out that there's a form in the file with a withdrawal in it and a man of twenty-four in it, he will do the correct thing, and the correct thing will be to get a witness-support account into his own file through his own office, and it will take him about nine days, and in those nine days the office is going to be the only account in the world of what that young man holds. And you and I have known that since Tuesday, and neither of us can stop it, and I am not going to pretend we can."

---

He went back up at ten past two and took his notebook off the corner of the desk where it had lived since Monday morning, and Priya Sandoval was at the far end of the room with a telephone headset on and she took it off one ear.

"Mine," he said. "It's going to the shop."

"All right," said Priya Sandoval, and put the headset back on, and did not ask a single question, which was the single most professional thing anybody had done for him in a fortnight.

The board on the wall had been washed. The three columns were back on it in marker — **Physical. Residue. Paper** — in Priya Sandoval's upright hand, and under **Physical** there was a case number, a street, and a date, and the street was one he had never been to and had never heard of.
