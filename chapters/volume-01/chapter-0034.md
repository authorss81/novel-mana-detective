# Chapter 0034 — The Sixteenth

The shop was quiet before eight on Thursday morning and Elias Venn used the hour the way he had used every early hour since the tenth: bench, kettle, notebook. He tested the drawer, found it clean, logged two repairs, and wrote the day's shape on a fresh page: *Alderney Row 09:00. Fallow St 11:00. Do not follow to Concord Hall.*

Otto Vahl came in at half past eight with yesterday's takings counted and the cat on his heels.

"Niko Senn's day," said Otto, who missed nothing. "Half past eleven."

"I am going to the aunt's at nine," said Elias. "Not to the Hall. I am asking about the sixteenth and leaving him to decide."

Otto nodded. "Cakes. The ones with icing. The woman in the corner shop lets you have them if you say the aunt's name."

"I know," said Elias, and bought them on the way to the bus.

Wen Senn kept the launderette counter at number six, Alderney Row, with the ironing board folded behind the door and the kettle on a ring that took eleven minutes. Elias Venn arrived at nine on Thursday morning with a bag of small cakes he had bought on the way and put them on the counter without comment, because he had learned from a telephone call on the tenth that cakes are a thing you do in that house and a man who mentions it has already made it a transaction.

Niko Senn sat at the table under the window with his hands round a mug. He was twenty-four, working nights, one night off in seven, and he looked like a man who had not slept before an appointment.

"Morning," said Elias. "I am not here about the half past eleven. I am here about the sixteenth of September."

Wen Senn put the kettle on. Niko Senn looked up.

"Say it properly," said Wen Senn to her nephew, without turning round. "No mumbling. He has come on a bus."

Niko Senn nodded. "The sixteenth. Sunday. I went to Kettle Rows in the evening. To leave a timetable slip for Tamsin Ro. She had asked me for the late bus times because the depot had changed them and she did not trust the notice."

Elias took the notebook out and opened it on the table where both of them could see it.

"Tell me in your own words, and I will write the times as you give them."

Wen Senn set a plate of cakes on the table — the ones with icing, the ones she liked — and sat on the edge of the ironing board with her hands folded, listening the way she listened to the launderette counter: completely, without interrupting, keeping the whole of it for afterwards.

"I got there about seven. Went up to the fourth floor. Her door was shut. I put the slip under it. Stood on the third landing a bit because the stair light was late." Niko Senn looked at his hands. "Heard a click. Cannot tell you where from. Cannot tell you what order anything was in. Left. That is all of it."

"Did you go into any room?"

"No."

"Did anybody speak to you?"

"No. Heard nobody. The building was quiet."

Elias wrote: *16 Sep evening, 4th floor landing, slip under door, click heard, order not statable. — N. Senn, 18 Oct.*

"Mr Venn," said Niko Senn. "The thing on the sixteenth is not finished and has my name on it as well as hers. I said that on the fifth and nobody asked me. I am saying it again now. I do not know what it was. I am not asking you to find it. I am telling you so you know I know."

"I know," said Elias. "I have written it as you said it. I am not making it into a route. You have an appointment at half past eleven and you do not have to keep it to keep your withdrawal true. The withdrawal stands however you decide."

Niko Senn looked at him for a long moment.

"The card," he said. "You have refused it three times."

"Yes."

"If I give you the card then it wasn't worth anything, it was evidence, and then I'll be the one who made it." He touched his pocket where the card sat. "It stays in my pocket. The name is Hardaker. You know that. I am saying it so you hear me say it in this house."

"I hear you," said Elias. "The card stays with you. The information moves and the document does not."

Wen Senn put the tea down and the cakes on a plate. Elias stayed ten minutes more, drank the tea standing up because there were only two chairs, and left at ten past ten with the notebook in his pocket and the cakes eaten down to two.

He did not follow Niko Senn to Concord Hall. He walked to the bus stop and stood there until the bus for the Verge Row side came, and got on it with his hands in his pockets.

The bus ran past the market with its shutters half up, past Halloway Street with its cold stores breathing white, past the chandler's below the flat where an old woman with a bad knee had put her hand flat on a table at seven in the evening on the sixteenth and breathed out on a tap. He sat on the right-hand side with his good ear to the engine and watched each street go past without getting off. The two dates sat in the notebook in his pocket and did not meet, and the not-meeting was the finding. A man cannot be in two rooms. A date cannot be in two books for the same reason.

He got off at Fallow Street at ten to eleven, bought tea from the stall on the corner, and carried it up the stairs above the tax office with the receipt from August still in his wallet beside the new one he was about to be given.

---

Neil Hardaker's room above the tax office in Fallow Street smelled of paper and floor polish. The price list was on the wall, forty credits the hour, the client book on the desk with the spine gone white, the consent form on top of it with the same four lines Elias had copied into the back of his notebook on the second of October.

Hardaker stood up when Elias came in at eleven.

"Mr Venn. Consult?"

"One question about your book," said Elias. "The sixteenth of September. You gave me two on-site dates on the third. The fourth of August and the sixteenth of September. I am asking about the second."

Hardaker opened the book without hesitation, turned to September, and ran his finger down.

The page held names in date order, premises beside each, reason beside that. Elias saw the fourth of August with its reason — *could not travel* — and then the sixteenth, in the same hand, with the same care. A man who keeps names because he is honest keeps them all the same way.

"Sixteenth of September," he read. "Halloway Street. Second-floor flat above a chandler's. Nineteen hundred to twenty hundred. Grounding session, Module Four. Person could not travel — bad knee, seventy-one. Brought the room to the person. Recorded premises, date, reason, as the module requires." He looked up. "No ward work. No drawing. No frame. Hand flat on a table, wait for a tap, breathe out, say a name. That is the whole of it."

Elias wrote it down: *16 Sep, Halloway, 19:00–20:00, could not travel, room brought to person. No ward work. — N. Hardaker, 18 Oct.*

"Did you take the brass clamp into that session?" said Elias, because he had to ask it once and be answered.

"No," said Hardaker. "I took a brass clamp out of a session with a frightened client in August, in this room, because he asked me to hold it. I have never taken anything into anybody's flat."

"Thank you."

Hardaker closed the book. "Mr Venn. I have turned down two referrals since August. I sent two of my own to the free service because I could not carry them. I believe what I did was a kindness and I think I am right. If the sixteenth is a date in your case, I would like to know, because I keep names in a book because I am honest and an honest book is a thing that can be read."

"The sixteenth is a date in my case and it is also a date in your book," said Elias, "and the two do not meet. That is what I came to find out. Your session was in Halloway Street at seven in the evening. The landing I am concerned with is in Kettle Rows. A man cannot be in two rooms."

Hardaker sat down slowly.

"Then I am glad you asked," he said. "And I am glad you wrote it in front of me."

Elias paid for the consult, took the receipt, and put it in his wallet with the other one from the third. Two receipts, two numbers, no address, no Concord registration. He was at the door when Hardaker spoke again.

"Mr Venn. The young man — Niko Senn. He paid forty credits and it worked, and he would like one thing in his life to have been worth forty credits. If anybody asks you whether he was taught anything in a flat on the sixteenth, he was not. He was in my room in August, four times, with his hand flat on my table."

"I know," said Elias. "I have it in his words and in yours."

He walked back down Fallow Street in the middle of the day with two dates in his notebook that did not connect, and understood that this was itself a finding. Niko Senn had stood on a third landing in Kettle Rows on a Sunday evening and heard a click he could not place. Neil Hardaker had sat at a table in Halloway Street at the same hour with an old woman who could not travel. Neither man was lying. Neither date explained the other.

At the shop Otto Vahl had a message on the counter in his own hand: *Rennick rang. Will attend Cutter St Friday ten o'clock. Bring nothing. — O.V., 12:40.*

Elias read it twice, wrote *Rennick Fri 19 Oct 10:00, bring nothing* in the notebook with the time, and stood for a while in the back room with the carrier bag under the window.

The rest of Thursday went to recovery, because four hours' sleep a night for a fortnight repays itself in the middle of the afternoon whether or not anybody has earned it. He ate lunch sitting down, which he had not done since Monday, and made tea in Otto's urn and drank it at the front bench with the radio off, because the shop was quiet enough that day to hear the yard gate. At half past three he went up and lay down for an hour with his boots on, and woke to rain on the sill and the cat on his feet. At four he mended a radio for Mrs Achterberg without charging. At five he walked to the clinic with a docket and stood in the corridor while Ahmed signed it and wrote the time in the book without being asked. On the stairs Mrs Achterberg stopped him with her shopping bag and said the top landing was still too quiet and that she was still listening for a kettle that was not there. He listened to the whole of it and said nothing about the decision he had made on Wednesday and written down every morning since, because there is no sentence a man can offer that which is not a promise about a room he has promised himself not to enter. He came home and wrote the day out full: *Niko asked, Hardaker asked, dates do not meet. Rennick Friday.*

Niko Senn had gone into a room at the back of Concord Hall at half past eleven that morning and had come out of it or not come out of it, and Elias did not telephone to find out. A man decides that for himself, and a telephone call from a stranger that afternoon is a way of being in a room the room does not know you are in. So he washed the two mugs, fed the cat, and sat on the stool at the back bench with the notebook shut and nothing to put in it, which was the first hour of quiet he had had since the eighth of October.
