# Chapter 0003 — The Margin

The Sequence Desk's temporary evidence room was a converted records store with a vent that had been failing since the previous winter, and Elias had learned to stand at the far end of it with his good ear toward the door.

His left ear had gone quiet in a stairwell in the small hours of Monday, the first time it had ever done that on him, and it had come back inside an hour and left him with the sense of a room that had moved and stayed moved. The hearing loss underneath that was older and was not going anywhere. He had not stopped sitting on the right side of people.

"Sit down, Venn."

Nadia Kest had a folder open on the table and a form on top of the folder, and the form was the one thing in the room she was not going to let go of until it was finished.

"Residency declaration," she said. "Abasi's arrived. It's good. It's also not filed, and an inspector's paper in a handbag is a rumour. This goes in the case file with a number on it, and after that it is not about you, it is about the case, and nobody can use it as a reason to throw anything out without answering for throwing out the reason."

"You want my address in the file."

"I want your address in the file," Nadia said, "in the place where a defence lawyer and a review panel and possibly a magistrate will find it in two years, instead of the place where a frightened inspector found it at nine o'clock in the morning."

Elias sat down and read the form. It ran to half a page. It asked when he had acquired his interest in the premises, what his tenancy was, who else had keys, whether he had any financial or familial connection to the deceased, and whether he had had any contact with the deceased outside the ordinary course of his work as a tenant in the building.

He answered all five. On the fourth he wrote *None*. On the fifth he wrote *Twice. I signed her tenancy transfer four years ago and held a light while her door ward was seated two years ago. Both are recorded on the building's work orders, which are in the file at the Sequence Desk under the address.*

"That last one," said Nadia, "is going to make your life harder."

"Yes."

"Then it's true."

He signed it. She read it, initialled the corner, and put the original in the folder and a photocopy in his hand, and then she did something she almost never did, which was to sit down on the table next to him instead of opposite him.

"Elias. I am going to say one thing and then I am going to leave it alone. You were in that room for nine seconds and you came out with a working that no instrument in this city can read. That is a wonderful gift. It is also the reason that nobody in this building is going to let you near a piece of paper without a witness, and if you fight that, you will spend the case losing."

"I am not going to fight it."

"You fought it at two this morning on a stranger's landing."

"That was not fighting. That was a woman on a stair at two o'clock in the morning and a door that was open when it should not have been."

"All right," Nadia said. "Then let us find out what is in it."

The case board had been assembled in the corner by Dace, who had a gift for it and no licence to touch it legally. Three columns. Physical. Residue. Paper.

**Physical.** A room rented for four years to a night-bus dispatcher with a stable night shift and a cat fed on Thursdays by a neighbour. No forced entry. A fight that stopped halfway through. A handprint with four fingers and no thumb, pressed hard and dragged. Nothing stolen.

**Residue.** Swab fourteen, lifted from the left edge of the desk at eleven o'clock, because the desk was the first thing the working touched. Swab fifteen, from the threshold, sealed at the scene and waiting for Saint Orra to tell them whether they are allowed to be useful at all. And a salt-glass plate at Saint Orra Reach, in the name of a forensic mage, with a seam on it that matched neither the victim nor the door.

**Paper.** A tenancy transfer from four years ago. A work order for a door ward from two years ago. A depot roster with four years of clean attendance. A building ward certificate expiring in March.

"That's it," said Nadia. "That's the whole case. It's the thinnest I've ever started. On a Wednesday, with a body, that would be a certificate and a press release."

"It is Monday."

"It is Monday, and Wednesday is on their schedule, and that is not a joke, Venn, that is the Registry's timetable talking to me through a schedule." She capped her pen. "Two things go on that board before you go near it, and they are both mine."

"Go on."

"Swab fifteen is not a sample yet, it is a thing in a bag in my car, and if the department's cold store eats it in a week then the department has decided what happened in that room and we will both be arguing about it from memory in a room full of men who were not there." She wrote it on a card and fixed it to the residue column. "And the second one is the plate. Doctor Quill lifted it herself, in a corridor, in front of two witnesses, and it is in her name, and if anybody in this building sends somebody to Saint Orra to collect it I will personally—"

"You will personally what."

"Write it down," said Nadia, "and put it in the file, which is the only weapon either of us has and which we are both extremely bad at."

"Where is the rest of the room?"

"Not lifted. Not until the residue lab says what is in it and the Registry says what is in *that*."

"Give me the swab."

Nadia did not hand it over. She took the sealed bag out of the drawer, put it on the steel table under the extractor, took out her phone, set it running, and then turned it so the camera could see the bag, the seal number and the wall clock.

"Swab fourteen," she said. "Seal four one one nine. Taken from the left edge of the desk of a deceased person in a room at this address, by Constable Dace, at eleven oh-four this morning, in the presence of Detective Sergeant Venn, for the purpose of establishing the order in which residue was laid down."

That was the formula. He had heard it a hundred times and had never once heard it used for this.

"That is not the purpose it was taken for," Elias said.

"No," said Nadia. "And I am about to write a second line. *Additional purpose: an order perception is sought from a witness with prior exposure. Witness is not an instrument and is not a trained examiner. The witness's perception is not evidence of cause, identity, or intent, and the sample is to be treated as compromised in the event of unrecorded contact.* Would you like to read it, or shall I read it aloud so that the phone hears you agreeing?"

Elias's ears had gone hot.

"Read it aloud," he said.

She did. He said, "Agreed," and his own voice sounded to him like a stranger's, and then he said, "Nadia, I am going to be honest with you about what this costs, because if I do not write it down now I will spend the next four days pretending I did not know."

"Go on."

"If I do this now, and I lose time, and the time I lose is the time I would have spent on the phone with the depot, or with Otto Vahl, or reading what a witness said to me at half past nine — then there is a hole in the case and the hole is mine, and the Registry will use the hole, and so will the owner of this building."

"Then the hole is logged," said Nadia, "and the hole is yours in the file instead of in your head. Which of those two would you rather have?"

He thought about the four minutes on the stair.

"The file," he said.

He washed his hands. He signed the bag. He logged his own name, the time, the purpose, and the fact that he was going in clean and had washed twice.

Then Nadia cut the seal with a blade on the camera, read the cut number out loud twice, resealed the bag, wrote the reseal on the corner of the form, and initialled it, and made him watch her do all of it, because a man who is about to put a hand on a sample should at least be able to say afterwards that the container was opened and closed on a record and not by him.

Then he laid his left hand flat on the swab itself, which is the only way a person in a records store can do what he was about to do.

He wanted to explain it to her first. He had been trying to explain it to somebody for eleven years and had never once been able to get past the first four sentences, and he had a sentence ready, and it was this: it is not a vision of people. Nobody appears. Nobody tells him anything. It is closer to walking into a room where someone has just put away the dishes and being able to tell that the plates came before the glasses, and that somebody's hand was already on the tap before the water started, and that the whole kitchen was assembled in one direction and then, at the very end, in another.

He said that instead, out loud, to Nadia, because she had earned it and because if the file was going to contain his gift, the file could at least contain an honest description of it. He told her the rest of it as well, because he had never once written any of it down, and the only two people in his life who had known it were a ward assistant called Hollis Ward, who had told him in his twenties that it was a rare sensitivity and not a destiny, and who had gone out of Elias's life eleven years ago without saying where he was going, and himself.

"Twelve seconds," he said. "If the sample is fresh and I do not fight it. Then dizziness, and I lose minutes, and I cannot hold a telephone conversation afterwards, and I do not remember the minutes. I can hold a pen. I think I can hold a pen."

"Twelve seconds," said Nadia, and wrote it down, and then, unexpectedly, put her hand flat on the table next to his, not touching the bag, and left it there for the length of a breath. "I will be here for the whole twelve seconds and for the ten minutes after it. Do it."

He closed his eyes.

It came in the way it always came, from the outside in: the extractor fan going from present to gone, and his own pulse going from something he could hear to something behind his eyes, and then the room.

Not a room. That was the wrong word, and it was the wrong word he had used for eleven years. There was no place in it and nobody in it. There was an order.

The working began at the desk. It bound itself to the desk — he could feel the way a working feels when it takes hold of a thing, which was like a sentence you have written and then cannot unsay — and it did not reach for the door. Not first. He had been wrong about that, and he could feel the wrongness now, the way you can feel a floor give.

The address went out and it did not narrow the way a door working narrows. It narrowed the way a hand narrows when it is looking for one person in a room, and then it turned, and it turned a *person* — a living thing, warm, standing there — and it did the turn to the warm thing and not to the chair or the door or the window, and Tamsin Ro's own small return-home working went off in the same instant like a dog barking at a stranger in a yard, four clean acts, tidy, stopping at the threshold exactly where Mara said it stopped.

And then there was a gap where her release was, and the gap was not empty.

The second trace had been in the desk before hers had finished. It had been laid in under her working like wire under a floorboard, and it had already chosen its man when she came home and put her bag down and put the kettle on.

He came out of it on the floor with his cheek against the tile, and Nadia was saying his name from a long way off, and somebody's hands were on his shoulders without shaking him.

He had been down for some time. He could tell because the extractor fan had come back and then gone again.

"What time," he said.

"Twenty past four. You've been—" Nadia stopped. "Elias, you were out for over three hours."

He got himself onto the stool. The room was the wrong width and had a wall where the window was, and the cold was in his teeth, and the left side of his head was gone. Not muffled. Gone. He turned to put his good ear at Nadia and found that he could not tell from the sound which side of the room she was on, and had to watch her mouth instead.

"That is new," he said.

"That is new," said Nadia, "and I do not know whether it comes back, and I am writing the time on it either way."

"Twelve seconds," he said.

"You were under my hands for forty minutes before that and you were answering me."

"You are telling me about it afterwards," he said, "which is the definition of afterwards."

She made him a sound that was almost a laugh and did not arrive. She put water in front of him and he could not pick it up. He sat with his hands on the table and looked at them, and they were shaking, and it was not the tremor of cold.

"It is there in the log," Nadia said. "I wrote down what you said and what you could not answer and the exact time you came back. Every gap is in the file, by the minute, and I wrote why."

"Write that it was mine to choose."

"I wrote the sentence before I knew it. Now drink the water."

He drank the water. He was aware, distantly, of the shape of the afternoon he had lost, and of how much of it was shaped like a hole, and of the fact that the reason he had a hole at all was that he had wanted a thing badly enough to do it while a police officer watched on camera and a seal number was on the bag, which was as close to proper as he had ever managed, and which had still cost him three hours.

Then he took out his phone and did the thing he had never once done in eleven years, which was to let somebody else's record stand in for his own.

A phone keeps a call log, and a call log does not care in the slightest that its owner has been lying on a floor. It said he had called the Orra depot at nine minutes to twelve and had spoken for four minutes about a rota, and had dialled the Saint Orra Reach switchboard at six minutes past twelve and hung up without speaking. It said nothing else at all until four minutes to four, when he dialled Otto Vahl's number and let it ring, and then again at twenty past four, and had got through on neither. Between a quarter past twelve and ten past three there was nothing at all, and there never would be anything at all again, and the nothing was three hours wide.

"That is a thing about me that I would like in the file," he said. "Not as feeling. As a gap with a width in it."

Nadia wrote it down, and then she wrote the three hours down again separately, because that was the procedure she had taught him in his first week and it was the reason he trusted her more than anybody else in the building.

"The desk is the anchor," he said. "Not the door. That is what I got wrong at two o'clock this morning and I want it in the file, because I told Abasi the wrong thing and she is going to act on the wrong thing. It binds to the desk and it goes to the door second, and her own working fires at the same time as the turn, and there is a second trace already under hers when it fires."

"Then how does the second release end up after hers?"

"Because hers is the small one," Elias said. "Ours is four acts and it stops at the threshold. Theirs has a longer way round. The two of them are not in a queue. They are laid on the desk at the same time and the second one is the longer one and it takes longer to finish, so it completes later. From the door, I read the end. From the desk, I read the beginning. Both are true. That is why I could not get hold of it in the corridor."

Nadia took it down in her own flat hand, all of it, and read it back to him, and then made a second copy because that was what a case file needed and not a memory.

"And the address," she said. "You said it narrowed like a hand looking for one person."

"Yes."

"Then it was not aimed at her."

"No."

"Then who is in that room, in the working, tonight, while you are sitting here doing arithmetic—"

"I do not know," said Elias. "And I am not going to guess. There is one address in that sequence and it is a person and it is warm, and that is the only fact I have, and the only person who can add to it is the one it was aimed at."

The phone on the table rang. Nadia looked at the screen.

"Kettle Rows station," she said. "They have found him."

"Who?"

"Niko Senn. Twenty-four. He lives in a walk-up on Verge Row, which is the outer ward, which is a long way from a stairwell in Kettle Rows at two in the morning." She read the rest. "He works nights. He was on the late shift at the Orra depot when we started looking, and he has been — they have been at his flat since four. He says he was at the building that night. He says he will not talk to the police, and he says, quote, he wants to talk to the woman who took his statement down at the hospital about a thing that is not about a door."

Elias sat very still on the stool, with the water he had not wanted in front of him, and understood that a man could be found and found innocent and still be the only person alive who had felt the thing that was done.

"Get him a lawyer," he said. "Get him Mara. Do not put him in a car, and do not let anybody from this desk go and stand in his doorway."

"Already done." Nadia was writing. "Elias. The Registry rang. There is a standard certificate form open on their system for this address, and it is a standard *self-directed working, single subject, closed within the dwelling* form, and they would like it countersigned before the hearing so that the hearing can be about the hearing."

"Before the residue is measured."

"Before the residue is measured," Nadia said. "They have written down what the room did and they have written it down already. Would you like to see the wording, or would you like to wait until the plate is measured and see whether the wording survives?"

Elias looked at the swab bag on the table, and at the seal, and at his own name in Nadia's handwriting, and at the clock on the wall that was going to be the only reliable clock in the room.

"Show me the wording now," he said, "and write down that I asked."
