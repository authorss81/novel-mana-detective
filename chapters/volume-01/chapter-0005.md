# Chapter 0005 — The Small Repair

The shop was called Venn Repairs by a man who had died in 2016, and Elias had kept the sign because it had been his mother's argument, not his, and because arguing with a dead woman about a sign is a habit he had never managed to break.

He had been in since eight. There was a queue of ordinary work, because Tuesday was the day the market traders came in with the machines that had failed on the weekend, and the machines that failed on the weekend were always machines that had been cheap, and the machines that had been cheap were always machines that could be fixed.

"Two hundred and ten," he said, to a woman with a drinks cooler that had started clicking. "Or I take the board out and it's a hundred and forty and it comes back in three days."

"Two hundred and ten," she said, and he did it in twenty minutes because he could, and she left, and he wrote it in the day book, and he did not eat.

The notebook was open on the bench. He had started it on Monday night with two columns and had been adding to the left one all day and the right one not at all.

Left: Tamsin's room, Kettle Rows, top floor, my own building.

The left column said: *ward on frame, starts in room, binds to desk, address is a person, turn lands on person, second trace under hers, second release finishes later; door ward click; 4 fingers no thumb; her mug struck, not dropped; times incompatible; corner man; two orders in one threshold.*

The right column said: *Morrow Street: no release was recorded at the threshold.*

He looked at the right column for longer than he should have and then wrote under it, in the same hand, *do not open the box,* and put the notebook away, and went upstairs to change his shirt, and came back down.

---

JA-4417.

That was the thread, and he had learned a long time ago that the thread is nearly always something dull: a stamped number, a serial, a name of a firm, a date. Criminals are romantic about their own organisation and careless about their procurement, and a case turns on a purchase order far more often than it turns on a confession.

He found the number in the tool registry first, because the registry was the only public place a number like that could exist.

The Kettle Rows Tool Registry occupied two shelves and a microfiche reader in the back office of the Ward Works district depot on Cutter Street, and it existed because every licensed ward tool in the city had to be traceable to a maker and a bench, on the theory that a tool that mends a ward might one day be the thing that broke one.

The clerk was a woman called Bess Yonkin who had worked there for nineteen years and had the specific hospitality of somebody who has watched a great many people come in wanting something they have not got.

"You're not the warrant," she said, before he had got his notebook out.

"No."

"Then you'll not have the dies."

"I'll have the ledger."

"The ledger's public. The ledger's a public register. I'll give you the ledger, I'll let you stand at that counter, and you can photograph the page, and that is the whole of what I have got for you this afternoon." She put her chin on her hand. "The dies are in the box and the box needs a Concord seizure notice with a case number on it, and if I hand you the box you will take forty photographs of a tool that a man stamped four years ago and you'll build a case on a machine, and I've seen that done twice and both times it was a waste of a morning."

Elias had a case number. He wrote it on his form and showed it to her.

"That's a number," she said.

"It's a case number for a death."

"Then you'll need the seal on it as well, and the seal's upstairs, and the man upstairs doesn't like being sent for things by people he's met once." She opened a drawer. "Page three hundred and eleven. Photograph it. Don't lean on the glass."

He photographed the page. Six entries for Jules Ardent, all in one hand-block, all stamped at Bramwell & Sons of Ash Street for the marking: JA-4412, JA-4413, JA-4414, JA-4415, JA-4416, JA-4417. *Portable ward clamp, c/w channel jaw, bench run of six, 4408 to 4417.* Beside 4417, in the same block: *to J. Ardent, 4408,* and then, later, in a different ink and a different, less careful hand: *4417 issued 4417 — J.A. bench.*

"Issued," Elias said.

"Issued," said Bess Yonkin. "Out of the maker's bench. The rest of that run is in the district. 4413 works on the escalator at the Orra interchange. 4415 has been in a stairwell in Kettle Rows since the spring, and if you go and look at it you will not be able to tell it from a torch."

"And 4417?"

"Never came back." She said it flatly. "That's a maker's record, not a police record. The registry doesn't follow them out. If he wanted it tracked he should have written a bench number in the box, and he did that on two of them and not on that one, and that is exactly the sort of thing that costs a man his certificate and I have told him so for four years."

---

Shop one was nine Pell Street, a first-floor room over a dry cleaner, and it belonged to Jules Ardent, who was twenty-nine and was out.

The door was not locked. It was a workshop door: locked against theft, not against honesty. Elias did not go in and read the bench book. That had been the old him, and the old him would have gone in and would have been right about the contents, and the new him stood on a landing and waited forty minutes in the cold for a man to come home, and then asked him for the book.

Jules Ardent came at four in the afternoon with a transit hi-vis under a jacket and a paper cup of something that was not coffee. He was a small careful man with cuticles gone and a way of holding a door open for you while deciding whether to let you past it.

"Venn." He did not sound surprised. "It's been on the news. Top floor."

"May I look at the bench book?"

"That is not a question people ask me."

"Yes it is," Elias said. "It is the question people ask when they have decided not to do the other thing."

Jules let him in.

The bench was immaculate. There was a lathe under a cover, a pegboard with hand tools in drawn outlines, a half-finished bracket in brass clamped in a vice, and a small shrine of practice pieces to one side that Elias understood to be a mechanic's equivalent of a police officer's box of memories: every wrong cut kept because throwing it away would be a lie.

The bench book was on the bench, open, and it was a better record than any of Elias's own notebooks, because Jules had written it for an inspector who might one day arrive, and the whole of his life was organised around that possible arrival.

Elias did not touch it. He read it standing up, from the doorway, and he photographed every page, and when Jules came and stood beside him and looked at what he was photographing, Elias said, "Read it with me. Point at the entries."

Jules pointed.

"Fourteenth," he said. "That's the one. Second-hand transit, bench run of six. And then the seventeenth of last month, and there it is. 4417 out, to a fella called Rennick, on a card from a clinic."

"What clinic?"

"Rehabilitation. I didn't see the card, I saw his copy of the number off the top." Jules turned the page. "He said a threshold ward needed reseating before the winter, which is nonsense, because a threshold ward is a door frame and I don't do doors, I do circuits. And he offered me eighty, and the clamp's worth about forty, and the bench time's worth nothing on a Tuesday."

"Did you sign for it?"

"No."

"Jules."

"He wasn't there to be lied to and I wasn't there to tell him the truth." Jules closed the book. "I handed it over in the doorway of my own shop and I felt bad about it for four days and then I felt fine about it for two hours, and that's the whole of my involvement and I would like to go back to being asked about a case now."

Elias looked at the photograph on the phone in his own hand, and felt something cold and clean move through him, and had to be careful for a moment not to let it show, because what it was — what it actually was — was the feeling of a case coming apart into pieces he could hold.

Two of six clamps in the district, documented. One missing. A stamped number. A false clinic card. A bench book that said *this is what I did* in a stranger's careful hand.

He had been twelve steps in front of Jules Ardent for nine hours, and now he was going to be twenty, and there was nothing in that sentence that was about ward mechanics.

"Jules," he said. "I want the clamp."

"No."

"Detective—"

"It's mine, it's a licensed tool, and the day I hand it to the police it comes back in a plastic bag with a property number on it and I never see it again without a lawyer and a form." His hands had gone flat on the bench, the way hands do. "And you know that's true, because you've seen that box. It's the same box every time."

"Yes. That's true."

"Then get a custody slip and I'll sign it."

"You haven't got one."

"I'll have one at four tomorrow morning."

Elias stood in the middle of a stranger's workshop and discovered that he had absolutely no idea who he was. He had walked into that workshop with a thread and a plan, and the plan had ended eleven minutes in, at a bench book, and everything since had been a man being reasonable with him, and there was no case here at all. There was a mechanic in trouble and a beautiful fact about a number.

"Four tomorrow," he said. "I'll have a woman from Saint Orra Reach here and a police signature on the transfer, and it will be documented rather than seized, because I am not going to put your tool in an evidence locker and lose it in a year."

"Why would you do it your way?"

"Because in about eleven months somebody with a better reason than mine is going to ask for that box, and I would rather your bench book said *transferred to Quill, residue laboratory, witnessed* than *seized and subsequently released*."

Jules Ardent looked at him for a while.

"Four tomorrow," he said. "Bring her. Not him, her. And I'm going to be in my own shop when you do it, and I'm going to photograph my own bench, and you can photograph that too."

---

He walked back up Tanager Street with the cold coming off the river and understood, about a third of the way, what had happened to him that afternoon.

He had gone into that workshop to use a man. He had a number and he had carried it there and he had worked a witness's testimony into it, and the satisfaction he had felt at page seventeen was not the satisfaction of justice. It was the satisfaction of a man who has found a person in a bed of nails and is about to use him.

That was the thing about the gift. The Margin was the only thing he had that nobody could check, and the temptation was never to *see* something impossible. The temptation was to see something and be certain of it, and then to be certain of it out loud, and then to find the person who agreed with him and treat that person as *the reason*. He had built half this case on Otto Vahl's cat and Nadia's arrest. He had watched Mara Quill lift a plate in a corridor and had felt *ahead*, and ahead was the thing he wanted, because ahead meant he did not have to be one of the people in the room who was wrong.

He stopped under the awning of the closed market and let a bus go past, and then he took out the phone and put in the number of Saint Orra Reach and asked to be put through to the residue laboratory, and when Mara picked up he did not say hello.

"Mara. I need a custody line and a signature at four tomorrow morning in a workshop in Kettle Rows, and I need to tell you why, and you are going to say no to about half of it."

---

The third ledger was the one that mattered, and he got it legally and slowly at nine that night. Licensed clinical premises keep a filing copy on site for inspection, and there is a counter, and a bell, and a visitor book, and he had to walk past the address twice before he was sure of it, and then he had to stand at a counter in a corridor and ask a receptionist to bring him the current year's intake filing copy out of the back, in front of her, and explain what he wanted it for, and hear her explain that she was required to write down the name of anybody who asked.

A rehabilitation clinic on Verge Row. Licensed, current, and — this was the part that made him stand in the doorway for a while with his coat in his hand — its intake book ran from the start of the year and had ninety-one names in it, and every one of the ninety-one had a card number, and every one of the ninety-one had a treatment on the schedule.

There was no account on its pages. There was no patient who matched a name on the card that Jules Ardent had photographed and posted to a police sergeant in a doorway, and there was no entry anywhere in the book for a card that the name on it could be photographed from.

The book ran, column by column, name, card, treatment, signature. Ninety-one rows. In the eleventh row, the name was a spelling of a different name that the Registry's own index would have caught in 2019, and in the fortieth row the treatment column said *follow-up* for a patient whose first attendance was not in the book, and in the eighty-eighth row the signature was a printed signature.

And there was no room at that address for a person who had been made to practise opening a door.

He photographed the page in the corridor, standing up, with the bell within reach of the receptionist's hand and no pretence at all that he was doing anything else. Then he wrote his own name and the time in the visitor book, in her ink, with her pen, because she asked him to and because he has spent nine days in a building of his own where nobody wrote anything down. He noticed the shape of his own signature going onto a stranger's page and had the distinct sensation of a man who has just put a future prosecutor's index finger on a piece of paper.

Then he stood in the wet outside on Verge Row for a while and let the fact settle, which was this: if that book was the record, then the record had been in a corridor on Verge Row all along, in a drawer a woman had to unlock, and the only reason nobody had ever read it was that nobody had ever asked.

Mara's voice came down the phone, flat with sleep and interest both.

"You've found a clinic."

"I've found an address," Elias said. "And a clamp. And a bench book. And a stamped number on two of them and a missing one on the sixth."

"And?"

"The clamp cannot be the thing that killed her." He heard how tired he was, and heard the tiredness leak into the sentence, and made himself put the next part in anyway. "Jules says a clamp holds a circuit open. It doesn't hold a working. If somebody has cut something to match the channel in his jaw, then they used his *drawing*, not his tool, and his tool is a piece of brass that has been in and out of stairwells for four years."

There was a short silence on the line.

"Then it is a carrier," said Mara, "and not a source."

"Yes."

"And a carrier needs something to be carrying." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. "Elias, do you understand what you have just said to me. If that clamp is only a carrier, then the thing being carried is Tamsin Ro's own working, and the working that killed her is her own, and there is a copy of it somewhere in this city, and a copy of a person's working does not come out of a stairwell brass tool. It comes out of a person, or out of a thing a person is very attached to. And it cannot be got by anybody who has not met her."

Elias stood in the dark on Verge Row with the clinic's back stair going down past his shoulder.

"Four tomorrow morning," he said. "Be at the shop."

"I'll be there." Then, after a moment: "Elias. Thank you for calling me before you called Nadia."

"I called you first because you are the one who will tell me I am wrong while it is still cheap."

"Yes," said Mara. "That is unfortunately correct."
