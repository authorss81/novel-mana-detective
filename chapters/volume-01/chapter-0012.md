# Chapter 0012 — The Slip in the Tray

The room was above a licensing office on Vine Street and it had a fan heater in the corner and a bench along one wall with a torn vinyl cushion and a jug of water that had been filled on Thursday.

Magistrate Hadwin Slade had a wig on a table in front of him rather than on his head, and a file in front of the wig, and he had been reading since before she came in, which she had accounted for by being eleven minutes early.

"Sit down, Inspector. Coat on the back of the chair, not the bench. I have had people on that bench for nine years and I have never once wanted anything off it."

Nadia Kest sat down on the chair.

He turned the file round. It was four pages, and the top page was hers, written out in her own hand at about ten past twelve, and underneath it was the endorsement she had spoken into a telephone at twenty-three forty-one on Friday night while sitting in the back of a car on Marshall Street.

"Read the second page," he said. "Out loud. I want to hear whether you wrote it at midnight or afterwards."

She read it. It was in his clerk's hand, not hers.

"Endorsement granted by telephone, out-of-hours, at 23:41, on the application of Detective Inspector N. Kest, Veyra Civic Police, Serious Crimes Unit, for entry to a dispensary stockroom at a community clinic in Kettle Rows, on the stated grounds that a device in the room was conducting a working against a named patient in an adjoining room. The applicant stated that she had not entered, had not opened the door, and held two officers at a second door. The applicant further stated that the named patient was Niko Senn, twenty-four, under a clinical hold."

"Correct."

"That is not what I asked you to read." He put his finger on the third paragraph. "This."

"Paragraph four," she said. "Read it to me."

"I have read it four times. You may read it to me if it helps you to hear it."

So she read it.

*4. It is recorded that the application for the above endorsement was made by telephone at 23:41 and that entry was effected at 23:54, a period of thirteen minutes. It is recorded that no application for a warrant was at that time filed with the court. No finding is made as to whether the period was properly employed. This minute is drawn to the attention of the officer named above and is to be read with any application subsequently made. Hadwin Slade, JCM, 22 September.*

Nadia Kest read it twice and then stopped, because there was nothing in it she could have done better and she had known that on Friday night at twenty-three twenty-nine, and there was no version in which she was going to hear it for the first time at eight on a Saturday morning.

"You told me on the telephone that I would be annoyed with you," said Slade.

"I did, and I was accurate, and I said it before I did the thing rather than after, which I understand is not the same as apologising in advance."

"It is not." He took his glasses off. "Inspector, let me be exact, because I am going to be asked to be exact by somebody in about four years. At twenty-three twenty-nine last night a magistrate in this building received a telephone call from a senior police officer in a car, and the request was not refused and it was not delayed, because there was a named patient two rooms above a locked door and a person on the line who could hear that she was frightened. I granted it. I would grant it again."

"Thank you."

"You have not thanked me for the fourth paragraph."

"I was not going to thank you for the fourth paragraph," Nadia said. "I am going to answer it, and then I am going to ask you a favour, and the favour is larger than the paragraph."

---

"The question you are going to ask me," said Slade, "is whether thirteen minutes is a lot."

"The question I am going to ask you is what the thirteen minutes were, and I am not going to answer it, because I was not in the room and neither were your two officers, and the man who was in the room has a faculty that is not evidence."

"Then what did you authorise?"

It was a fair question and it was the whole of the last four days in one sentence, and she had known it was coming since about two o'clock that morning, lying awake in a chair, and she had gone over the answer and it had been bad every time.

"I authorised an entry," Nadia Kest said, "on the telephone, at twenty-three forty-one, into a dispensary stockroom, on the word of a man who can see something he cannot prove. I did it because there were nine people in that building, and because one of them was twenty-four and had a fever of thirty-seven eight, and because the light over the dispensary counter had gone out four times in three hours and the fourth time was longer. I would like it recorded that those are the grounds and that none of them is a legal ground."

Slade wrote for a while.

"That is a very bad answer," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"It is also an honest one, which in my experience is the only kind worth writing down." He put the pen down. "Now your favour."

"Paragraph four says it is to be read with any application subsequently made. I am going to make an application on Monday morning, in daylight, on notice, for a warrant to a different address — the private premises on Carrow Lane — and I am going to make it because of a thing I found on a piece of paper on Wednesday and have not been able to put down since, and which is not connected to your midnight in any way that I can prove, and which I am not going to tell you about at eight o'clock on a Saturday morning."

"You have just told me you have a thing you cannot put down and asked me for nothing in exchange."

"Yes, sir."

"All right." He turned to a fresh page. "I will hear the application on Monday. I will not hear it early, and if I hear it at nine o'clock on Monday morning I shall object to it, and you shall not be surprised. What I will do is leave paragraph four where it is, and I will not amend it, and I will not read it out in a way that helps you. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"You are telling me that you are going to be scrupulous and that being scrupulous will not help me."

"That is the first sentence anybody in your department has ever said to me in this room that made any sense at all," said Slade. "Go and eat something. You look worse than the man you are defending."

---

She was in the car at twenty past nine with the entry under her coat and she sat there for a while with the engine off, because the piece of paper changed the shape of everything and she needed four minutes to find out what shape.

The paper did two things.

The first was the obvious one. The entry existed, stamped, with a time on it, and it was a lawful document, and on Monday morning a warrant application for a different address would be heard by a man who had already written down, in his own hand, that a police officer had gone into a room in thirteen minutes without having filed anything. She had survived the thing. She had not survived it cheaply, and the price was not a fine and was not a complaint, it was a sentence in a magistrate's minute with her name in it, and that sentence would be read in four years by a defence solicitor who was looking for a way to say that the Sequence Desk did things.

The second was worse, and it was this: that minute put a number in the world. Thirteen minutes. And there was a working in that room that had begun at some hour nobody could name and ended at twenty-three fifty-three when nine inches of galvanised steel came off a brick, and there were forty minutes of that night that a man could not account for and could only date from a transit log, and a man who could not account for forty minutes of the same thirteen minutes.

If anybody ever compared those two numbers, the arithmetic would look very bad, and the arithmetic would be a coincidence, and the coincidence would be the only thing in the case that a competent person could attack.

So she wrote the file note before she started the car.

*Saturday 22 September, 09:26. The above minute records an interval of thirteen minutes between telephone endorsement and entry at Marshall Street. D/Sgt Venn has recorded a separate unaccounted interval of approximately forty minutes on the same evening. Inspector's assessment: the two intervals are unrelated, the second being a personal cost of a documented reading which the officer has reported in four places and which the assigned inspector has declined to rely on. Inspector records the assessment in advance so that the two figures may not later be confused, and accepts that a coincidence in arithmetic is not a defence to anything. N. Kest.*

Then she drove to the office.

---

The routing slip was in the second tray where it had been since Thursday morning, and it was where she had put it, which was the part of this she would have to live with.

*For attention re: cert. schedule, Morrow Hill. A. Orr, D/Chief — the Morrow Street sequence position, by close of Friday.*

She had read it twice on Thursday, put it in the tray, taken it out of the tray, and put it in her own file. And on Friday it had been in her own file, which is a place things go to be thought about later, and Friday had gone by at twenty to five in a corridor, and Saturday was now, and there was a place in the file where a thing should have been and was not.

It had been a request on Thursday. It was not a request any more, because on Friday afternoon somebody had put an annex sheet under it, and the annex was typed, and the annex had the Deputy Chief's office heading on it, and the annex asked for three things.

*1. The form category used in the Morrow Street report for an act entered after a turn.*
*2. The box number of that category in the current concordance of sequence-box codes.*
*3. The office which completed the box.*

Nadia Kest read item three four times.

Item three was a question that could only be answered by opening a file, and the file was a certified record from twelve years ago in a Tribunal archive on the far side of Morrow Hill, and every person who could answer it was either dead or retired or had a reason to give an answer that suited somebody.

And item two — the box number — she could not answer either, and she had realised that at about half past seven that morning in a car outside a licensing office, and it had not been a surprise, because she had spent Friday afternoon in a laboratory listening to a forensic mage explain that a box with four printed lines in it is a category on a form, and that a category is a line item, and that line items are printed so that a printer can order the right form.

She went through it in order, in her own head, the way she would have to go through it in writing.

She could send the annex back unanswered. That was available. It would be read as a police department deciding that a form category is a secret, which is not true, and it would be read that way by somebody who wanted it to be read that way, and it would create a fact where none existed.

She could answer items one and two and refuse item three. That was worse. It would be an admission that the category matters, from a department that has never said it does, in a document that goes up a chain.

Or she could write the position as a department, in the department's voice, and attach the annex as *received and referred*, which is what happens to a question that is not answered in the document that asked it.

She took a clean sheet out of the tray and wrote four hundred words.

*The Morrow Street matter: sequence position.*

*1. The record certified in that matter attributes a ward collapse of 2017 to the final responsible working of a named maintenance fitter, and the attribution is disputed and has been for twelve years. Twenty-three persons died. No dangerous-working finding was made at the time.*

*2. The Sequence Desk holds no certified sequence for the disputed act. The Desk's position is that the certified account is the strongest available account and is not, in the Desk's view, complete.*

*3. The Desk's position is unaffected by the connected Kettle Rows matter, in which the assigned detective has declared a prior interest in a document he has not produced, and which is recorded in the connected file.*

*4. The Desk is unable to answer the annex. A form category is a publishing convention of the Proof Registry and is not a matter of sequence. A departmental position on a publishing convention of a certifying authority is not a position a department should take.*

*5. This matter should be addressed by way of a formal file request to the Tribunal archive, made by the Deputy Chief, on the Deputy Chief's authority.*

She read it five times, which was two more than she needed, and signed it, and dated it, and put *N. Kest, Detective Inspector, Sequence Desk* at the bottom, and sent it up.

Item five was the true sentence and item four was the excuse and both of them were true at the same time, and she had put both of them in writing, which is the difference between a decision and a piece of thinking-out-loud.

Then she sat with the annex for another eleven minutes, and at some point in the middle of them she admitted to herself, in the plain flat language she used on herself when nobody was in the room, that item four was not an excuse.

If the category went into a departmental position, then the Kettle Rows file acquired a second matter attached to it, and a detective with two matters is a detective with a divided interest, and a divided interest is a thing an advocate finds on a Tuesday afternoon for the price of a phone call. She was not protecting a secret. She was protecting a file, and the person in the file, and the only way she knew to protect both of them was to make the question somebody else's.

And she was not going to tell Elias Venn that on Saturday, because he was nine minutes' walk from a clinic full of people who had been in the dark, and because he had a hearing in the morning, and because there was a version of her that would have made a joke about it and a version that would have said the truth and both of them would have been a way of putting it on him at a moment when he had nothing to put it on anybody else.

She initialled the annex *received and referred, 22 September, N. Kest*, and clipped it to the front of the file, and did not send it.

---

The reply came at four o'clock from the Deputy Chief's office and it was three lines and it was signed by the assistant who had been an assistant for eleven years.

*Noted. The file position is recorded. The Tribunal archive has been asked to prepare a full sequence position for the Morrow Street matter for the Deputy Chief's office, on the Deputy Chief's authority, and will report by Friday the fifth of October.*

Nadia Kest read it twice.

Somebody was going to open the Morrow Street file. Not him. Not her. A clerk in an archive on the far side of Morrow Hill, with a request on paper with a Deputy Chief's authority on it, preparing a full sequence position for a man who wanted a summary by close of Friday and had not had it.

He had wanted it by Friday because Friday was a date on a form. He would get it in eleven days because Friday was a date on a form and nobody in the chain of command had ever asked whether a date should be a date about a person.

She wrote the reply into the file in full, and then she took a photocopy of the countersigned routing slip and the annex and the reply, three pages, and put them in a folder, and wrote on the folder *441-KR — disclosed to assigned detective, 22 September, before the 24th*, and initialled it, and signed the time.

Then she went down to the ground floor and made Priya Sandoval hold the folder up so that Elias Venn could see her sign it in front of a witness, which was not procedure, and which she did anyway.

"You have got until Monday night," she said. "Not because I trust you with it. Because a man who is told on Saturday and reads it on Sunday morning in front of a registrar has had the whole weekend to be angry about it, and I would rather be the one who has to look at you."
