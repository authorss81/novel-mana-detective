# Chapter 0032 — Behind the Counter

The shop telephone rang at seven on Tuesday morning and Elias Venn answered it in the back room with the notebook already open, because a man with no file learns to sleep with a pen.

It was Ahmed from the clinic, to say the board had been looked at once more at half past six by a man who stood eleven feet away with his hands in his pockets and did not touch anything, and that the ninety-odd copied pages were curling at the corners because the clinic keeps the heating off in the mornings and had told the caretaker three times. Elias thanked him, wrote *board looked at, 16 Oct 06:30, touched nothing* under yesterday's lines, and put the kettle on.

Otto Vahl came through at eight with the radio bench's lamp in his hand and the cat following him like an apprentice.

"Assessor in the morning," said Otto. "Drawer still sticks."

"It sticks," said Elias. They had agreed on this the way men agree on a thing that is both true and useful. The drawer did stick, because Otto had wedged a sliver of wood into the runner on Sunday night after the assessor's clerk had gone. Twenty years of carbon slips sat in it — fuse wire, elements, flex — and none of them would help a man looking for channel section, but the fact of being looked at would cost a morning and a morning was a thing the shop could not spare.

He worked the bench till half past nine, closed a kettle, logged it, and took the stamped copy of CS-3 out of the folio long enough to check the reference line once more against the notebook. He did not take the whole bundle. The carrier bag stayed on the bench under the window where it had been since before the telephone in this shop started ringing, with the cat forbidden from it by an agreement the cat observed when it was being watched.

Niko Senn's name sat on the page from yesterday with the line under it. Elias read it twice before he left, the way he read the prohibition sentence before any laboratory, and then he locked the back room, told Otto he would be back by dinner, and walked to Cutter Street in rain that came and went.

Fenwick & Marsh kept their counter on the ground floor of 12 Cutter Street, in a room with a high window and a draught that moved the carbon slips on the spike. The board on the wall said *Draughtsmen. Estimates. Records kept.* Under it, in a smaller hand, *Registers not open to general inspection.*

Elias Venn stood in the queue at ten o'clock on Tuesday morning with the stamped copy of CS-3 in his inside pocket and the miscellaneous receipt stub in his wallet, three people ahead of him and a builder asking for a stair drawing from 2024 with no job number to ask under.

The builder paid and took the copy. Elias had watched the whole of it because there was nothing else to watch, and because a counter is a place where competence shows. She did not hurry, did not sigh, did not make the queue feel like a queue. She turned pages with a bone folder so the paper would not tear, wrote the number on a slip in pencil, and filed the slip before she handed the copy over. Eight years at a counter teaches a person that the book outlives everybody who asks for it.

When his turn came he laid nothing on the counter. He had learned at Bramwell's and at the trading-names counter and at the forms counter that a man who puts paper down has started an argument about the paper.

"Good morning," he said. "I am a member of the public with a stamped copy of a produced drawing. I would like an entry in your register read in place."

Imogen Draper looked at him. She was about fifty, with pencil marks on the side of her right hand and the particular patience of a person who has been asked for things by people who do not know what they are asking for, for eight years.

"Reference," she said.

"DRG HW-CS-03, Sheet one of two, Issue B."

She wrote it down without reaching for anything behind her.

"Sheet one of two," she said. "Issued thirtieth of September. You have a stamped copy of a production. Show me the stamp."

He took the stamped copy out of his pocket, held it at arm's length with both hands visible, and turned the front sheet so she could see the *copy* stamp on it without it leaving his hands. She read it standing back, the way he had read the Halloway door schedule standing back, and nodded once.

"That is a copy of a produced document," she said. "It is not your drawing and it is not our drawing. It is a copy the Registry gave you because you are a party."

"Yes."

"Then I am going to tell you what I tell everybody, and I am going to tell it to you once, because you look like a man who will write it down." She put her pencil down. "The register behind this counter is a third party's record. It is kept for makers who pay for drawings. Reference, sheet, issue, who drew it, when. It opens on a case number from an office that holds the matter, or on the written authority of the maker who paid for it. It does not open on a stamped copy, however stamped, and it does not open on a member of the public, however polite."

Elias wrote it down in the notebook on the counter, with the time, 10:14, and read it back to her.

"On a case number or a maker's written authority. Not on a stamped copy."

"That is right." She picked the pencil up. "And I am going to tell you the second thing before you ask it, because everybody asks it. I can confirm that an Issue A and an Issue B under that reference exist. I cannot read you either of them, and I cannot tell you who drew them, and I cannot tell you why they were drawn. A register says who drew a sheet. It does not say why, and if you want why you are in the wrong building."

He closed the notebook. The queue behind him shifted.

"Is there a form for the request?" he said.

"There is no form for being refused," said Imogen Draper, and for the first time something like humour crossed her face. "There is a form for being opened. The officer writes his number on it, the keeper signs it, the entry is read in place and photographed in place. Nothing leaves. If two things have to appear in one frame, the camera moves, not the things. That is the whole of it."

"Thank you," said Elias. "That is the map."

She had found the builder's drawing in four minutes, and the builder had gone out lighter than he had come in, and Elias had stood in that queue for twenty minutes learning the only thing a queue can teach anybody: that a person who is good at a counter says the same three sentences to every stranger in the queue, and not one of them is a different sentence.

Elias put the stamped copy back in his pocket without it having touched the counter. He had been in the room twenty minutes.

Outside on Cutter Street he stood for a while with his back to a shuttered chandler's and wrote three more lines under the morning's entry.

*Refused correctly, 16 Oct, 10:14, I. Draper. Register is third party's record. Opens on case number or maker's authority. Confirms A and B exist, reads neither.*

*Form: officer's number, keeper's signature, read in place, photographed in place.*

*Niko Senn — will not borrow standing. Reason written here so I do not forget it at a counter.*

A bus went past with rain on its windows. October had turned in the night and the street smelled of wet paper.

He did not go straight back. He walked the long way round by the market, past the stall where Mavis Brannigan had sold fish for eleven years before a cold room in Halloway Street took her, past the letting office with its file of cards, past the chandler's below the flat where Neil Hardaker had sat on the sixteenth of September with an old woman who could not travel. The city laid itself out in premises and dates. Every room had a book. Every book had a keeper. The keepers were not villains. They were people who wrote things down so the next person would not have to guess.

He stopped at a tea stall, bought tea he did not want because the woman had remembered him from September, and drank it standing up with the notebook in his pocket and the rain in his face. Niko Senn's name sat on the page with the line under it. He had written it so he would not forget it at a counter, and the counter had come and gone and he had not forgotten. That was the whole of the morning's victory, and it was not small.

He walked back to Kettle Rows by way of Fallow Street, past the tax office with the second-floor room above it where Neil Hardaker kept a client book with two on-site dates in it, and did not go up. The second date was the sixteenth of September and nobody had asked about it yet, and Tuesday morning in a queue was not the place.

In the shop Otto Vahl had the Factors drawer open with a screwdriver in it.

"It sticks," said Otto. "I told the assessor it sticks. He said he will come back in the morning with a schedule."

Elias looked at the drawer. The carbon book was in it, the statements filed behind with a bulldog clip, twenty years of monthly slips in a hand that had changed twice when the Factors changed clerks.

"Leave it sticking," said Elias. "If he brings a schedule, he brings it to me first."

He went through to the back room, put the notebook on the shelf, and stood looking at the carrier bag for a while. The stamped copy was back in the folio. The register was behind a counter where it belonged. A competent woman had refused him once, correctly, in the exact words he would have used if he had been behind that counter himself.

He took the notebook down again and wrote at the foot of the page: *On what document does an officer open a register for a man who is not an officer? Ask Nadia. As a favour. Pay for it.*

The afternoon went to ordinary work because ordinary work was the only thing that would have him. He rewired a plug, tested three elements, turned away a man who wanted a ward cooker mended without a certificate. At three the rain came hard and the shop filled with the smell of wet coats, and Mrs Achterberg came down from the fourth floor for a fuse and stayed for tea in the back doorway, looking at the carrier bag without asking about it.

"Top landing's quiet," she said. "Too quiet. I keep listening for a kettle that isn't there."

Elias made her tea in Otto's urn and did not ask what she meant. Tamsin Ro's room stood empty two floors above them with its door shut and its ward cold, and the frame he had read three times in his life sat behind that door with nothing new to tell him. He had decided on Wednesday last not to go up and put a hand on it, and the decision held because he wrote it down every morning: *do not go up. Fourth reading would be breach without consent.*

At five he walked to the Kettle Rows station with a docket to file about a customer's radio, and Priya Sandoval at the counter told him the woman from yesterday had been back, satisfied that the four sheets were not a police notice, and had gone away. He thanked her, came back through rain that had stopped as suddenly as it started, and ate in the back room with the door open so the cat could choose.

Niko Senn's appointment sat on the page for Thursday morning at half past eleven, two days off, moved from the board morning because of the board. Elias wrote beside it: *do not follow to Concord Hall. Asked in house, answered in house.* Then he closed the notebook, put it on the shelf, and slept upstairs from ten till four with the window open on the yard, because a man who is going to ask a favour of an inspector needs to have slept.

The cat came in, stepped round the bag, and sat on the folio with the air of a creature that had decided the warmest place in the room was paper.

He moved the cat gently off the folio and onto the blanket, and the cat forgave him by going to sleep at once.
