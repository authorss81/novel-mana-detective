# Chapter 0001 — The Door That Opened Twice

The stairwell ward light on the second landing was late.

That was all it was, at first. A small brass-cased light on a bracket at knee height, wired into the building's ward bus, licensed four years ago to keep the treads from turning slippery and to catch a person who came up too fast in the dark. It had been late before. Elias had replaced the ballast himself in the spring, and the contractor who owned the certificate had never come back to sign off on the work, so the light still ran on a setting that drifted about a second and a half behind the clock.

Tonight it was late by four.

He stood at the bottom of his own stairs with his coat over his arm and his key still in the lock of the shop behind him, and he watched the light come up while the kitchen clock in the room above his own apartment said fifty-two minutes past one. It did not flicker. It did not surge. It simply arrived late, the way a man arrives to a shift he has already decided not to bother with.

The coffee had gone cold in his hand some time ago. He had been carrying it up three flights with the intention of drinking it, which was the sort of thing he did instead of eating.

"You've been standing there a while," said a voice above him.

He looked up. Mrs. Achterberg was on the fourth floor in a dressing gown with her cardigan over one arm, and she had the particular stillness of a woman who has decided she is not going to be surprised by anything tonight.

"Sorry," he said. "Did I wake you?"

"You were making a noise with the shutter."

"I was making a noise with the shutter."

"You always make a noise with the shutter." She came down four steps and stopped, one hand on the rail. "Tamsin's door's open."

Elias put the cold cup on the step behind him. "Which one."

"The top one. The room at the end of the landing. It's been open since I came down." She looked at him. "She's on shift. She's been on shift since eleven. If somebody's in that room, they didn't use the front stair."

It was the correct thought. It was also, he understood a half-second later, the thought a person reaches for before looking at anything. He had been the nearest Sequence Desk sergeant for six years because he was never the furthest. He lived on this site, in this building, under this stair, and had done since he was nineteen and repairing radiators for a man who took cash.

"I'll go up," he said. "Don't come with me. If it is somebody in there, the stair is a bad place to be."

"I wasn't going to come with you," said Mrs. Achterberg. "I came down to tell you. Nobody in this building wants a fuss tonight."

That was true enough, and it was also the reason she had come down, and he did not press on it. He took his torch from the shop and went up his own stairs, past his own door, and up two more flights to the top.

The room was at the end of the landing, and its door stood open the width of a hand.

He knew the room the way he knew the rest of the building. Tamsin Ro had lived there four years. She was a night-bus dispatcher for the Orra depot, and the deposit slip on the door frame said so, and he had signed the transfer himself when the previous tenant left, because the shop was the only business in the building and people asked him to look at forms. He knew that her hours ran backward. He knew the light she left on for the stair. He knew she kept the kettle on the sill because the window leaks when the wind is off the river.

He stood to one side of the opening and looked in.

The first thing was the smell. Not the smell of blood, which he had hoped for in the stupid way that a person hopes for a thing that will at least be simple. Cold tea and hot dust. Burned hair. A trace of scorched salt, the way a working smells when it has been pushed too fast through a current that had nothing left to give.

The second thing was the chair, over, and the mug, out, and the timetable on the floor with the corner folded back on itself.

The third thing was that the room was not in the order a fight leaves.

He shone the torch across the floor without stepping over the sill. Boots, one print clean, one print smeared, both pointing in. He had a smear of handprint on the desk that was not her hand — too broad, four fingers, no thumb, pressed flat and dragged. The desk chair had been shoved back hard enough to mark the paper. The window was shut. The bed had not been sat on. The little electric kettle on the sill had boiled dry and switched itself off, and the plug socket above it was dark.

And the door was the thing he could not put down.

The door had a ward on it. Every rented room in Kettle Rows with a ground-floor shop beneath it had a door ward, licensed or not, because the buildings are old and the stairs are the kind that people fall down. Tamsin's was a small return-home working: a door that recognises its own tenant and lets her in without a key. A licensed thing. A four-part working that costs about ninety credits and takes eleven minutes to seat properly.

Elias had watched her seat it. He had held the light.

The physical lock was a deadbolt with a thumb turn, in the old position, unfastened.

The ward on the frame was doing something else.

He put his hand flat against the jamb, low, where the framing had been drilled and filled twice, and the world tipped.

It was not a vision. He had given up expecting one years ago, and the people who wanted a vision from him were disappointed. It came the way a stair comes when you are tired: not as pictures but as order, as the sense of what came before what, laid out flat and without any of the reasons.

The working began inside the room. That was the first thing, and it was wrong, because a door ward starts at the door. This one started somewhere around the desk and it bound itself to the desk before it touched anything else, and then it reached out and chose — he had no word for *chose* here, only a direction, a narrowing — and then it turned, and turned the way a door turns, and then it released.

And the release was not hers.

He knew what her working looked like. He had watched it seat itself. Hers was a small clean thing, four acts, tidy, and it stopped at the threshold. It did not do this. This one went past the threshold and out into the landing and did something on the other side of the frame, and the last act of it came after the last act of hers, and it was not hers at all.

Then the floor came up and he was sitting on the step with his back against the wall, and the step was moving under him in a way that had nothing to do with the step.

He had been in the room for about nine seconds. The clock on the wall said one fifty-four. His phone said one fifty-eight. Both were wrong, and the phone was the one he had been carrying when he came up.

He put his hand flat on the step and made himself wait for the wall to stop being the wrong angle. The coffee cup was still downstairs where he had left it. The kettle on the sill had been off for hours.

The order was still there, and it had not faded the way his fear had.

*Begin inside. Bind to the desk. Address the door. Turn it. And then, after, a second thing closes itself — a thing with no owner, no weight, no reason in it, sitting exactly where a door working's release should be.*

He had seen that shape before. He had a name for it in a drawer in his own apartment, and the name was not his, and the file it was written in had a length of red thread knotted around it because he could not make himself unknot it or throw it away.

He did not open it. He was on the fourth floor of his own building, and it was nearly two in the morning, and the woman who slept in that room had been on shift since eleven and was not due back until six.

He pulled out his phone and called the police line, and then he called Nadia Kest, and he said the address first, and the floor, and the door.

"They'll send the nearest unit," Nadia said. "Don't touch anything, Venn."

"I haven't touched anything," he said, which was true, and was also the sentence of a man who had just put his hand on the frame.

"Hold on." He heard her keys, which meant she was already dressed. "Elias. What is the working."

He looked at the dark rectangle of the doorway and tried to say it in a way that would not make her write it down as a hallucination, because she would, and it would be in a file, and the file would be the right shape for burying it.

"It's a door ward," he said. "It starts in the room instead of at the door. And the release is wrong."

"Wrong how."

He shut his eyes. His left ear had gone quiet, which it had never done for him, and he had to open his eyes again to be sure he was not also going blind.

"There's a second act at the end that she never performed," he said. "I can't tell you who did it. I can tell you it happened, and that it happened after hers, and that it isn't a fault. It isn't a drift. Somebody put it there."

There was a pause on the line long enough for him to look at the door again.

"Nadia."

"I'm here."

"There is a man on the corner outside this building and he is looking at the front of it and not at the cordon. I have not been able to see his hands from here."

Nadia made a small sound that meant she was writing something down.

"Stay on the stair," she said. "I'm twenty minutes."

The first two responders came at ten past two: a young pair from the Kettle Rows station, Priya Sandoval with her hands in her pockets and her partner Marek Dace carrying a stair light. They were good. They came up slow, they read the door before they entered it, and Dace put a shoe under the frame so it could not swing.

Sandoval looked at Elias on the step and then at the room and then at Elias again.

"You're the one who called it in."

"Detective Sergeant Venn. Sequence Desk. I'm on the second floor." He nodded at the ceiling. "I live in the building. That was hers."

"Christ." She looked at her notebook, then stopped, then put the notebook away and took out the phone instead. "Okay. Wait here."

"Wait here is the stair," Elias said. "The frame is the evidence. Don't move the door, don't lean anything on the jamb, and don't let anybody put a hand on that frame until Mara Quill is here."

"Mara Quill."

"Saint Orra Reach. Residue laboratory. Tell her Venn. She'll know what it means."

Sandoval looked at the door. The frame was a plain painted frame with two drilled and filled patches in it, the way frames in this building all had. It did not look like anything. That was the difficulty with doors. A door is the least remarkable object a person owns, and it is the only one in the house that opens to both the street and the room, and everything anybody says about who came in has to pass through it.

Dace set a strip of tape across the threshold at ankle height, the way they did for a scene, and the tape went dull where it crossed the frame.

They went in. Elias stayed on the step with his back to the wall and his hands on his knees and let the two of them work, because there was nothing in that room he could hold that he was allowed to hold, and because the second he had walked out of it, the order in his head had started to come apart at the edges like something held too close to a bulb.

Nadia arrived at twenty past two with her hair wet and a paper cup of clinic coffee that she put down on the step beside him without comment, and then she sat down on the step below him and did not ask him a single question for about two full minutes.

That was the thing about Nadia Kest. Thirty-nine years old, eleven years in the Serious Crimes Unit, and the only officer in Veyra he would take orders from. She knew exactly how to wait, and she knew exactly what it meant when a man needed two minutes of being asked nothing at all.

"You lost time," she said finally.

"Four minutes. I think."

"That's twice this month."

"It's the second time I've lost time. The other time I lost a molar." He turned his head slightly to give her his good side without meaning to. It was a habit and she had never once let him get away with it. "There's a room upstairs. I can see the order of a working in it. And the order has a thing in it that shouldn't be there."

"A second release."

"After hers. And it isn't drifting. Drift is sloppy — drift leaves a smear and no intent-shaped hole. This is seated. Somebody hung it on a frame and let it set." He heard how it sounded. He heard it the way she was hearing it. "I know what that sounds like at four in the morning to a woman who has to write it in a box."

"Then don't say it in a box," Nadia said. "Say it in a room with a door in it and a lawyer's worth of people listening. Did you touch anything?"

"The jamb. Once. When I went up."

"Anyone else?"

"No. The old woman on the fourth floor saw the door open and told me. Dace is fine." He paused. "The frame is the only thing I've been near and I put my hand on it before I thought about it, and I'd have done the same thing in any other building in this city, and if I had, there'd be a second set of my prints on it and the residue would be gone."

Nadia drank her coffee. "Mara's twenty minutes out. She'll take a plate. Until then nobody touches that frame, including the four of you."

Down at the corner, past the police car and the two cones someone had put out on the wet road, a man had stopped to watch.

He was not doing anything. He did not approach, and he did not take out a phone in the way that people take out phones now, which is to say in a hurry and at an angle. He had his hands in his pockets and he stood the way a person stands who has decided to be counted standing there, and after a while he looked up at the second-floor window of his own building — at Elias's apartment, unlit — and then back at the front of the building, and then he walked off toward the river with the unhurried pace of somebody who has finished his shift.

Elias watched him go and did not follow, and understood, with a clarity that arrived far too late to be useful, that a man who stands in the rain counting the windows of a building is a man who has been given a reason to know which window matters.

Four minutes of his life were gone and would not come back. Somebody had paid for a plate in a laboratory in Saint Orra Reach, and the plate was going to cost him the one thing he had left that nobody could question.

The frame was going to be read, in front of him, by a woman who did not believe a word he said, and it was going to say something he could not say, and he was going to have to stand there and be an ordinary man who lived upstairs.

It was almost three. Somewhere downriver a night bus came in on the last run of the shift, and Elias heard it change pitch the way he had heard it every night for six years, and for once he could not tell whether that was a thing he knew or a thing the room had put in his head.
