# Chapter 0016 — Five Documents

Bramwell and Sons of Ash Street had been cutting letters, numbers and marks into other people's metal since 1908, and the shop was two rooms deep with a partition in the middle and a smell of hot steel and old machine oil, and the old man behind the counter was Desmond Bramwell, who was seventy-one, and who had cut a man's initials into a door plate in 1994 and had never forgotten it.

Elias Venn had been in a version of this shop on the eighteenth of September with a camera and a request for a ledger, and had got a page of the tool registry and nothing else. He came back on Wednesday with a different question and found that the different question was much easier to refuse.

"The dies," he said. "I want the die ledger."

"You've had the ledger."

"I've had a page of a *tool* ledger. I want the die ledger, and I want the two entries in it for the profile that is an open square over a filled square, cut as a pair."

Desmond Bramwell put both hands flat on the counter, which is a thing that men of a certain age do when they are enjoying themselves.

"You know what that profile is," he said. "That's a maker's acceptance pair. Every clamp maker in this district stamps one and every man in the trade has one in a drawer. There's six makers in this city and I'd guess they between them have had that profile cut forty times in thirty years."

"I want to know how many times you cut it, and for whom, and when."

"And what happens when I tell you?"

"Nothing happens," said Elias. "It happens to the two men in the second column instead of happening to you, which is why I would rather you told me. But you are not going to, because the die ledger is not a public register and the dies are in a cabinet and a case number does not exist in this matter, and I have not got a warrant and I am not going to invent one this morning to make a conversation go better."

"Then we understand each other."

"We understand each other," said Elias, "and I would like it recorded that you said it first."

Desmond Bramwell opened a drawer, and took out a book, and did not open it, and put it back.

"What I will do," he said, "is tell you what a die shop does, and you may write it down as conversation, and it is true. A die shop cuts profiles. A die shop is asked for a profile and the customer sends a drawing, and the drawing is a piece of paper, and the drawing is worth nothing and the die is worth everything, and that is the entire business model and I have been in it for forty-one years. A man will send me a drawing in an envelope and I will cut a die and post it to him, and in nine cases out of ten he is not the man who will use it, and in the other one he is, and in either case he is not going to tell me and I do not ask, because a die is a piece of hardened steel and steel does not have a conscience."

"Then tell me this and I will go. How many of that profile?"

"Two," said Desmond Bramwell. "Both times in August. The first went out on the seventh to an address in Kettle Rows, to a licensed maker, paid, receipted, and I have a maker's number on the order. The second went out on the nineteenth to an address that is a post box, and the name on it was a trading name, and I was paid in a way I am not going to discuss with a police officer in my own shop."

"Two dies. Same profile. Three weeks apart."

"Two dies," said Desmond Bramwell. "And if you ever stand in front of a magistrate and say that to me, I will say yes, and I will say that I cut a great many dies in August, and I will say that the second one was not stamped with anybody's initials, which is not a thing I have said to anybody, and which I would like noted as an act of memory and not as an offer of help."

---

The brass and foil merchant was on Cutter Street and had been there longer than the tool registry and kept a different kind of record, because a foil merchant's record is money and money is kept for seven years whether anybody asks or not.

He gave Elias the invoices without being asked, standing behind his own counter with a folder already half out, because a man who keeps invoices for seven years is a man who expects to be asked and has an opinion about being made to work for it.

"Stencils and stock," he said. "Forty sheets of foil, four tenths, ninety by one-forty. Sixty lengths of conduit, galvanised, three hundred. A gross of channel section, which nobody in this trade buys unless they are cutting a run, and I have had exactly one customer buy a gross of channel in nine years and he used to be a refrigeration fitter." He turned the pages. "The sixteenth of August, the twenty-second, the fourth of September. Same customer, same address, same order, and I will tell you what I told him on the first one."

"What was that?"

"That if he is cutting his own channel he is not a customer, he is a competitor, and I would want a trade name and I would want it on every invoice, and he said yes and gave me one, and it is in the file, and it is *Halloran Supply*, and Halloran Supply is not a company, it is a name he gave me in a shop, and I wrote it down because I invoice to what I am given."

"Address on the invoice?"

"Left-hand envelope, a post box, in a district I could not tell you." He shut the folder. "What I will say, and it is the only thing I have, is that he paid cash on all three, and that cash is a decision a man makes in about a second, and that people make it when they would rather there were not a record, and that I have never once in nine years lost money on a customer who paid cash."

Elias stood on the pavement outside and wrote three things in his notebook, and the third of them was the one that mattered: *the profile is the product and the profile was cut twice, once for the man who made the first frame and once for a post box. The die is what moves. The die is the travel.*

---

The ward fault log for the Kettle Rows and Halloway district is not a police file and is not a mystery. It is a plastic box on a counter at the Tanager Street works depot, and a woman called Marguerite Osei who had not made the mistake of being related to anybody in the Registry keeps it, and it is a public record because anyone who rents or owns property in a ward-protected block is entitled under the municipal schedule to see what has been reported at that address in the last five years.

Elias asked for the box on Wednesday at twenty past one in the afternoon and was given the box.

Sixty-one slips for Halloway Street alone in the last three months, which is more than any street in the district and more than double the next one. He put them in date order on the counter because the woman at the counter watched him and then, at about the eighth one, stopped watching him and started helping.

"Those are the stair lighting," she said. "That whole bank. There is a fault on that stair that they report every week and sometimes twice in a week, and it has never once been fixed, and it will never once be fixed now, because the man who used to come and be angry about it went to a nursing home in April."

"Resolved ones. Show me the resolved ones."

He went through them and the pattern took about four minutes to appear and then took another twenty to be sure of.

One hundred and six slips for the district in the quarter. Eighty-one marked resolved. Of the resolved ones, seventy-seven were closed on the same day the fault was reported, and of those seventy-seven, sixty-eight carried a contractor's attendance code, and every attendance code on all of them was one of four, and the four codes on the index card nailed to the lid of the box were not names.

They were letters.

"M-2," Elias said. "What is M-2."

"That is on the approved list for the district and the approved list is in the office, and I am not allowed to give you the approved list, and I am not being difficult, that is the same book I have to sign the top of every morning."

"What does the M mean?"

"It means municipal contractor and then a number, and that is all it means, and it has meant that since before I came here." She turned the box round so he could see the index card. "You are going to ask me whether the man who came to that woman's door on the second of September was M-2, and I am going to tell you that I have never seen any of them. They are not municipal staff. They are on the list and they come and they go and I get a code on a slip and I never get a name, and if I asked for a name I would be the second person to ask this month and the other one was told it was in the manual."

"Somebody else has asked."

"Wednesday," said Marguerite Okpara. "A woman from the municipal contracts office, on a phone, very polite, asking for the distribution of attendance codes by post code. I said it was not something I could do and she said she understood perfectly and would raise it with the works manager, and then she said, *and can you confirm the mapping is unchanged since the April revision*." She shut the box. "I said I could not confirm that, and she said thank you, and she rang off, and I have thought about it every day since, and I do not know why I have thought about it every day since."

Nadia had the other two by half past four and she had them the easy way, because everything about an insurer is public by statute and Veyra had more statute than it had weather.

There was a ward continuity office. There were eleven of them in the city and they were not insurers in law, they were risk offices, and the reason the word insurer had got into everyone's mouth was that the thing they sold behaved like insurance and the thing they published looked like a risk model, and everybody in Veyra had been saying the word for two years without anybody in a legislature finding the time to be precise about it.

The office that maintained the schedule covering rehabilitation account 44-9 was named on the schedule. The office that maintained the schedule covering the Kettle Rows and Halloway district ward block was named on the schedule.

"Two firms," Nadia said, from the other side of the desk, reading from a filing on a screen with a public document open in front of her. "And that is two names I can read, and I have read them, and neither of them is one of the four attendance codes on that box, and neither of them has anything in this case that I can put in front of a magistrate, and I am going to say that out loud now so that you cannot build a wall out of them later."

"What are the numbers?"

"The 44-9 schedule has a rehabilitation account with a premises address, a card-issuing facility, and a quarterly settlement cycle, and it is a schedule of *premises that are not covered*, which is the part I cannot get past. They publish what they will pay for. They do not publish what they will not. And 44-9 is in the second book. There is one line on that schedule about a patient file and it says *discharge* and it has a date on it, which is Monday the twenty-fourth, which is Niko Senn's discharge, and there is nowhere in this city now for a continuity office to send a letter about him."

"The district block."

"The district block's ward continuity premium went down eleven points between the quarter ending in June and the quarter ending this month." Nadia turned the screen. "And the block's reported faults went down by sixty per cent over the same period. The model is priced off reported faults, Elias. It is priced off what people ring up about. So the premium is falling because people have stopped ringing up, and people have stopped ringing up because somebody has been going to their doors and telling them the equipment is fine and giving them a card for the next man."

Elias sat very still.

"So they have made the district cheap on purpose," he said.

"They have made the district *cheap to insure* on purpose, or they have done the thing that has the same effect, which in this city is the same thing." She closed the filing. "And I am going to say the rest of it and I am not going to enjoy it. You can see the whole of this coming and you have been able to see it since Saturday morning, and you have got four documents and a fault box, and none of it is admissible, and none of it is a crime, and there is not one thing in it that stops a man answering a lamp."

"I know."

"You do not get to say *I know* like that to me," Nadia said. "You are not the one whose contract starts at midnight tomorrow at sixty per cent of what it was."

---

He went back to Halloway Street at half past five and Kirsten Brannigan gave him the sheet, and it lay on her own office desk where it had lain for eleven weeks, and he stood back from it with his hands visible, and Mara Quill photographed it where it lay, in the client's own room, with the client's own permission in writing, and the sheet stayed in the client's drawer.

It was a printout of a floor plan with a line marked on the ceiling, a corner circled, the hours between one and four shaded, and a tick box in the top right.

And in the bottom left, in a small clean hand, five lines that were not part of the plan at all, and that had been printed on it because they were on every one of them.

*Line: ward supply, cold room spur, ceiling, north side.*
*Position: 900mm from the corner, above the door head.*
*Do not isolate. The line must be live at the start and at the end.*
*The unit will hold what is on the line while it is fitted.*
*Confirm the patient is in the room.*

Elias Venn read the last line twice and then put his hand flat on the back of his own neck, which is where a man's fingers had rested three or four weeks ago in a room above a tax office, and then took the hand away again.

"Doctor Quill," he said. "Would you put the date and the time on the sleeve, because I would like to stop looking at this for a minute."

The sleeve said *26 September, 17:52, Halloway Street, second floor office, document read in the presence of the client, unaccompanied, original in the client's custody. Photographs by M. Quill, in place, two exposures. K. Brannigan present throughout and signed.*

Then he walked to the corner and stood in the street for a bit with his hands in his pockets.

There was no crime anywhere in the five documents. There was a die cut twice, a purchase of foil and conduit and channel, an account with a return clause on its tools, a schedule of premises that an office did not insure, a price that had fallen because the district had stopped complaining, and a box on a counter with four letters in it and no names.

And at the end of it, printed on every sheet, in a small clean hand, free of charge, to anybody who bought the product and was not the person being used:

*Confirm the patient is in the room.*

He went back to the office and wrote it on the board in his own hand before he wrote anything else.

*26 Sept. Five documents. Bramwell: profile cut twice, 7 Aug and 19 Aug, second to a post box. Cutter St: 40 foil, 60 conduit, 1 gross channel, 16/22 Aug and 4 Sept, to a trading name, cash, collected by a courier. Ward fault log, Tanager St: 106 in the quarter, 81 resolved, 77 same day, 68 with an attendance code, four codes, no names, list in an office. Continuity office schedule: 44-9 in the NOT-COVERED book. Premium down 11 points since June; reports down 60%. The five documents contain no offence.*
*26 Sept. Printed on the sheet, in the supplier's hand, at the bottom: CONFIRM THE PATIENT IS IN THE ROOM.*
