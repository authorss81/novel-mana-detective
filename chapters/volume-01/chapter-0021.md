# Chapter 0021 — The Flag on the Board

The board had three columns on it, and on Monday morning at a quarter to eight there was a burglary in it.

Physical, Residue, Paper, in marker, in the sequence desk's own hand, in eleven years of nobody ever wiping it properly, and under Physical somebody had written *Holloway St, keys taken from door, no force* and under Residue *—* and under Paper *insurance claim 8841, claimant Sable* — and then had run an eraser down the whole of it, hard, four times, and the ink had gone grey and smeary rather than gone, so that the first two letters of *deceased R* were still legible under Holloway Street like something under ice.

Elias Venn stood in front of it with his coat still on and a coffee he had made at six and not drunk, and read what was left of his own handwriting, which was: a *d*, an *e*, and the top half of a *c*.

He had written a case on that board for ten days. Two hundred and six separate things had gone onto it, most of them in his own small bad hand, and the sum of it was that a woman had died in a room two floors above his own flat and the working that killed her had been carried into that room on a piece of metal, and the three columns had held all of it, and now the three columns held a keys theft and a claim against a different Sable, not the one in the September ledger, and the eraser had been the good kind, the kind from the stationery cupboard, and not the sleeve of a sleeve.

"You've seen it," said Nadia Kest.

"I have."

"It went up at ten past seven. Priya did it, and she has apologised, and she is not to be told that it is all right." Nadia had her coat over her arm and a folder under it and the look of a woman who had been in a building since seven. "The flag went up at eleven on Sunday night, which is earlier than I said it would and I want that noted, and the Registry's index office rang me at half past twelve to ask whether the assigned detective had any connection with a certified record in the Tribunal archive, and I said the question had been put to the Deputy Chief's office and that I was not the office that answers it, and the woman on the telephone said *of course* and put the phone down in a way that meant she was writing."

"What is it now, then. Factually."

"Factually you are the assigned detective on 441-KR until somebody in writing says otherwise, and you are a man who has applied for a certified record in another matter, and every report anybody writes about 441-KR from this morning carries a motive that is not in the case." She put the folder under the board, which is a thing she had never once done in ten days. "And practically, Elias, you have four days and four hours."

"Four days and four hours," she agreed. "You asked for it to be served after the board. That is the deal and I got it and I would do it again. Serve it, and you are off. Do not serve it, and you are on a connected matter and there is a form for that too, and it is worse, because the form says *the applicant is aware that the record may be material to the conduct of the assigned detective*."

"Which of those did you tell the Deputy Chief's office."

"Which of those did I tell them." She squared the folder. "I told them the first. And I told them the time, and I told them that the service was to be after ten o'clock on Thursday the fourth, and a man in that office wrote the time down in front of me, and I made him read it back, and I would like you to notice that I have made a man read a time back to me and that this is what four hundred words has done for my professional reputation."

Elias Venn looked at the board for a while longer and then put his coffee on the desk, because he was not going to drink it and holding it was a way of not starting.

"Is it the board or the file," he said.

"Both, and in that order, and neither of them is anybody's fault." Nadia Kest said it flatly, which was the whole of what she had been doing for ten days. "The file you can be reattached to. The board is a piece of laminate with three columns on it, and it was the only thing in this building that made you say where you were going before you went, and there is now a burglary on it, and there is a form for that too, called a duty record, which is a good idea, and I have argued for it for six years, and I would like to be able to recommend it at eight in the morning instead of at two in the afternoon."

Then she said, "Go and find your witness," and went out, and he stood under the board for another minute, and the coffee went cold on the desk, and he left it there, which was a thing he would have done differently a fortnight ago.

Alderney Row is a terrace of eight houses off a cobbled yard two streets back from the ring road, and number six is the one with a launderette counter in the front room and a hand-painted sign, and the hand-painted sign said **WASH & PRESS** in a style that had gone out of fashion before the woman who painted it was born.

Wen Senn was sixty-six and had worked that counter for nineteen years and had no telephone in the house, and she made that fact clear to a police officer on the step before he was through the door, in the voice of somebody who had said it to a lot of people.

"I told the last one as well," she said. "The one who came on Sunday. He didn't want to come in either, he just stood on the step and talked through the gap, and I said it through the gap, and he was very polite about it, and then he gave my nephew a card through the gap as well."

Elias Venn stood on the step of number six, Alderney Row, at twenty past ten on the morning of Monday the first of October, and felt the entire shape of the last three days rearrange itself.

"What did the man on Sunday look like," he said.

"Neat." Wen Senn said the word the way Dr Okafor had said it about the maintenance man in a Ward Works vest, and he heard it, and it was the same word. "Glass on the counter and everything in its place. Suit for a Monday. He asked whether my nephew was in, and I said he was out, and he waited about twenty minutes, and my nephew came in at about nine and went out with him for a cigarette and came back in with a piece of paper and went upstairs."

"Where is your nephew."

"Upstairs. He's on nights. He should be asleep now and he isn't, and that's because you lot never ring before you come, and I have told the last three of you the same thing and I am telling you the same thing."

---

Niko Senn was twenty-four and had been at Alderney Row for one night and had slept about four hours of it and was in the room he had had at thirteen, which was a room with a bed, a wardrobe, a window onto a yard and a photograph of a woman who was not Wen Senn and was not his mother either and had never been identified in the ten days she had been on that wall.

He had a plastic bag with a clean shirt in it still, and he had a mug, and he had a leaflet on the table, and Elias Venn sat down opposite him and did not start with a question.

"Right," said Niko Senn. "Say the thing."

"I am not going to."

"Which thing."

"Any of the four or five things I have come in here to say, and I will tell you why, and then I will say the ones I am allowed to say, and you can tell me to go home." Elias Venn put his notebook on his knee and did not open it. "You are a party on Thursday. You have been a party since the twenty-first of September and I have never once explained that to you, and everything else I have done for the last fortnight has been a version of doing it without you. So. Thursday the fourth of October, ten o'clock, a review board, it sits in public, you may be there, you may bring a person with you, and it will decide whether a piece of paper can put you on a bus to an address on Verge Row. That is the whole thing. I have never asked you whether you wanted to know that and I am not going to now, because you would say no, and you would be entitled to."

Niko Senn put his mug down.

"You've been in here every day since the twenty-first," he said.

"Not every day. Eleven times." He said it without heat, the way a man says a thing he has counted. "And every one of them you had a piece of paper, and about half of them you asked me what I could remember, and about half of them you told me you weren't writing it down, which I know is a thing men do, because you said *this is not evidence unless you make it* on the first night and I have not stopped hearing it."

"That is correct."

"I know it's correct. I have known since you said it." He turned the mug round. "You've been helping me. I want that said, because I know what it's cost you and I'm the one it's been costing. I'm not stupid, and whatever that man on Sunday night thought when he said it, I'm not."

"What did he say."

"He said the police had stopped." Niko Senn looked up. "That's the word he used. *Stopped*. He said the officer who lives upstairs has been stood down from the case and the case is being looked after, and there's a service at the Council for people who've seen something and can't hold on to the order of it, and it costs nothing, and you don't have to give anybody your name to start with." He laughed once. "That's a good one, isn't it. Three weeks of that and then a man on my step telling me the Council will hold it for me."

Elias Venn did not move and did not speak for a moment, and what went through him was not a thought about the case. It was the plain physical fact that Nadia had told him in a car on Sunday afternoon at about half past two, in a car park, that somebody in his own building would know by Tuesday, and that a man had come to a door in the outer wards thirteen hours ago, on Sunday night at about nine o'clock, and had said it out loud, and had been forty-eight hours early, and had not been wrong.

"Niko. Listen to me carefully and answer the question and don't worry about the answer." He leaned in, which he knew was a habit, and then did not. "He said *stopped*. Are you certain he said stopped?"

"He said stopped. He said it twice." Niko Senn frowned. "Why?"

"Because a flag went up in a building on Morrow Hill at eleven o'clock last night, and it is written in a system that about forty people in this city can read, and none of those forty are me." He put his hands flat on the table, and then noticed himself doing it, and took them off again, and there was a small unpleasant silence in which neither of them said anything about a man in a smock. "He also said he was from the Council."

"He said Witness Services. That's what the leaflet says." Niko Senn pushed the leaflet across the table. "I was going to give you that and you were talking. Here. It's nothing, it's a public thing, they leave them everywhere. Half the people in this city couldn't tell you what the Witness Service is."

It was a single sheet, printed both sides, on the kind of paper a hospital uses for discharge instructions, and the ward line above the title said **CITIZEN INFORMATION — WITNESS SERVICES, CONCORD HALL** in a small type, and the title said:

**If you have seen something happen, we can help you hold the order of it.**

Elias Venn read it standing up in his own hand, at arm's length, the way he had read four printed lines off a counter in a clinic ten days ago, and he was aware while he did it that a boy of eleven had once done the same thing in the same room and had not thought it was anything.

The leaflet said that a working left residue in a place or a person, and that residue could be read by a trained examiner, and that a residue on its own was not an account, and that the account came from a person. It said that most people could not remember the order of an event they had seen, and that this was not a fault of character and could not be improved by effort. It said that there was a free service, at the Witness Services counter, at the back of the ground floor of Concord Hall, where a trained practitioner would go through an account with a person and help them hold the order of it, so that the account could be certified.

And it said, in a box of its own, four lines:

*Hold the order you actually perceived. If you cannot hold it, say so. An uncertain account recorded as uncertain is worth more than a complete one you cannot stand behind. Do not be helped into an order you did not see.*

Elias Venn read the box three times and had to put his hand on the table.

"That is the whole of it," said Niko Senn. "It's a leaflet. It took me a fortnight to go in there and it's a leaflet."

"Have you been in there."

"Twice." He said it without any weight, which was the worst way to say a thing. "Monday, and Saturday. They're at the back past the certificate office and there's a rack by the counter with about forty of them on it and a woman with a clipboard who gave me one of these and said I didn't have to give my name to start with, and she was right, she wasn't lying, she was completely right, I didn't have to give my name to start with, and then at the end of it there was a form and I did give my name."

"Niko—"

"Don't." He held a hand up. "I know. I know what you're going to say and I want to say the other half of it first because you never let me say the other half of anything. I know you think somebody sat me down and put this in me. I know that's what it looks like. And I have sat in a room at the back of Concord Hall with a woman called Kaye who was kind to me and who has got a certificate on the wall for it and who told me at the start that nothing she did could put anything in my head, and she was right, she was *right*, and it's the truest thing anybody's said to me since the police woman told me I could stop at any time."

He stopped. He had his hands flat on the table again and he had put them there on purpose this time.

"Ask me the other thing," he said.

"I am not going to ask you the other thing."

"Why not."

"Because whatever you tell me in the next four minutes will be true, and I will believe you, and I have got a piece of paper in this case that I wrote myself in about four minutes on Friday the twenty-first because a woman in a laboratory told me that a man who has one private certainty is going to be taken apart by the first competent defence in this city." Elias Venn put the leaflet down flat on the table between them. "And because there is one version of Thursday morning in which you walk into a room at ten o'clock with the account you have carried since the seventeenth, and one version in which you walk in with something else instead, and there are more ways for an account to become something else in fourteen days than there are people who would ever admit to it, and I cannot see one of them from here, and neither can you, and that is not suspicion. That is the shape of the position anybody is in."

Niko Senn looked at the leaflet for a while.

"You're saying I can't trust my own head."

"I'm saying that nobody can, and that this city has built an entire building for the purpose of not having to, and that the woman in there is good at her job and you are not the only person she has ever done it to."

"Then what's the point of me."

"The point of you is that you are twenty-four, and you have an aunt who does not have a telephone, and you were discharged by a company on Monday the twenty-fourth for missing an appointment you did not know about, and on Thursday morning at ten o'clock somebody is going to try to put you on a bus to Verge Row." Elias Venn put his hand out flat, palm up, the way he had on Sunday in a corridor on Marshall Street, and then took it back again, which was becoming a habit that meant something and not something else. "Come to the board. Bring your aunt if you want to. Don't rehearse anything on Thursday. Don't read anything anybody gives you between now and Thursday, and if somebody gives you anything, bring it in that hand and don't leave it anywhere."

"She'll be nervous."

"Your aunt will be extremely nervous and she will be the most credible person in that room, because she has never once been coached and it will show."

Niko Senn got up and got his aunt's cardigan off the back of the chair and put it over the leaflet so that it stayed flat, and said, "If you want that, I've got to know. You don't get to keep taking things off people and then being right about it. That's what the man in the smock—"

He stopped.

Elias Venn did not move, and did not help him, and did not say a single word, and it took about four seconds, and in those four seconds he understood that he was in a room with a man who had been about to say a name and had heard himself doing it, and that the hearing was the important part and was not going to happen again.

"You were saying," said Elias.

"I was saying you don't get to do that." Niko Senn's voice was slightly unsteady and he steadied it. "You came in here and you made me put my cardigan on it and you're not taking it. So take it. It's a leaflet. Half the street's got one."

He left it. Elias Venn folded it once and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, and it was the only piece of paper he took out of number six, Alderney Row, on the first of October, and Wen Senn put her cup in the sink and watched him do it and said nothing, which was the correct conduct of a woman with a launderette, no telephone, and four men through her door in a fortnight.

---

He wrote it up in the car on the cobbles at half past eleven, in the small bad hand, with the times on it, and then he did the thing he had been not doing for three days, which was to go back and read item eight of his own note of the twenty-ninth of September, signed and dated and filed as a note and not as a request, with a printed instruction along the bottom telling anybody who processed it as a request that somebody in this building had done something worse than what he had done.

*8. A 4(c) entry is completed by a certifying office, and every issued certificate carries the name of the office that issued it. There is such a certificate in this file: C-441-KR-04, issued 16:10 on Wednesday 19 September and attached to the transfer order in the patient's hand, which is in a clinic's own file and is also posted on a public board in a waiting room. I have read the box on that document four times. I have never read the issuing-office line.*

He had written that on the twenty-ninth of September, in his own hand, about a sheet of paper twenty feet away on a public board, and had gone home instead.

The certificate was in a clear plastic sleeve in the Kettle Rows Community Clinic's own file, and Dr Okafor had put a copy on the waiting-room board beside the transfer order and a block of ninety-odd photocopied pages of her own maintenance book, and the boy of eleven had read it standing up with his finger on the line, and Elias Venn had read the box on that document four times and had walked away from the same wall twice.

He got the sleeve out at twenty to twelve, in a corridor, with nine people in the room and a television on with a woman in a Civic Concord vest explaining that district protection was intermittent, and he stood back from the counter and read the issuing-office line, and it took him about four seconds, and then he stood there for about a minute and forty of them.

It said:

*Issued by: **Witness Services (Sequence Support)**, Concord Hall, Morrow Hill.*

And then, in the same eight-point type, a distribution line he had also never read: *This certificate may be relied upon by any office holding an open file in the same district.*

*Witness Services (Sequence Support)* was an office. It was a name on a line, the way a form's code is a name on a line, and it was the issuing office of a sequence certificate in a dead woman's case, and Elias Venn had put that in writing on the twenty-ninth of September and had not read it for two days because reading it would have cost him a case.

The leaflet in his inside pocket said there was a free service at the Witness Services counter, at the back of the ground floor of Concord Hall.

He put the sleeve back into the file, and the file back on the shelf, with his hands visible and without touching anything else, and wrote on the back of his own left hand, in the small bad hand, the date, the time and the name of the office, and did not scrub it off.
