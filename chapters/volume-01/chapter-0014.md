# Chapter 0014 — Monday the Twenty-Fourth

Morrow Hill on a Monday morning is a place that runs on a timetable, and the Proof Registry's preliminary attestation list for that week was on a board outside the hearing rooms in a frame that had been screwed to the wall in 2011, and 441-KR was on it at ten o'clock in Room Two on the second floor, and the room had four chairs on one side of a table and six on the other and a wall clock that was eleven minutes fast and a recorder plugged into a socket that had been replaced in March and worked.

Elias Venn came in at five to ten and was the first person in the room except for a man from the Registry's systems office with a laptop, who was eating a bun, and a woman who set out five identical folders and made everybody take the one with their name on it.

Registrar-Counsel Bettina Haile came in at ten exactly and thanked nobody for being on time. She was in a dark suit and she had the draft in front of her in a ring binder, and she did the thing she always did, which was to make the room comfortable by removing everything from it.

"This is a preliminary attestation and it is not a hearing of a dangerous working," she said, "and I am going to read that out because two of the people in this room arrived this morning expecting a hearing, and the difference matters. In a dangerous-working hearing the question is whether a working occurred. In a preliminary attestation the question is narrower and duller, and it is whether a sequence on a document has been checked against a standard, and the document goes into the world either way. Nobody is accused of anything in this room. Nobody is convicted of anything in this room. What I am about to do is put a stamp on a page."

Around the table on the wider side: Wren Abasi, Concord Ward Works, with her coat still on and a folder of her own. A clinical observer from the Registry called Ms Verity Okpara, who had a notebook and had not spoken yet. Nadia Kest, in uniform, in the second chair, with a folder that was four pages thick and a pen she had not uncapped.

Elias Venn, in the first chair, with nothing in front of him at all, because he had refused to countersign the document and had therefore been given, by a clerk at eight-forty, a clean copy of a draft he had never held in his hands.

"You have no papers," said Haile.

"I have no signature," said Elias. "I have not been given the paper to sign. I would be content to be given the paper to refuse in front of the recorder, but I was not offered it, and I did not ask for it, and I am not going to make a small ceremony out of an administrative absence."

Haile looked at him for a moment. Then she turned to the systems man.

"Are you the clerk for file 441-KR?"

"Yes, Registrar-Counsel."

"I am going to need you to confirm two entries on the screen, in front of the recorder, and to read the entry time and the issue time of the sequence certificate in that file, and the opening time of the case file it came out of, in that order, and to say where you are reading them from. Not from memory. From the screen."

The clerk put his bun down.

"From the case management system, the Proof Registry's own instance, which is the same instance your office writes to," he said. "Certificate C-441-KR-04, sequence certificate, second page, issued sixteen ten, Wednesday nineteenth of September. The document it is attached to, transfer of place of residence under continuing treatment, registry outbox reference nineteen sixty-five, produced by the Morrow Hill outbox at sixteen fifty-one on the same day. The case file it was produced from, file 441-KR, opened at nine twenty on the eighteenth."

"Thank you," said Haile.

Elias wrote three times on the back of a sheet of paper. Forty-one minutes.

"Sergeant." Haile turned round. "I am going to stop you there, because I can see the arithmetic arriving, and I would like to deal with it now while the room is calm. The interval between those two entries is forty-one minutes. Is the certificate defective?"

"I do not know," said Elias.

"That is not what I asked."

"I am answering the question I can answer." He put the pen down. "A sequence certificate was issued at sixteen ten on Wednesday for a document that was not produced by the outbox until sixteen fifty-one. Those are two entries in your system, read by your clerk, from your screen. What produced that interval, and whether it matters, is a question about your system and I am not the right person to ask it in this room. But the certificate is the document. It is what carries the sequence. And it was issued, in your system, before the document it certifies existed as a document."

Wren Abasi said, without opening her folder, "That is a known behaviour of the outbox. Certificates are generated against the request, not against the print. It has been the behaviour since the system was installed and there is an explanatory note in the manual."

"Then somebody has written the note in the manual," Elias said.

"Somebody has written the note in the manual," said Wren Abasi, "because it is true, and because a note in a manual that says *this is how it works* is a stronger document than a witness in a room."

Nobody argued with her. He had not expected anybody to argue with her. He had expected the two times to go into the record as two times, and they did, because a clerk had read them off a screen with a recorder running, and the only thing anyone in the room did with them was explain them, which is what institutions do with facts that are inconvenient and not false.

Elias asked for the record to show that he had put the interval in issue. It was noted. Then Haile read the draft.

She read all two and a half pages, and she read them well, and nobody in the room interrupted, and the draft said what the draft had always said: a working occurred in a dwelling at a Kettle Rows address, it was self-directed, it was single-subject, it contained no third-party address, it closed within the dwelling, and no further inquiry into the conduct of any third party was required. It was well made. Every sentence in it was true, and every sentence in it was a reason not to look at anything else.

"Does the assigned detective wish to comment?" said Haile.

"Only on the schedule."

"That is not what I asked, Sergeant, and I have not asked it twice." She shut the binder. "You refused a countersignature at sixteen forty-one on Friday. That refusal is in the file. I have read it. It is a four-item document. Item three says that you are unable to say what the act is, whether it was performed in the room or delivered into it, who made it, or why, and that you are aware of at least one ordinary explanation for your own account that you cannot exclude."

"Yes."

"That is the most extraordinary thing I have read in this job in six years and it is going to be read aloud in a tribunal in about four years by a man in a wig who will be extremely fair to you." She wrote something on a pad. "I have never in my life had an assigned detective hand me a document that says *I cannot exclude an ordinary explanation for my own perception*, and I want you to understand that from where I sit that document is worth more than a conviction, and I want you to understand that it is also going to cost you a case, and I am not going to pretend otherwise in order to be kind to you at ten o'clock on a Monday."

"I did not write it to be kind to you."

"No," said Bettina Haile. "You wrote it in a laboratory on Friday afternoon because a forensic mage told you that a man who only has the inside of his own head is going to be taken apart by the first competent defence in this city. And she was right. And now I have to decide what to do with it, and I am going to tell you what I am going to do before I do it, because I have been told by two people in the last four days that I am the sort of person who does things politely and then they find out later, and I would rather not be that again."

She turned to the systems clerk.

"I am going to direct that the Disagreement of the Assigned Detective, all four items, be attached to the attestation as Schedule One, in full, unedited, with the date and the time of the refusal and the name of the officer refusing, and that the attestation cite the schedule. Am I permitted to do that without a hearing?"

The clerk said, "You are, Registrar-Counsel. It is a schedule, not a finding."

"Then do it," said Bettina Haile. "Item one, granted. I will tell you why I am granting it, because you will think about my reasons, and you are entitled to. A certificate with a schedule attached is a document that a person can read and check and disagree with, and I would rather issue one of those than issue a clean one, and I would rather issue that than issue nothing, because issuing nothing is what I did with your countersignature on Friday and it is not a thing I am proud of. Schedule One. Done."

"Item two," said Elias. "Which is that the file record that a person named Niko Senn, twenty-four, exists, is held at the Kettle Rows Community Clinic on Marshall Street under a clinical hold of Friday the twenty-first, and is not a witness to the working in the dwelling."

"Denied," said Bettina Haile.

"May I ask why?"

"You may ask why and I will answer it and you are not going to like it." She turned her chair round so that she was facing the wall clock that was eleven minutes fast. "An attestation is a statement about a sequence. It is not a statement about the population. If I record a named individual in the body of an attestation to which he is not a party, on a day he has not been asked, then that document becomes a document about a person he cannot see, cannot contest, and does not know exists, and it will be copied by every office in this building by Thursday, and in nine years it will be read out in a compensation hearing to somebody who wants to know whether that young man was in a room. He was not. I have read the file. He is a witness to an attempt and not to a room, and putting his name on this page would convert him from a witness into a subject, and you of all men in this city know what happens to a subject."

"Record him somewhere, then."

"He is recorded," said Bettina Haile. "He is recorded in a clinical hold filed by a physician on Marshall Street, in a form with her name on it, in a system this Registry does not control and cannot overwrite. Doctor Okafor is in this building's lobby at this moment because I asked her to come, and she has that hold in a file, and it is a better record than mine because it is hers."

Elias looked at the table for a moment.

"That is correct," he said.

"I am glad," said Bettina Haile. "And it is useless to you, and I have never in six years pretended that the correct thing and the useful thing are the same, and I do not propose to start." She wrote on the pad. "Item two, denied. Anything else?"

"Item three. The assigned inspector's retention of the assigned detective on this matter, which is a conflict of interest, is the inspector's decision and is on the record, and it is challenged here by the Registry's own assessor."

Wren Abasi's voice came from the other side of the table, flat and thorough. "The residency declaration was volunteered in the assigned detective's own hand before any inspector entered the room. It is the strongest single piece of self-incrimination I have seen in four years of doing this. I would like it noted that I said so at the time and I have said so since."

"Noted," said Bettina Haile. "Item three, noted. Nothing further?"

Elias said, "One thing, Registrar-Counsel, and it is not an item."

"Then it is not an item and it will not be minuted."

"Record that the residue in this matter was measured by an instrument and that the instrument's operator is not a licensed examiner and does not hold a Concord registration for her plates, and that a plate which is not on a register is the testimony of an uncertified person, and that if anybody in this building ever wants to say that no instrument in this city found anything unusual in the room over the deceased, then that sentence is true and it is true this morning, at ten o'clock, and I have recorded it before it is said."

The room was very quiet.

"That is minuted," said Bettina Haile.

At twenty past ten, Haile read the attestation into the recorder and it went out over Elias Venn's refusal, on the Registry's own authority, with Schedule One bound to the back of it. It was a *preliminary sequence attestation*, it was not a dangerous-working finding, it closed nothing, and it said nothing at all about how Tamsin Ro died. The public line remained the four words on the information sheet of the nineteenth: a contained accident involving an unregistered ward tool.

At half past ten, Nadia Kest stood up to correct the minute on one point, and read out the words she had prepared, and Inspector's number and Sequence Desk and her own name went into the recording, and the minute was corrected to show that the assigned detective was retained on the matter by the assigned inspector on the seventeenth of September, with knowledge of his declaration of residency at the scene, on the condition that he did not work alone; and that the condition had been complied with on the twenty-first; and that the condition was hers.

Elias did not look at her while she said it. He looked at the wall clock, which was eleven minutes fast, and at about a quarter to eleven he worked out that the real time was ten forty, and the hearing had taken forty minutes, and none of it had been won.

In the lobby, with his folder under his arm and the light of a stairwell on him, Dr Sana Okafor was standing with a plastic wallet of her own and the Registry's refusal letter in it, which had come to Mara Quill on Friday and which said, in a sentence that Mara had read twice and then put face down on her bench, that no instrument in the city had found anything unusual in the room over the deceased.

"Mr Venn," she said. "The board in my waiting room has the transfer order on it. I am going to add a name to it this afternoon. Whose name, not the patient's. Mine, as the clinician who filed a hold on a person who would otherwise have been put on a bus at six o'clock this morning. Is there anything in that room that anybody can use?"

"Yes," said Elias. "There is a schedule with my name at the bottom of it and an item three in which my inspector states in her own voice that she knew where I lived and kept me anyway. That is the only thing in that folder that will still be true in nine years."

"And the certificate?"

"The certificate is true, complete, and says that nothing happened that needed a third party. It is a very good document. Somebody chose every word of it before we knew what was in the room."

Bettina Haile came through the lobby door with the ring binder under her arm and stopped when she saw the two of them together, and did not appear to mind.

"Doctor. Your production is in and I want to thank you for it, and I want you to know that the box was full." She said it to Okafor and not to Elias. "Forty-one months is an unusual thing to hand over and it has saved this office about a fortnight."

"It was not a favour to the office," said Sana Okafor. "It was forty-one months of my clinic's own faults on a public wall, and your assessor read it on Friday afternoon off a noticeboard at four feet and she did not have to ask me for any of it."

"Yes," said Bettina Haile. "That is what the schedule exists for. It is very rarely used in that direction and I intend to say so in my report, which nobody will read." She turned round to Elias. "Sergeant, one thing before you go, and then I will leave you alone. A schedule is appealable. That is what a schedule is. I have attached your four items to my attestation this morning and I would like you to understand that in about four months somebody with an interest in the Registry's position will be able to apply to have Schedule One struck out, on the ground that it is an unverified lay statement from an officer with a declared conflict, and I have not thought yet about who would do that, and I have not thought about it because it is not my job until somebody does it."

"You have thought about it," said Elias.

"I have thought about it since about half past ten this morning," said Bettina Haile, "which is the reason I am telling you about it in a lobby instead of letting you find out in October."

He stood in the stairwell of Concord Hall with a folder under his arm and understood that he had just been handed, by the woman who had beaten him, the one thing he actually needed from today, which was not hope. It was a warning with a date on it and a name of an office, and he would be able to write it down at half past two on Monday afternoon and he would not have to be told twice.

"Mr Venn," she said, and stopped, because he had already said it. "Yes. I have. That is what I have been doing since half past ten this morning, and I would like you to know that I did it anyway, and that the reason I did it anyway is a piece of paper in this folder with four numbers in it and no name on it, and I am not going to tell you whose four numbers, because I have not asked, because if I asked I would have to answer the next question, and I have a hearing of my own in about six weeks." She put the binder under her other arm. "Somebody chose every word of my attestation before we knew what was in the room. I signed it this morning with my eyes open and I would like you to have heard that from a person rather than from a copy."
