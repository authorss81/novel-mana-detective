# Chapter 0030 — On the Board

The marker in Dr Okafor's hand stopped moving at about twenty past six and she turned round and said, "No."

"There has not been a question."

"There has been a question since a quarter past five and you have not answered it and I have been standing here an hour waiting for you to." She put the marker in her pocket. "Four sheets are going on that wall in about two hours, in a room with twenty-two chairs in it, and people will read them, and in three days somebody will write something about them, and you have until half past eight to tell me who it costs."

Elias Venn stood in the corridor of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic and discovered, in the way you discover a step in the dark, that he had known about this since Monday and had spent the whole of Monday and a Sunday getting ready to do it and had not once been asked the question.

"Say them," said Sana Okafor. "Out loud, in this corridor, in the order you have written them, and I am not going to tell you whether you have got them right."

He put his hands flat on the back of a chair, which he had been doing since Monday, could not stop doing, and which was now in four documents in two buildings.

"Niko Senn," he said. "His order is in it, his withdrawal is in it, and a minute from Thursday puts him on a fourth-floor landing in Kettle Rows on the sixteenth of September. Three documents, one paragraph, and that is a route."

"Sana Okafor."

"The ninety-odd pages. The moment something on that board answers a Registry intake sheet, your production stops being your failures and becomes your involvement."

"Jules Ardent. A practice notice on his own door, and then a second one, and then a room."

"Marguerite Osei. A plastic box in a municipal depot with no register of who has read it, and a woman who answers at a counter on her own time."

Sana Okafor listened to all four of them without moving and then she said: "Now say the one you have not said."

"Mara Quill."

"No." She shook her head. "Say the one you have not thought about, and you have not thought about her, because she is the first person you told, so you have been assuming ever since that she is safe. Sit down, because I am going to be unpleasant and I would rather you were sitting."

He sat down on one of the twenty-two chairs.

"Marguerite Osei," said Sana Okafor. "You have said her because she is the obvious one, and because she was the last name in Mara Quill's mouth in your back room on Sunday afternoon, and because you are a decent man who does the right list. I want to tell you what I think about that, and then I am going to pin your paper up."

"Go on."

"You read that box on the twenty-sixth of September at about twenty past one in the afternoon, in a depot on Tanager Street, standing up, and you read four letters and no names, and you went away. For a fortnight you have been the only person in this case who has been in that depot, and you have never written her name in a document, and you have told yourself that makes her safe." Sana Okafor sat down on the chair opposite him, which she did not do in waiting rooms. "It does not make her safe. It makes her unlisted. And the second anybody puts a paragraph on a wall that mentions a fault log, a municipal clerk with no register entry is a person who can be asked to produce her box, and she will have to decide, at a counter, on her own time, whether she is a public servant. You have made that decision for her at twenty past six in the morning and you did not ask her."

"She would have said yes."

"She would have said yes, which is why nobody has ever asked her anything," said Sana Okafor. "And that is the whole of the lesson, and it is why I put forty-one months of my own maintenance book on a wall instead of giving a policeman one page. The people who are easiest to find are the people who say yes. I have watched four of them in this waiting room in three weeks say yes to things they should have said no to, and I was one of them, and I put a page of a book on a wall to make it cost me. I was not brave. I was better at finding a way of being brave that did not involve asking anybody else."

---

"Pin them up," she said at about ten past eight.

He unfolded the four sheets and gave them to her, and she read the first line on each of them and then the last paragraph on the fourth, and then took the marker and wrote on the board itself, above the space, in her own hand, three lines:

*Four sheets, handwritten, by a member of the public. Received as a document. Not a request, not a finding, not a certificate.*
*Pin them here because this is the only public wall in this matter and it is mine, and it is mine because I filled it with my clinic's own failures in August before anybody asked me to.*
*— S. Okafor, MBBS, Kettle Rows Community Clinic.*

Then she wrote the date under his name at the foot of the first sheet, in the small bad hand he had written on Sunday night, and pinned the four sheets up flat with four drawing pins, one in each corner, because a folded sheet on a wall is a notice and a flat sheet on a wall is a document.

"There are nine of you," she said to the waiting room, "and there will be more. I am going to read the names out loud before he reads the sheets, and I am not going to soften any of them, and if any of you is one of them I would like you to know that I have said so first."

---

There were nine people in the waiting room at half past eight on Wednesday the tenth of October, and one of them was Jules Ardent, sitting in the fourth chair from the door with his coat on his knees and the folded notice from his own door in his hand, and who had come because there is a notice on his door and nowhere to be on a Wednesday morning, and because a man had telephoned him on Sunday afternoon to say there would be a document on a public wall in this district with his name in it, and had not asked him to come, and had not expected him to.

Sana Okafor read the names out loud.

"Niko Senn, who is twenty-four and who is not in this room. Dr Sana Okafor, who is putting it up. Jules Ardent, who is in the fourth chair, and who said he would get up and leave if I made it awkward, and it is not awkward, it is a wall. And Marguerite Osei, who keeps a municipal log in a depot, who is not in this room, and who has not been asked about any of it."

Nobody looked at Jules Ardent. He had expected somebody to, said, "That's right," to nobody, and did not get up.

Then Elias Venn read the four sheets out loud.

He got through the first of them and he knew, about nine words in, that he was not going to be able to tell you afterwards which of the four things he had said first. There was no sequence in him. There was a man in a corridor with a hand on a door frame and a woman with a marker and a room with nine people in it, and the four sheets had an order printed on them by the order of the paper, and there was nothing at all on top of that saying what came before what.

So he said so.

"I am going to read these in the order they are written on the paper," he said. "That is the only order I have. I have not got the order of the last day and a half and I am not going to stand in this room and pretend to you that I have, and if I stop in the middle of a sentence it is not because the sentence is difficult, it is because I cannot always tell you which sentence came before it."

"Are you all right?" somebody said from the back, and it was an ordinary question from an ordinary person, and the first time in a month anybody had asked him it.

"No," said Elias Venn. "I am going to read these out and then sit down, and Dr Okafor has told me that is the correct order for a document and not for a person, and she is right, and I would like the room to hold both of those things at once."

And he read them, in the order they were written, which was: a certificate and the name of the office that issued it; an intake sheet and a printed heading and a description entered into a box by telephone at fourteen twenty-two on the twenty-first of September by a police officer who had been asked a reasonable question; a form, a practice note, and a sentence about an account not being impugned; a leaflet with four lines in a box on it, and the same four lines in a publication on sale at a forms counter for three credits; a curriculum, two modules, page four and page five; a board minute of Thursday, item three.

And then the last thing, which he had added in his own hand on Sunday night at about half past ten and had not told anybody about, which was not in the four sheets at all, and which he said to nine people in a waiting room, standing up, with nothing in his hands:

"And there is one more thing and it is not a document, and I am the only person in this city who can say it, and I am saying it because Mara Quill told me on Monday that a list of documents can be answered with a document and this one cannot."

He said it slowly, twice, because nine people were listening and because it was about a pen.

"A drawing office drew a channel with a seat in it. Somebody cut a seat into brass and it does not have a tool in it. It has a pen line in it, and where the draughtsman changed pens, the thickening went into the metal, and there is no tool in the world that does that. Whoever made that carrier worked to a picture, and the picture was made from a description, and the description gave a depth, and the depth is written on the drawing as *Fig. 3, Q*, and Fig. 3, Q is a threshold measurement a woman certified in a Concord office on the twenty-seventh of September, in pencil, in her own name, and the certified copy is in a drawer in that building which she cannot open."

He stopped.

"I cannot tell you who drew it, or where. I have a drawing, a number, a piece of metal in a laboratory, and a woman who will not put her plate on a register. That is all I have, and I am putting it on a wall because a wall is the last place left that a man with no file and no standing can put anything." He put the four sheets down. "My own account of the order of that working is unverified and I have said so in four documents. I live at the address. And this is about where a written description of an order came from, and not about how anybody died, and I would be obliged if it did not become a story about a young man of twenty-four, because there is a man in this room with a notice on his door and I would like him to be able to leave."

---

Jules Ardent left at about ten past nine, on the way the whole room went, and said nothing to anybody, and Elias Venn did not look at him.

At about ten o'clock, the dispensary counter on the corridor side of the waiting room got a note, and the woman at the counter brought it to Dr Okafor and not to Elias Venn, which was correct, and then Dr Okafor read it and gave it to him.

It was a municipal form, one side, in the flat official hand of a woman who has filled in about nine hundred of these and does not enjoy any of them.

**TANAGER STREET WORKS DEPOT — MUNICIPAL WARD MAINTENANCE**
*Note to a member of the public.*

*1. This depot will not provide a statement to the officer named below in respect of the ward fault log, or any slip in it, or any question about the distribution of attendance codes, on any future occasion, without a formal request in writing from an office that holds the log.*

*2. Reason given: two such requests in the past month. One of them was told to me that it was in the manual.*

*3. I am not refusing a formal request. I am refusing a person. Those are not the same and I would be obliged if the difference was recorded.*

*Marguerite Osei. Ward fault log. Municipal employee, Grade 4.*

He read it three times, the third to be sure there was nothing else on it. There was not. No address, no number, and not the internal post, because it had been put in her hand and she had put it on the counter herself, which meant she had walked into a building on Marshall Street, off a shift, to put a piece of paper where a doctor would see it, and had gone back to a depot.

He did not go and find her. There was nothing to find her about.

"That is correct," he said. "And I would not have done anything else, and if I had been in her place I would have written the same three lines in the same order, and I would have put the last one in first, because it is the only one of the three that anybody is going to read."

"Are you going to reply to it?"

"No."

"She may think—"

"She may think a police officer did not answer a municipal clerk's note, and she will be right, and in four months the only thing she will know about me is a man who put four sheets on a wall and then did not write back." He folded the note once and put it in his inside pocket. "If I write back, then it is correspondence, and correspondence has a file on it, and the second she has a file on it she is a person who corresponded with a police officer about a thing she was asked for twice. Nadia Kest worked that out in a car on Sunday afternoon and it took her twenty-two years and it cost me a case. I am not going to ask Marguerite Osei to be the second thing she has understood recently."

He took a pen out and wrote four lines on the back of his left hand in the small bad hand, and did not scrub them off: *She is right. I am sorry. Do not write to her. — E.V., 10 Oct.* And the hand went into his pocket, and it stayed in his pocket for the rest of the day.

---

Nadia Kest came in at half past eleven and did not come to the waiting room. She stood with him in the corridor by the fire notice — the miscellaneous receipt from Sunday night, the stub, not the counterfoil — and read it, and took a pen out and turned it over and wrote on the back of it in her own hand: *Same four sheets, one copy only, pinned to the waiting-room board of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic at 08:14, 10 October 2029, at the request of the member of the public, and the board is that of a third party. No copy has been made. — D/I Kest.*

"I am not filing it and you are not asking me to. I am recording where the paper is, because in about a year somebody is going to ask how many copies exist, and the honest answer is that the four sheets are one document and they are on that wall, and a wall is a public room, and a public room is not a custody chain. If somebody photographs a thing off a wall, that is a photograph and not a copy of the paper, and the difference is going to matter to somebody in about four years and not to me."

---

He went down to the Sequence Desk at about half past two in the afternoon, which was a mistake, because he has no file and no reason to be in the building, and the car park was the reason he had come, and Paul Rennick was coming out of the door with his coat over his arm and a photocopy of the four sheets in his hand.

"I have read it twice," said Rennick. "There is a photocopy of it in my pocket. It was made at the counter of that clinic at about eleven this morning by a woman at the desk who did not ask me for a form, and it is a photograph of four sheets off a wall and not a copy of the four sheets, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that it is not in my pocket. I am not the officer on it and I am not going to pretend to be, and I am going to say the only thing I am entitled to say, which is three things, and then I am going to open 441-KR."

"Go on."

"Anybody may publish. There is no law against it, no form for it, and no office in this city that can stop a member of the public from putting four sheets of paper on a wall. That is not a loophole, that is the arrangement, and I have said so in a classroom for six years and I have never had to say it to a man whose name is in a bundle I am holding." He squared the copy up. "Second: it changes nothing about who holds the file. You are not on 441-KR. I am. That happened on Friday morning at forty to twelve, it did not happen because of this, and it is not going to be undone by it, and if you were hoping four sheets on a wall would put you back on a case, it will not, and I would rather you heard that from me on a Wednesday afternoon than from a clerk in a fortnight."

"That is fair."

"The third thing is that I am going to put it in the file."

"You do not have to."

"I know I do not have to. I am going to because it is a document, and it was in a room where people were, and in four years the sentence that finishes anybody is a file that has four sheets of paper about itself in a corridor and not in the file." He put the copy in his inside pocket. "That is the whole of my respect, and it is not very much, and you are entitled to find it cold."

"I have found it useful since Friday," said Elias Venn. "It is the first time anybody has given me a reason instead of a kindness."

Rennick got into his car and said nothing else, and Elias Venn stood in the car park in the afternoon with his hand in his pocket and four lines on it and nothing at all to do until Saturday.

---

He did not go up to the fourth floor of his own building.

That was the whole of the decision, made at about five in the afternoon standing in the yard of 14 Kettle Rows with a set of keys in his hand, and it took about ninety seconds, and it is worth saying what the ninety seconds were spent on, because it was not spent on courage.

Mrs Achterberg's door and Tamsin Ro's door are four inches apart on the same landing, and Elias Venn has read the frame of the second one three times in his life, and the third time was twenty-three days ago at about ten to two in the morning, and he could go up four flights and put a hand on it now and find out in four seconds whether anything had changed, and nobody would see him do it, because the top of the stairs is empty at five on a Wednesday.

He would have found out. That was the point. And it would have been the fourth reading of a threshold in a case where he had a written prohibition with his own name at the bottom of it, and a woman in a laboratory who had given him a plate and a floor, and a morning of his own order of which he could not account for four hours and forty minutes.

And then he would have had a number. A number is not a case and a number is not a witness, and a number goes in the notebook, and the notebook is on a shelf above a bench in a room where a man of twenty-four has withdrawn the only account he ever gave. And if the first thing anybody did with the number was go and check it with his own hand at five o'clock on a Wednesday afternoon, then the number was worth nothing, including to him.

He put the keys back in his pocket, went down to the shop, made a pot of tea in Otto Vahl's urn and drank it standing at the front bench. The cat was on the radio bench in the window in the last of the sun, and the ceiling was still making a noise like a wasp.

At about six o'clock the telephone in the shop rang.

It was Niko Senn, from a call box, in a voice that had been indoors all day.

"Mr Venn. It's Niko Senn. We came up the street for it. I've read it twice, and I could only read it twice, because of the woman at the counter of our launderette, who is my aunt, and who has been crying since about half nine and will not stop, and who reads at about a word a second, and who cannot get to the end of the second sheet because she keeps going back to the top of it to check the words." He stopped, and Elias Venn heard the sound of a call box in a street he did not know. "I'm not ringing to tell you off. I'm ringing because I did the thing you'd do. I went down the street and I got the little cakes, the ones with the icing on, the ones she likes, and it's not even the right hour for them and the woman in the shop let me have them anyway, and she's bringing them up now, and I am ringing to say that I did it."
