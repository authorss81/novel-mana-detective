# Chapter 0103 — Nobody Gives a Name at Night

He asked the same question at the same counter on Friday morning, because he had used it on Wednesday and a general question is a general question and he did not see why a Tuesday should buy him anything a Wednesday had not, and Petra Lund told him to go away in a way that was not unkind, which he took as a correct answer.

He asked it at about a quarter past ten, and she was slower about it than on Wednesday.

"You get one," she said.

"I asked it on Wednesday."

"You asked a different one on Wednesday and it turned out to be a better one, which is not a system and is not going to become one." She turned her pad round, which she did not need to do. "Ask it properly and I will answer it, and if you ask me for anything about a person I am going to put the bell on and you are going to go, and I will not feel bad about it, and if you come back on Monday I will still be here."

"Who logs a withdrawal."

"The front office does. Not a person. A tray, and a book, and the book is a hardback with a date on the front of it and it is about a hundred and forty pages and it has been the same hardback since 2024, and it is not indexed by name, and it is indexed by the day the thing was logged, and the day it was logged is the day it came off the post." She wrote the time on the pad and showed it to him. "That is not the day it was written. I want to be fair to you. I do not know what the day it was written was, because the form is not the book and the book does not carry a date off the document."

"Then somebody in a building knows a letter was signed on the eleventh and nothing until the twentieth."

"Somebody in a building knows that nine days passed." She closed the pad. "That is the only thing I have got and I would like it noted that it is not as much as it sounds like on a bad day and it is more than nothing on a good one, and I do not have a good one today, and I have had nine days in a row of not-good ones and I do not know what to tell you."

She put the pad down and then picked it up again, and he understood that she was going to say something she had decided about at some point between Wednesday and Friday and had been waiting for a reason for.

"There is one more thing and it is a general thing and I am allowed to say it because it is about a book and not about a person." She turned the pad round one more time. "**A case that has been closed does not have an owner.** That is not a complaint and it is not a theory, it is a fact about how a building works, and it is the only fact I have learned in nine years that I would defend in a room. When a case is open it has an officer and the officer is responsible for it and you can go and stand in front of the officer and say *this is yours*. When it is closed it has a number and a store and a retention period and a date on the form when the papers go to be destroyed, and after that date a person in a records office opens a box and does a destruction run, and she is not the person who decided anything, and she does not read them."

"How long is the retention period on a dangerous-working case."

"That is two questions," said Petra Lund.

"That is one question and it is a general one and I have had one and you said you had given it to me and you did not, and I would like the number."

"No," she said. "But I will tell you that the number is printed on the front sheet of the file and not inside it, because a records clerk has to be able to look at a box and know whether she is allowed to open it, and that is the only thing about a case file that is designed to be read by a person who is not the officer, and it is one word, and the word is a number of years, and I have never seen it on a dangerous working and I have seen it on about forty of other things and it is never less than ten."

"Ten years."

"I have said a number that is not an answer," said Petra Lund, "and I would like it noticed that I have said it to a man in a good coat on a Friday morning and that in about four years it will be in a bundle, and I have thought about that between Wednesday and this morning and I have decided I do not care, because if it is in a bundle then a woman wrote it down at a counter in answer to a general question, and that is the best possible place for a number to be, and I have been in this chair for nine years and I have never once been thanked for one."

At about half past eleven he was in a hostel on Tanners Row, because a man who works nights and has lost his room and has not got anywhere to go goes where a man who works nights goes, and that is not a reasoning process, it is a floor and a body.

The desk had a bell on it, a leaflet rack, a coffee machine from about 1994 with a notice taped to it, and a woman of forty-four behind it who had been on that desk for eleven years and who took his coat off him before he had got it out of his hands.

"No," she said.

"I have not said anything."

"You were going to." She hung the coat on a hook. "It is Friday, it is half eleven, and you have come in out of the cold with a face on you, and there is one question people come in here with and I am not answering it and I am telling you that I am not answering it so that you do not have to stand there and decide whether to say it."

"I am not going to say it."

"I know, and thank you, and it is still going to cost you the same." She sat down. "Hester Kwan. Thirty-one beds and a bell on the counter that a person rings for themselves, because I tried the other way for about a month in 2021 and a man of about forty told me in the queue that he did not ring bells for himself at home either, and he was right, and I have never rung one on anybody's behalf since."

"I am not asking about a person. I am asking about a building."

"That is a thing men say."

"It is true in this instance," said Elias Venn, "and I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise, and I am going to tell you one fact about the building and you can do what you like with it. A man who worked nights in a service on Anselm Street has not got a bed in it, and he is fifty-four, and if he came in here at three in the morning after a shift he would take a bed, and if he did not come in here then he is somewhere with a floor."

Hester Kwan looked at the floor for a second, and he watched her decide to say the next part, which she had not been going to say.

"There is a man on my books at the moment who fits that exactly," she said. "He has been in and out since October and he comes in at about half three and he goes out at about nine and he has not paid for a bed since the second week of November and he is not on any scheme I know of, and I have not asked him for anything, because he comes in and puts his hands on the counter and does not want anything."

"How long has he been coming in."

"Since October."

"Then he has a floor," said Elias Venn.

"He has a floor and a card," said Hester Kwan, "and the card is in his coat, and I have known since about November that there is a card, and I have never asked him for it, and I have never asked him for it, and I am telling you that I have never asked him for it, and if you write that down I will know you have written it down."

He wrote it down.

"Why," he said. "You can refuse me, so why does it matter that I write it down."

"Because of the column," said Hester Kwan. She turned round and pulled a large book off the shelf behind her without looking at it, and opened it on the counter, and it was a hardback ledger with ruled columns and about eleven years of entries in four or five different hands, and she put her finger on a column heading. It said **WHERE WENT**.

"It has been empty for eleven years," she said. "It was empty when I came and I filled it in for two years and then I stopped, and I know exactly why I stopped and I have never told anybody why I stopped, and I am not going to tell you why I stopped, and I have just told you that I have never told anybody, and you can work out the rest of it yourself and I would rather you did."

"A man came in with a different name," said Elias Venn.

"A man came in with a different name, and a woman in an office in Calder Street asked me to put down a street, and a house on Cartwright Street in 2023, and I gave all three of them, and in the February of my first winter I was the kind of clerk who thought the reason would be told to me later." She shut the book. "It has not been told to me later. It is going to be a very long time and I am going to keep this desk."

"Somebody could put a name in that column today."

"Anybody could," said Hester Kwan. "**That is why it is empty and not why it should be filled in.**"

He went to 2 Anselm Street at about twenty past eleven on Friday morning because a building where people sleep is a different sort of building on a weekday morning than it had been on a Thursday afternoon, and he wanted to see it in daylight with the day workers in it.

There were three of them. A man of thirty-six at the counter with a mug and a newspaper, who did not look up. A woman of about forty-four in a cardigan over a smock, who was doing the list on a clipboard, and who said good morning before he had said anything, in a voice that was pitched for a hall and not for a stranger.

"Good morning," he said. "Is this the office?"

"It is, and there is nobody in it you have met."

"I have not met anybody anywhere."

"Then you are a man I have not met," said Perpetua Ayodele, "and I am going to be polite to you for about two minutes and then I am going to go back to my list, and I am telling you the two minutes now so that you know when it is over and I am not doing it to be unkind, I am doing it because it is Friday and there are six people in that hall who need beds before four o'clock and there are two of us on until two."

He said thank you and sat down on the second chair and did not take a notebook out.

He had a name, which was the thing he had come for, and he had been told twice in two days that he was not going to get it, and he had a note to leave and no reason to press.

"I am not going to ask you for anything," he said. "I am going to sit here for two minutes and then I am going to go and come back on a different day, and I want to say one thing out loud and then I am going to go back to your list."

"You are going to say it out loud anyway."

"I am," he said. "There is a man who worked nights in this building. I am not going to say his name and I do not have it. I am going to leave a piece of paper on that table with my name and a telephone number on it and nothing else on it, no building, no street, no case number and no reason, and he can ring me or he can not ring me, and either is a complete answer and I am not going to come back a third time."

Perpetua Ayodele wrote on the clipboard and did not look up, and he said the thing he had come to say.

"I have been in four buildings in five months and I have asked about four people and I have been given three of them, and every single time the reason was somebody protecting somebody from a piece of paper, and every single time they were right." He put his hands on his knees. "The paper I am going to leave has my name and a number on it and nothing else. A piece of paper with a man's reason on it is a thing a person finds in a flat in a house, and I am not doing that to a building of forty-one people for one man of fifty-four. If somebody finds it who is not him, they will read two lines and there is nothing in it. If he finds it, he can use it or he can burn it."

"And who signs for it."

"Nobody. I am going to put it on that table and it is going to sit there."

"Everybody in this building signs for everything," said Perpetua Ayodele, and looked up for the first time. "That is the entire reason this building still exists and it is not a system, it is what you do when there is a fire door at the top of a stair and forty-one people sleeping behind it. The person who signs for it is the one on nights, and the one on nights is not me, and I am not going to tell you what the one on nights is called, and that is not a rule in this building, that is me, personally, and I would like you to write down that it was me personally and not a policy."

"Noted."

"Write it down properly, not noted." She went back to the list. "Two minutes."

---

At about a quarter past seven that evening he came back, and the man of thirty-six was awake and drinking tea out of a plastic cup and there were six people in the hall and a television on the wall with the sound off, and a fire door at the top of the stair with a bar across it.

Perpetua Ayodele was not there. The man on nights was.

He was about fifty-four and he had a night's sleep in his face and he came down with a clipboard and did not ask who he was, because in a building like that at seven in the evening you do not.

"I am from the Rowan Trust's open day," he said, and it was a lie, and he said it easily, and Elias Venn let it stand because it was the only door open and because he had said out loud in a hall at half past eleven that he was not going to come back a third time, and this was the second time and it was not the third.

"You want to see the beds."

"I want to leave this." He put a piece of A4 on the counter. His name, a telephone number, and the word *Anselm Street* underlined once, and nothing else. "And I am not going to stand here while you read it. I'll go and sit down."

He sat on the third chair and looked at the wall.

The man on nights read it, and put it face down, and wrote four letters and a time in the top corner of it, and clipped it to the board under the fire notice, and did not take his pen off the board.

"There is no reason on it," he said.

"No."

"Mind."

"No."
The man looked at him for a second longer than the transaction required.

"You are the one who came in on Thursday and counted the chairs," he said.

Elias Venn did not ask how he knew that. He had counted the chairs at about a quarter to four in the afternoon on the Thursday, in the hall, on his way out, and there had been a woman of seventy-eight in the first chair and a man of about thirty-one in the second and a boy of about nine on the floor with a book, and he had counted them because he counts them now.

"Yes," he said.

"Then you will be back," said the man on nights, "and I am not going to tell you anything today, and I want that written down as me being difficult and not as me being frightened, because there is a difference in this building and everybody in it knows the difference and almost nobody outside it does." He put the pen in his pocket. "I do not sleep till about three. And do not come at three, because whatever you are, you will have been standing outside since about one."
