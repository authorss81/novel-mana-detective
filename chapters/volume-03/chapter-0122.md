# Chapter 0122 — A Second Hand in February

Sana Okafor rang him at about ten past six on Monday evening, which she had never done, and she spoke for about two minutes and he wrote one line.

> *I am not asking you to come. I am telling you that I am going to do a thing on Tuesday morning at about eleven in a corridor at 2 Wexford Row and I would like one person in this building at that time who is not on the staff and not the police, and I have decided that it has to be you, and I am telling you the reason instead of asking you for it, because I would not do the second thing to you.*
>
> *The reason is that when I write it, it is going to be on the back of that sheet. And the back of that sheet is in their building, in their frame, in a wooden holder, and in about a year somebody in that company is going to be asked whether the sheet is complete, and the answer will be that it is complete, and the sentence on the back of it will be the only thing on that piece of paper that anybody put there. I have nobody outside my own chain who is not the clinic and not the police. You are the fourth person in six months who has been in that building and you are the only one of the four who has not got a reason to change it.*
>
> *I am not going to discuss it tonight.*

He said all right and she said goodnight and that was the whole of the call.

He did not sleep much. He got to Wexford Row at about twenty to eleven on Tuesday morning and there was a bus stop at the corner that he had used twice in a fortnight and a yard gate standing open and nine bays and a stack of folded chairs under a canopy that he looked at for about a second and a half and then deliberately did not look at, and he had been in this car park in March for two hours and forty-five minutes because a doctor had told him to sit in it.

The corridor outside the day room had a chair in it with a blanket folded over the back of it and a plastic cup on the sill of a window that gave on to a wall about four feet away.

Mavis Dellow was on the chair.

"You have come," she said. "I said you would."

"I have not been asked to."

"You have not been told not to," said Mavis Dellow, "which is a different thing and I have been in a building a long time and I know the difference, and do not stand over me, because if you stand over me then you are the one who is asking."

He sat down on the floor against the wall opposite, at the other end of the corridor, with about nine feet between them, and put his hands on his knees.

Sana Okafor came out of a room at about eleven, saw them both, said nothing, and went to the wooden holder on the wall by the door and took the sheet out.

It was ruled in days and it was a working document and it was the operator's. On the front of it, in a second and heavier hand than the rest, in February, there were three lines and a time against one of them and no reason against any of them. A transfer out of that room at a quarter to six in the afternoon. A transfer back into it the same day with no time on it. And a third line that was a correction of the second, in the same pen, on the same afternoon, and the correction said the same thing a second time.

"What I want to say about those three," said Sana Okafor, "is that they are not a record of a night. I want to say it before anybody else says it, because if nobody says it then they will be a record of a night in about eleven months when somebody is looking for one."

"It looked like a record of a night," said Elias Venn.

"It looked like one because I have told you it was one." She turned the sheet so he could see the front. "That is a working sheet and it is written up at the end of a shift, and the operator's end of a shift is not the same as the ward's end of a shift, and the ward finishes at eleven at night and this building's office finishes at six, and the office writes the day's sheet up on the Friday for the Monday. That is the whole of it. The transfer was in daylight, at a quarter to six in the afternoon on the eleventh of February, in front of everybody on that floor, and somebody carried her out of a warm room into a cold one and put her back again two hours later, and there is a piece of paper in this city that would record that, and it is not this piece of paper."

"Why not."

"Because **the operator's form has no box on it for a reason.**" She said it without any heat. "There is a box for a bed, and a box for a band, and a box for a care need, and a box for a date of review, and eleven of them were filled in last November by a man who is not a nurse and who did it well. There is no box that says why a person was moved. There has never been a box that says why a person was moved. So a person was moved on the eleventh of February, and somebody wrote it down, and there was nowhere on the paper to write down the part that matters, and so it is not there, and no one has done anything wrong, and that is worse."

Mavis Dellow had not moved and had been listening with her head turned.

"Heart," she said. "It was my heart, and it was a Wednesday, and I was in a chair in this corridor and I asked to go back in because I wanted my tea, and the day girl did it on a trolley and the office wrote it down after. **Nobody has ever written down that it was my heart. Nobody has ever asked.**"

"Right," said Sana Okafor.

"You are going to write it down now."

"No," said Sana Okafor. "I am going to write down what you asked me to write down on the twentieth of March and nothing else, and I told you then that if you wanted nothing then I would write nothing, and this is the same, and you are not getting the tea out of me and you are not getting anything else either."

---

The charge nurse's desk was at the other end of the corridor and had a telephone on it that had a piece of paper taped to the cradle with four numbers on it, and it was not a ward telephone, it was a corridor telephone, and it had a rule about it and the rule was that it was for people who could not get to the ward.

Delphine Otieno was fifty and had been in that building twelve years and was on the desk until two, and she came out at about twenty to twelve and stood at the holder on the wall and looked at the back of the sheet, and did not touch it either.

"That has been on the back of that sheet since March," she said. "Two things, now."

"Two things."

"One — and I am not saying this to be clever, I am saying it because there is nobody in this building who can say it who is not me and I have been waiting since January for somebody to ask me." She put her thumb on the frame of the wooden holder, not on the paper. "**That sheet is not a record of the operator's business. It is a working sheet, and it goes to head office on a Monday, and the ward's own sheet goes in the ward's file, and there are two sets of paper in this building about the same person on the same day and they do not agree with each other and never have.** I wrote to them about it in 2028. I got a reply in about five weeks and the reply said, and I have it, and it says that the ward attendance sheet is an internal nursing document and is not a record of the operator's business and is not a record of the premises."

"And the sentence on the back of it."

"Is on an internal nursing document," said Delphine Otieno, "and it is not a record of the operator's business, and it is not a record of the premises, and it is going to go to head office on Monday with a piece of paper that says *patient reports* across the top of it, and there is nobody in this building and nobody in that company and nobody in this city who is going to be able to tell me otherwise."

"Does that matter."

"It matters to you it does not matter to me," said the nurse, "and I know that is a hard thing to sit in a corridor and say to somebody. **I have sat on this desk for twelve years and I have put four thousand people in and four thousand people out and I have a page for every single one of them and not one page in this building has ever been in front of anybody who could be asked about it.**"

Sana Okafor did not answer that and did not look at her and said, "Thank you," once, in a flat voice, and the charge nurse went back to the desk, and the telephone with the four numbers taped to the cradle was not used while either of them was in the corridor.

---

She turned the sheet over at about a quarter past eleven.

The back of it was blank. It had been blank since the sheet had been printed, and there was nothing on it at all, and it was still blank apart from one sentence and the conditions under it in a doctor's hand from the twentieth of March, about a foot from the bottom edge.

She wrote with a biro out of her own bag because there was no pen case in that corridor.

> *I do not know.*
>
> *Written on the back of this sheet on 26 March 2030 at 2 Wexford Row at the request of M. Dellow, who asked for a doctor to write that she does not know whether it matters, and who knows, and who does not know, and who has not been given the date of the night. Nothing else was written. S. Okafor.*

Mavis Dellow had to get up to read it, which took her a while, and she read it twice, and she put her finger under the four words and then took it away again.

"That is four words and a date," she said, "and it is the first thing anybody has written down in this building that I have asked for, and I have been in here since the seventh of February."

"Yes."

"And you put a name under it."

"I always put a name under it. That is the whole of what a document is." Sana Okafor said it flatly. "**The four words are yours and the name is mine and I have put them both on the same piece of paper on purpose, because a piece of paper with four words on it and nobody's name on it is a rumour, and I am not having a rumour in a ward.**"

"Will it go in a file."

"It will go in a file when somebody copies that sheet, and somebody will copy that sheet, and on the copy it will say *patient reports* or it will say *nursing observation* or it will say nothing at all, and by then it will be a different sentence and it will not be yours and it will not be mine, and that will happen in about a year, and I am telling you that now on purpose so that you are not lied to by it later."

Mavis Dellow sat with that for a bit.

"Then write it on the wall," she said.

"The wall is theirs."

"The wall is plaster, and I have been on a chair in front of it since the seventh of February, and the holder is on the wall and the sheet is in the holder and the back of the sheet is the part that has never been used, and there is a space above the door with nothing in it." She stopped. "No. That is me wanting a thing at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday because somebody has just given me four words. **Put it in the holder. That is where it goes. I would rather it is a sheet in a holder than a thing on a wall, and I have thought about that for a full minute and I am not going to change my mind.**"

Sana Okafor put the sheet back in the wooden holder with the back of it showing, the way she had put it back on the twentieth, and the front of it carried the three February lines and nothing else that had happened since the twentieth of February.

Elias Venn had been sitting on the floor against the opposite wall for about an hour and had not asked anything at all, which was new for him and which was the first thing he had actually noticed about himself since the sixth of March.

He had one question. He had had it since the Wednesday, and it had been sitting in him the way a thing sits in you when you know you cannot ask it and you keep the shape of it anyway.

The operator's day room has a day staff and a night porter and no night rota, because a licensed day ward is not open at night. There was a door on that corridor that was on a latch at ten to ten on the evening of the twenty-second of January, and somebody came through it, and there were three people in the room, and one of them said sorry, love, twice, to somebody who was not her.

He had worked out, in nine weeks, in about four minutes at a time, that the only way anybody in this city could ever tell him which door that was would be to ask three women of seventy-one, eighty-one and sixty-nine to write down the same sentence, and then to put the three sentences beside the writing of a police report, and to look.

He was not going to do it. He was not going to do it because he could not read a person, and a woman of eighty-one had told him a thing was not there and he believed her, and because the two rules on the back of a delivery note in a laboratory in Saint Orra Reach were in his coat drawer in a laboratory and not in his pocket and they were in his head, and because a woman of thirty-eight in a corridor had said *they are kind about it, that is what I want said*, and because in nine weeks nobody had asked him to do anything and he had not done anything, and doing that one thing would have been the end of all of it.

"Sana," he said.

"No."

"I have not asked you."

"You were going to ask me whether the operator keeps a night rota, and then you were going to ask me whether the day staff can describe the writing in a report from the twenty-second of January, and then you were going to say that you were not going to do anything with it." She had not looked up. "**It is the third conversation we have had about that night and it is the first one where you did not want me to give you something.**"

"Is it."

"It is," said Sana Okafor. "And I want to say one thing about it and then I am going to go and do the rounds, and I am going to say it because I have been in this building for a fortnight of Tuesdays and I have never had anybody thank me and I am not going to start."

She went into the day room and shut the door, which is a door that shuts.

---

Nobody in that corridor had told Elias Venn not to go into the day room. In March a doctor had told him, in a corridor at Vine Street, in four numbered rules, and the second rule was that he did not get to have that conversation, and he had said it back to her. Nobody had said anything of the kind to him on Tuesday the twenty-sixth of March, and he had sat on a floor for an hour with a question in his mouth and had not asked it, and he understood that the rule had stopped being a thing that was done to him somewhere in the last three weeks and had become a thing he was doing, and that this was worse, because a rule that somebody else is keeping can be argued with and a rule you are keeping can only be obeyed.

At about ten past one Sana Okafor came back out and stood at the holder on the wall and looked at the back of the sheet for about four seconds without touching it.

"She is going to ask me again," she said. "Not today. She will ask me in about three weeks whether the four words are still on the page, and I am going to say yes, and she is going to say that is the first time anybody has told her that a thing was still there."

The blanket was folded on the back of the chair in the corridor and there was nobody on it, and it had not been folded since the seventh of February, when a woman of sixty-nine had gone and sat on it and had not been able to say the sentence she had come to say, and it was the first time since the seventh of February that the corridor had had nobody in it at ten to one in the afternoon.

Nobody said anything about that either.
