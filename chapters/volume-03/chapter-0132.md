# Chapter 0132 — A Tray That Was Heavy

Wren Court was at the eleventh house on Calder Street and he had walked past it twice in March without reading the board, and the board said *Wren Court · Residential and nursing care · Enquiries 9 to 5*, and under that in smaller letters, *Registered provider: Standish Care Limited*, and a telephone number.

He had walked past it twice because he was looking for a garage.

He had a telephone number for the woman in his own book in his own hand and he had had it since Monday, and it was a Calder Street number and the building was four minutes' walk from where he was standing, and he had not rung it, and the reason he had not rung it was that a woman who gives a number to a stranger at a counter is being traceable and not being called, and he had understood that in the garage on Monday afternoon and he was not going to lose it on a Tuesday morning.

The porch had been a shop. You could see where the counter had been in the shape the floor had gone shiny in a rectangle, and the door had a bell on it, and there was a fire door at the end of the corridor past the desk that had a bar across it and a wedge of wood under it, and a lift that was a chair on a cable and a sign over it saying *please do not use the lift for anyone who cannot get to the stairs safely*, which is the most frightening sentence in the building.

The desk was in what had been the window, and there was a woman at it of about sixty-one with a cardigan and a telephone and a rack on the wall behind her with pegs on it.

"Can I help you."

"I am not here to enquire about a bed," said Elias Venn. "I am here about a piece of paper, and I am going to tell you that up front so that you do not spend the next ten minutes finding out whether I have come about a person or about a price."

"Then I shall not ask you the price either, because you have told me and I am not interested in it." She put her pen down. "Pamela Reece. I have been on this desk twenty-six years and I do the day side, and the day side is enquiries and admissions and telephone, and the night side is nurses, and there are no nurses on this desk at any hour of any day and if anybody tells you otherwise then they have been in a different building."

"What I want to know about is the week of the twenty-fifth of January."

Pamela Reece's hand went about an inch towards a drawer and stopped.

"Which week of the twenty-fifth of January."

"The twenty-fifth of January to the thirty-first."

"That is a week, and you have given me both ends of it. **Now, before I say anything else, you are going to tell me whether you are a relative, because if you are a relative then I can say about nine things to you and if you are not then I can say about one, and the one is that the forms for that week went up to head office and came back and I am not allowed to say what is on them.**"

"I am not a relative."

"Then I am not allowed to say what is on them," said Pamela Reece, "and I am going to say something else instead, and I want it understood that I am saying it because you did not pretend."

"Go on."

"**We send the week's admissions up on the Friday in a tray. Not the week. The forms. The forms go in a tray and the tray goes out on the Friday on the bus, because the bus is cheaper than a van, and it comes back on the Monday, and in between there is a person in an office in another town who is required to have read every line of every form and is required to have initialled it, and that is the whole of the arrangement and it is a good arrangement and I have never known it to fail.**"

"Then the forms for that week went."

"They went and they came back and **the tray was heavy**, and I remember the tray, because the tray is a grey plastic tray and it weighs about nothing with four forms in it and it weighs a great deal with nine, and I have carried that tray eleven times in twenty-six years and I remember every one of them, and the reason that one was heavy is that the week's admissions were nine and the ordinary week is four or five."

"Nine admissions in a week."

"Nine, and I have said the number out loud and I have said it because you have come in on a Tuesday and told me you were not here about a bed, and **I am not going to be the one who decides what you are here about.**"

She got up. She was not fast and she did not want help.

"Four of them were from a hospital and one of them was from a house and the other four I have got here on the file, and I am not showing you the file, because the file is the file, and there is a difference and you would not enjoy being told about it by a police officer nearly as much as I would not enjoy saying it to one."

"I am not a police officer."

"You are a man in a good coat in a hall with a smell of this building coming off you, and you have told me the week you are interested in before you have told me your name, and I have decided you are somebody's solicitor or you are nobody, and either way you get the form and not the file." She went through a door and came back with a plastic wallet and a bundle of four sheets clipped at the corner, and she put the bundle on the desk and squared it. "**Take that off the peg and look at it. That is not a favour. That is a form. Anybody in this city may read a form. If we had a copy of everybody's birth certificate on this desk it would be the same, and I have said that sentence out loud twice in twenty-six years and both times to a man in a good coat.**"

He read it standing at the counter.

It was one side of A4 and the printing was four years old.

> **ADMISSION RECORD — WREN COURT**
> NAME
> DATE OF ADMISSION
> BED
> REASON FOR ADMISSION
> ASSISTANCE REQUIRED — (three boxes) INDEPENDENT / ONE PERSON / TWO PEOPLE
> PREVIOUS ADDRESS
> PREVIOUS PROVIDER
> DISCHARGE DESTINATION
> SIGNATURE OF PERSON COMPLETING

Nine headed spaces. He looked at it for about twenty seconds.

"Which one of these is who arranged it."

"None of them," said Pamela Reece.

"That is not an answer, that is a list."

"That is an answer, and I have given it to three people in twenty-six years and the first two of them asked me to check the other side and there is no other side, and the third one was a man from a charity who wanted to know who had signed a form away from his own relative and I told him the same thing, and he asked me to write it down, and I said no, and he went away quite cross." She put her finger on the counter beside the form. "**The form can name a company. PREVIOUS PROVIDER, and a company has a name and a registration and a book and you can ring it, and if you ring it they will tell you about the bed and the week and the fee and they will do it inside a year because that is what they are for. And there is no box anywhere on this piece of paper for a person, and there is no box anywhere in this building, and it is not because anybody decided to leave it off in 2026 when we got the printing. I have asked for one. I asked in 2019 and a woman in another town wrote back and said that the form is an administrative record of a placement and a person is not an administrative record of a placement, and she was right, and I have put that letter in the drawer and it is the only letter in the drawer.**"

"Then how does anybody get here."

"By walking in, or by coming up off a hospital list, or by somebody telephoning me, and about four times in twenty-six years by a man on a bicycle." She said it flatly. "**I have a telephone and a book. That is all I have. There is no referral form, there is no waiting list, there is no panel and there is nobody to approve anybody. A person who telephones me and wants a bed and I have a bed, they have a bed, and if you do that the wrong thing happens about twice a year and both times it has turned out to be somebody's husband or somebody's daughter who had a bad fortnight and did not know what else to do.**"

"That is not authorisation."

"No," said Pamela Reece. "**It is not authorisation and I have never once pretended to anybody that it was, and I will tell you the sentence I have said in this chair for twenty-six years, which is that the desk decides and the desk is a person, and if you want it not to be a person then you take the desk away and give it to a form, and I do not think anybody in this building wants to give it to a form.**"

She sat down again, and there was a bell on the counter, and it went.

It was a man of about seventy with a woman of about forty and it was not a relative. Pamela Reece said three sentences to him and gave him a piece of paper and he went out again, and the whole of it took about ninety seconds, and it was the ninth of the week, and it was nothing at all.

"You said nine," said Elias Venn.

"I said nine. He is one of the four I have on the file and he came from a house and he has been here since Friday." She did not look at the file. "**You did not ask me which one and I am going to point out that you did not ask me which one, and I have been on this desk for twenty-six years and in this city that is not a small thing and I have noticed it and I am not going to make a speech about it.**"

"Those nine marks down the margin of that form," he said. "Head office has to initial every one of them. Whose initials are they."

"**That is the best question you have asked me and it is the first one anybody has asked me in about nine years, and I am going to answer it properly, which is that I do not know, and not because they are secret.** They are not a signature. They are a mark, and a mark is a person saying *I have read this line*, and reading a line is not the same as writing it, and so nobody at head office is ever the author of an admission form, and every year I write to them and ask them to initial the whole page so that the page has one name on it, and every year they write back and say that would defeat the purpose."

"What is the purpose."

"**That anybody at any point in the next eleven years can be proved to have read it, and cannot be proved to have written it.**" She said it the way she said everything, which was as though she were reading it off a card. "That is a genuinely clever arrangement and I have never in twenty-six years seen it go wrong, and it has also meant that in nine years there has not been one single piece of paper in this building with the name of a member of staff at the end of a line, and there would not be one if the building caught fire tonight, and I have not been able to work out whether that is a good thing or the worst thing about working here."

Elias Venn stood at the counter with a form in his hand and felt the building. That was the whole of it and it was not a faculty and it was not a reading and it was not anything he could put in a book. The building was warm in a way that buildings are warm when there are people in them, and there were people in it, and on Monday the twenty-eighth of January a woman had telephoned a removals firm off her own book and paid cash at the door for a bed in it for eleven nights, and he did not know whether the man who slept in that bed was upstairs at that moment or in a hostel in Kettle Rows or in a room over a chip shop, and there was no document anywhere in this city that said, and there never had been, and that was not anybody's doing and would never be anybody's fault.

He could have asked. There was a woman on the desk, and a file, and nine admissions, and a piece of paper on the wall with a bed in it, and he had nine words in a chip shop and a man with a coat pocket and a line in a garage office that said **GAYNOR RIDDELL**, and if he had asked one more question, in the right tone, on a Tuesday, in a chair, to a woman of sixty-one who had told him things twice that she had never told a police officer twice, he could have found out in about ninety seconds where a man is who is not lost.

He did not ask. The chapter does not say why, and neither does he, and there is a difference between the two and it is the difference between a person who cannot and a person who is not going to.

---

The stair went up past the day office to a landing with four doors and a fire door at the end of it, and on the wall opposite the stair, at eye level, there was a board.

It was a piece of hardboard about two foot by three, painted over at some point in a colour that had been white, and it had been used for something else first, and there was **a ruled line ruled in pencil for every name that went into the building**, and the pencil had been gone over twice in about four years, and beside each line there was a surname and a first initial and a length of stay. Elias Venn stood on a landing and looked at the lines and did not count them, and understood as he stood there that not counting them was the hardest thing he had done in six weeks.

He stopped there for about four seconds, and then he went back down the stairs.

Pamela Reece had come out of the desk and was standing in the corridor with her cardigan off over one arm and she had plainly been going to the linen store.

"**You went back down,**" she said.

"I did."

"Then I am going to say one thing and I am going to go to the linen store, and I am not going to say it twice, and I have said it once in twenty-six years and that was to a magistrate who did not need it. **Every name on that board is on a form, and the form is on a pegboard in that corridor, and a form is a form, and a name is a name, and the two of them are not the same object and never have been and there is not one piece of paper in this building that has ever been a record of a person, only a record of an admission.**"

"I noticed."

"I noticed you noticing," said Pamela Reece, "and I noticed that you are a man who reads notices, because you have read the four on this wall and the four on my desk and the twenty on the pegboard in eleven minutes, and you have not asked me a single one of the four things I have had ready since you came in." She put the cardigan over her arm. "**You have been in this building for forty minutes and you have not asked me a single question about a person and I would like you to know that I noticed, and that it is going to be in my head at four o'clock, and that I do not know yet what to do with it.**"

Then she went to the linen store, and the corridor was quiet, and a lift went past overhead going down.

---

He went down the corridor to the day room because a corridor is a place to walk in a building that has nothing else for you, and the day room was at the end of it with the door open and a television on with the sound off.

There were nine chairs. He counted them, and it was nine, and he was aware of the number for the rest of the afternoon in the way he had been aware of numbers since the twenty-second of January, and he did not use the number for anything, and nothing in the rest of the day was connected to it, and he did not say one word about it to anybody and there was nobody in the room.

On the wall by the door there was a pegboard, and on the pegboard were the week's forms, clipped at the corners, and eleven of them, and the eleventh was the week of the twenty-fifth of January and it was still on the board, and it had been initialled along the left margin in nine places in one hand, and in the seventh place down there was a tick, and the tick was in a different ink.

Elias Venn looked at it for a while.

"It is not one of ours," said Pamela Reece, from the desk, forty feet away, without being asked.

"I did not say anything."

"You have been looking at the same tick for about ten seconds, which in this corridor is a question, and I have watched men look at things in this corridor for twenty-six years." She stood up. "**And no, I cannot tell you whose hand it is, and I am not being difficult, and I would say that to anybody including a magistrate. I have worked in this building for twenty-six years and I have signed about four thousand things and I could not tell you a colleague's handwriting if it were in front of me, and I would not know it if it were my own from November, because we all write the same nine letters and we all learned the same nine letters off each other in about 2011.**"

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it," said Pamela Reece. "**Somebody in head office went through nine forms with a pen of their own and a head office is four counties away and I have met one of them, in 2019, and I could not tell you now what she looked like.**"
