# Chapter 0120 — A Book With Two Names In It

He was standing outside a housing options office on a parade at about eleven on Friday the twenty-second of March 2030 with four things written on a page in his own book, and the four things were the four hands, and he had never written them down in that shape before and he wrote them in a different order from the one they had happened in, on purpose, because he wanted to be looking at a queue and not at a story.

> **One. A second officer, who produced a compiled extract in an office in a building in Kettle Rows, at about ten to ten at night, on the eleventh of December 2029.** He put a piece of paper in front of a man and the man read it three times.
>
> **Two. A supervisor, who told a sergeant at twenty-five to seven in the evening on the twelfth of December that the matter was closed.** Four words, in a way that is not a document, from a telephone that was in a room, and a telephone is not anywhere else afterwards.
>
> **Three. A records clerk, who signed a file into a store at about a quarter to seven the same evening.** A signature on a form. The form is in a store. The person who signed it has signed about four hundred of them and has not been asked about one.
>
> **Four. About four people, who are permitted to be in the room when that form is signed, and one of the four has told me there is no third thing a store does.**

He had written *about four* on the fourth line and he had underlined it, and the underline was the only underline on the page, and he had put it there in February and had not taken it away.

---

The housing options office had a blue sign, a waiting area with eleven chairs in it, a counter with a screen, a back door onto a yard, and a man of about thirty-six behind the counter in a fleece with the Royal National at the top of the list and nothing under it, and there were two people on the chairs and neither of them was waiting for him.

Elias Venn had got there by asking. He had asked at four counters between nine o'clock and a quarter to eleven, and three of them had told him that it was a matter for an office and not for them, and the fourth had told him that people whose cases had been closed did not come back to a housing options office, they went somewhere else, and had not told him where, and had said, *and if I told you it would be me that gets the call*.

He stood at the counter for about two minutes.

"I am not here about housing and I have not got a case and I am not going to pretend to have one. I am a member of the public and I have spent six months finding out what happens to a person when a case is closed against them, and I have got as far as four hands and a signature, and the signature is the end of it. **I am looking for the person that a person goes and finds when a case is closed.**"

Ray Tindall put down a pen.

"Right," he said. "I am going to tell you three things and you are going to hate all three, and I have been at this counter four years and I have never once been asked for any of them, and I have got a queue of two and neither of them is here about anything."

"Go on."

"One. **I do not know where they go.** Two. **Everybody who comes in here and asks me that has been somebody whose landlord has stopped paying for something, and not one of them has been a person whose case was closed, and I have never once seen a person come in this door for that reason, and I have asked two colleagues about it and one of them said it had happened and would not say when.**" He looked at the two people on the chairs. "Three, and this is the one I have thought about. They do not come in the front door. **They come in the back door, and they come in on a Friday, and it is always a Friday, and I have never worked out why it is a Friday, and it is not because of the weekend, because a person who has just had a case closed does not want a weekend.**"

"Why do you say they come in the back door."

"Because the back door is the yard door and the yard is where the bins are and there is no sign on the yard door," said Ray Tindall, "and a person who has been in a police station once does not want to walk in past a sign in a parade. I have had about a dozen of them. I have not written any of it down. There is no form. **I have got a drawer of forms in the back office and there are about four hundred in it and there is not one of them for this, and I have looked, and I am not a police officer and I am not a doctor and I am thirty-six and I have got a counter.**"

He looked at the door and then at the yard behind it, through the screen.

"You are the first person who has come in and asked me for a person," he said, "and you are the first person who has come in and not wanted me to give you a name, and I am going to tell you a thing, and I am telling you because I have been at this counter four years and I have been right about about nine things and I have never told one of them out loud."

"Go on."

"**I can tell you where a woman with a book of first names lives, and I am telling you, and I am telling you because there is nobody else in this city who would tell you, and I would like somebody to have said it out loud in a room before somebody comes and asks me about it in a corridor.**"

Elias Venn did not move.

"What woman."

"A woman of about sixty-three. She was a ward sister, and I know that because a woman who was in a place with me at about nineteen said so, about two years ago, on a Tuesday, and I have not seen her since." Ray Tindall spoke to the screen. "**She has a house. It is a terrace and it is not a nice one and it is four streets from a parade like this one, and people go there, and I have sent four people to that door in four years and I have never been inside it and I have never seen a form come out of it and I have never been asked to put anything in an envelope about it.**"

"How do you know it is a house and not an office."

"Because it is a house," said Ray Tindall. "There is a woman of sixty-three in it with a hardback book and she writes people's first names in it and she does not write the other one, and I have known that for about two years and I have never asked her why, and I have worked out why on my own, in a queue, in about four minutes, and here it is: **a surname is the first thing an institution asks for.** If you turn up with nothing but a first name then you are a person and not a record, and there is no form in this city that can do anything at all to a person who is not a record. And if you turn up with a surname then you are four seconds away from being one, and everybody who has ever come to that house has been somebody who had lost the only copy of the thing that had their surname on it."

He put the pen down.

"And it is not an institution and she is not paid and I have never seen any money move, and I have sent four people there and I have never once been asked to send anybody and I have never told anybody. And you are the first person I have said it to, and I have said it to a man in a fleece with no name on it, which means I have said it to a man with no name on it, and if you have come from a building with a name on the door then I have made a mistake and I do not care."

"The address."

Ray Tindall looked at him for about three seconds and then wrote it on the back of a form and pushed it across the counter with two fingers, and did not read it out, and did not say the street name, and did not say it again when Elias Venn said thank you.

Elias Venn did not look at it. He folded it once and put it in the inside pocket of his coat next to a plastic wallet and next to a piece of A4 with a name and a number on it, and he was aware, for about the fourth time in five months, that the inside pocket of that coat had become a place where things went that he could not account for, and he did not say anything about it, and the man behind the counter saw him not say anything and nodded as though he had been given something.

---

He did not go to the address on the Friday.

He stood outside a parade for about a quarter of an hour after that with the folded paper in the inside pocket of his coat and then he walked about two miles, deliberately, in the wrong direction, and then he came back, because he had worked out on the walk what he was doing, and what he was doing was buying himself the rest of the afternoon so that he would not arrive at that door at two o'clock on a Friday having found it on a Friday, which is what anybody in this city does when they are frightened and about nine of them have been frightened about things in this city.

He said the thing out loud on a low wall at about a quarter past one, to a dog, which was wrong, and then again to the wall, which was not much better, and then he wrote it in his own book, because it was the only way he had ever found of saying a thing and meaning it.

> *What I have learned in five months, and it is one sentence and it took me five months: a case that has been closed does not have an owner, and a person whose case has been closed goes and finds somebody who will give them a name, and that somebody is not an institution and is not paid and keeps a book of first names and no surnames because a surname is the first thing an institution asks for.*
>
> *And the second half, which is the half I did not know until a woman of thirty-four found a form that was not there: a person who has not been given a form is a person this city has not finished with, and a person who has not been given a form is also a person nobody can refuse, because a refusal is a thing you put in a box, and there is no box.*

Then he wrote a third line, which he had not intended to write, and looked at it.

> *I have lost nothing in five months.*

He read that three times. It was true, and he knew the three things that had been true about it — the left ear, the room with eleven chairs in it, and about four minutes of somebody else's grief that he had gone and got out of a book in a refuge in Kettle Rows and had told nobody about — and he wrote *nothing* again underneath it, and underlined it, and did not write what it had cost, because he could not put a number on it and because a man who puts a number on a room stops being able to be in the room.

Then he said it out loud, once, on the low wall, to the dog, which had come back.

"I have lost nothing in five months, and I am going to go and use it."

---

The box on the corner of the parade had a door on it and a list of four numbers on a card inside the glass and it worked, and the line was dead, and then it was not.

"Eleias Venn." He spelled it. It is spelled with an E and he had been spelling it with an E for eight years.

"Nadia Kest."

"You said to ring you and say one word, and I have not said it and I have not rung you, and I have had it in my pocket for sixteen days, and I am ringing you at a quarter to two on a Friday from a box on a corner and I am going to say the word and I am not going to say anything else, and if you ask me one further question about it I will not be able to answer it and we will both have to live with that."

There was a pause of about a second and a half, and then a door closed on the other end of it, in a corridor, at a quarter to two on a Friday afternoon.

"**Asked,**" said Elias Venn.

Nothing.

Nadia Kest said nothing. She said nothing for long enough that he could hear a corridor change and a stair and then a second corridor, and then she said, "I have not got a question," and stopped, and he waited, and she did not add anything to it, and she had promised on the sixth of March that she would not ask him one further question about it for as long as she lived, and she was keeping it, and the keeping of it took about nine seconds of a line with nothing at the end of it.

That was all. There was no instruction and there was no anger and there was not one word about the fortnight, and at the end of it he said "thank you" and she said "go and do it" and the line went.

He stood in a box on the corner of a parade for about a minute and forty seconds with the receiver in his hand, and he was aware, the whole time, that he had not said a true thing, because he had been asked on the sixth of March to ask somebody with a warrant and a good coat who was allowed into a building, and he had not, and a man of about thirty-six behind a counter on a Friday afternoon was not that, and the word had gone down a telephone to a woman who had told him the word was enough, and he had made it mean something smaller so that he could say it, and he had done that on purpose, and he had known he was doing it, and it was the first time in five months that he had put a thing into a sentence knowing it was not the whole of the sentence.

He had said the word and she had not asked and he was not going to ring her back.

He put the receiver down on its hook and the box swallowed the click, and the parade was there.

He stood on the pavement of a parade at half past two on a Friday afternoon and let the last of the afternoon go, and did not go in and find out, and that was the hardest thing he had not done in six months, and there was nobody there to see him not do it.

Four streets away, off a parade that looked exactly like this one, there was a house with a hardback book in it, and a woman of sixty-three in it, and a first name on a page and no surname anywhere on it. And he had stood outside a door for six months and he had got an address, and he was not going to knock on it on a Friday afternoon in the same week he found it.

The reason was not caution and it was not discipline and it was not one of the two rules written on the back of a delivery note in a laboratory. It was that a man who knocks on a door two hours after a man in a fleece has told him the address has not come to the door, he has been sent, and there is a difference between the two, and in five months he had not worked out which of the two he was, and he was going to take until Monday to find out.

He had told nobody that either. The man in the fleece had written it on the back of a form and had not read it out, and would go home at half past four and lock a blue door behind him and not think about it again until somebody came, and there would be no name on the form but his, and it was the eleventh piece of paper in five months that had come into his own hand out of a stranger's, and he was not going to spend it on a Friday.
