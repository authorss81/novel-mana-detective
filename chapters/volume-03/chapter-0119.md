# Chapter 0119 — A Refusal That Is Neither

This is neither of the two things this city does to people who come asking. It is not a refusal on the ground that you are the wrong person, and it is not a refusal on the ground that what you want is not good enough. Nobody in Vine Street has looked at whether it is good enough. What has happened at about twenty to ten on Thursday the twenty-first of March 2030 is that a woman of thirty-four with a stamp pad and four years in the building has established that there is no box in the world for what he is carrying, and has been kind about it, and has told him so, and has spent eleven minutes of her own time going and looking.

He wrote that down at about half past nine that morning, standing up, in a waiting room with eight chairs in it.

---

The waiting room outside the magistrates' chambers at Vine Street has eight chairs, four of them with the vinyl gone off the arm, a table with a jug of water and about nine paper cups, a radiator that knocks, and a notices board with nine hooks on it and a card over the top hook.

**Forms available at the counter. Please ask.**

Elias Venn had counted the chairs when he came in and had counted them again from the far side and it was eight both times, and it was not eleven, and he wrote *8* in his own book and then looked at the number for a second and put a line through it, because he was not going to be a man with a book of chair numbers.

The eight other hooks were bare, and under them, screwed to the plaster, there was a small wooden tray with a card clipped to the front of it, and the card said **TAKE ONE**, and the tray had about sixty forms in it in three sizes with a rubber band round each stack.

That was where the woman of fifty-two from the third chair got her form on the Tuesday, and the woman with the crutches from the fourth, and the man of about thirty who had a hearing at eleven and had been in at half past eight.

Elias Venn stood in front of the tray for about ninety seconds and read the top form of each stack. There was one for a person who wished to be notified of the result of a hearing, one for a person who wished to be addressed by something other than a name, one for the conduct of a hearing in a language other than the language of the court, one for the conduct of a hearing behind a screen, and about fifty that were the same sheet of paper with a different form number printed at the top and a different box ticked in the middle.

"There is a form for a person who wants to be called something else on the day," he said. "I have seen that form. It is in a police station four miles from here, and I have watched a woman behind a counter there read a form out loud to a man and turn the book round so that he could read it back, and I have never once seen anybody take this one."

"Then she is ahead of us," said Colette Marr, "and she is welcome to it, and it is a good form, and there are eleven of them in that tray and they are all the same sheet with a number on it, and in four years I have never once seen anybody take one."

He counted the stacks. There were three. He did not count the forms, because he was not going to be a man who counted a tray of forms in a corridor, and he had got to the point in his life where he would leave a tray alone.

"There is a clerk on the public counter called Ines Kolar," said Colette Marr, "and she is the one who put that card there, and she has not got a box for it either, and she will tell you the same thing I have told you and she will say it faster."

"Then why are you telling me and not her."

"Because she will tell you it is a matter for the court, and it is not. It is a matter for a drawer, and drawers are mine."

"There is a woman of thirty-three in a police station who says the same thing," said Elias Venn, "and she has nine hundred forms in a drawer, and she has told me that there is no drawer in this city with in it a form for a person's account being different from a document, and that she has been in nine of the eleven buildings that have a drawer in them."

Colette Marr looked at him for a second.

"Then there are two of us, and I have never met her," she said, "and I am going to write her office on a pad in this book tonight, and I am not going to tell you that I have, and I am going to do it about a year after she has stopped expecting anything, because that is when a counter assistant has about nine minutes."


"There is a woman coming," Colette Marr said, from the hatch, at about ten to ten. "You are third."

"I am not here about anything I have filed. I have not filed anything. I have come to ask you a general question and you are allowed to say that you are not allowed to answer it, and I am going to say the rest of it before you do, because I have learned that it saves about forty minutes."

"Then I am not going to be able to help you and I have got four minutes before she comes." But she did not go.

"I am asking about a name. There is a box on a public document, in a district office across the city, with a post in it and no name in it, and on Monday a registrar-counsel told me in about nine seconds that she was not going to put a name in it, and she was right, and I have come to find out whether a member of the public can get one."

"No."

"Then I would like to know why, and I have been told the answer twice by two people who are competent and who disagree about the wording, and I would like it in the mouth of somebody who has to go and look it up in a book while I wait."

Colette Marr put the stamp pad down.

"**What is the name for,**" she said.

Elias Venn did not answer for a moment.

"That is not a reason."

"That is the question the office asks and I am the one who has to write it down," said Colette Marr, "and I have written it down about two hundred times in four years, and I am not going to write down *a man stood in a corridor and said nothing useful*, because the day somebody reads this book in twenty years, in a hearing I will not be at, the whole of what I do is in these two hundred lines. So: what is the name for."

"I do not know. I have come to find out and I have not found out, because there is nothing to find out with."

"Right," said Colette Marr, and wrote something on a pad, and turned it round so that he could see it, which was her way and it was not her way, she did that at a different counter in a different building and he had never once seen her do it. Then she turned it back and crossed out what she had written and wrote a different thing, and looked at it for a moment.

"Now I am going to tell you the whole of it, and I am going to tell you because you asked and because you said what you were not going to do, and I have got four minutes and this is the only interesting thing that has happened to me this week." She put the pen down. "**A registrar's name is not protected. That is not why I cannot give it to you. The name is on a document that anybody may inspect, and it is printed on the front, and it is not a secret and there is no box on any form in this city that says *keep this name quiet*.** What a name is, is that it is a *reference*, and a reference is only worth something to a person who has a thing to attach it to. To show what a name is for, you have to show a proceeding. And there is no proceeding."

"No proceeding."

"There is no proceeding," said Colette Marr, "because a proceeding is started by somebody with a standing to start one, and standing to start one is given to you by a court, and a court gives it to you in a room, and you would have to come to this room and ask me for a thing and be refused, twice, and then come back a third time with a reason, and I have watched about four hundred people do that and every single one of them has been somebody with a case, and a case is a thing you have got and you are not the holder of it."

"That is not a refusal on the merits, then."

"No," said Colette Marr. "**And it is not a refusal on jurisdiction either, and I want to be careful, because the two of those are the two refusals this building knows how to write down, and if I write one of those on a piece of paper then there is a bar on it, and a bar on it is a thing you can appeal, and an appeal is a proceeding, and I have just told you why there is not one.** I am not refusing you. I am telling you that there is nothing here yet, and that nothing-here-yet is not a thing this building has a procedure for, and I have been in four buildings with drawers in them in nine years and I have looked."

"You have looked."

"**I have looked,**" said Colette Marr. "There is a form for a person who wants a record altered because the record is wrong. There is a form for a person who wants a record altered because the record is incomplete. There is a form for a person who wants a copy of a thing they are entitled to. There are about nine hooks on that board behind you with nothing on them and every one of them was a form in about 1988, and I could tell you what four of them were for, and I could not tell you what one of them was for after I looked at it in the drawer on Tuesday, and it is in a drawer and it has not been used since the year I started and it is not *obsolete*, there is no word on it saying obsolete, it is just a hole in a board."

She looked at the clock.

"That is the whole of it, and it is worse than a no, and I am sorry, and I have said it as well as I know how and I have had four years to get good at saying it."

---

He was at the Proof Registry at ten past one the same afternoon, and the queue was four deep, and Bettina Haile came out from behind the partition at about twenty past one and said, before he had got to the counter, "No."

"You have not asked me."

"You asked me on Monday and I said no and you have come back, and I have had three days to think about whether I was right, and I was, and I would like to say something about that before we start." She put the tray down. "**I have been asked twice in nine years whether a name can be withheld, and both of those times it was a person who wanted to write it down for a file, and both of those times I said no, and I have never regretted either, and you are the first person who has come back a second time and I have thought about it every day since Monday and I have decided that I am still going to say no and that I am going to say it faster.**"

"That is not what I came for."

"Then what have you come for."

"I have come to ask you what the difference is between the piece of paper I have and the piece of paper I have not," said Elias Venn, "and I have worked out the answer myself on a bench on Monday and I want to see whether you have got the same one and whether you have got it better."

Bettina Haile put her cup down, and it was not cold this time.

"Ask."

"You gave me nine lines on Monday for a certificate you have never seen and you have never met the person in it, and every one of the nine was about a fact. An office. A reference. A sequence. A date. A time. A fee. And the whole of the nine is a fact about this building, and you told me the nine lines are the only piece of paper in this city about that certificate that anybody in the world can have, and the certificate itself is a fact about a night, and it is in a payer's drawer in a building you will not name for me."

"Yes."

"**So the name is the smallest of the four things I have not got,**" said Elias Venn, "and it is the only one of the four that is a word, and the other three are a building and a night and a person, and you have not withheld a word from me this week. You have told me a post. What is in the box is a post."

"Yes," said Bettina Haile. "And on Monday I gave you a reason and it was the true one and I have not improved on it in three days, and if you have come to improve on it then you have wasted a morning, and if you have come to tell me what you worked out on a bench then I would like to hear it and I have got about eleven minutes before the queue gets to you."

"Everything in a record of issue is a fact about an office," said Elias Venn, "and every field of it is available to any member of the public who can produce an account number, including the schedules, including the ward, including the day, including the fee, including the time, and there is not one of those fields that is a fact about a night. The only field in the whole of this business that is a fact about a night is the acts, and the acts are not in this building, and the acts are on a piece of paper in a drawer four miles away, and I have paid two credits for a copy of it and I have it in a bag and I am not going to show it to you, and it is the only document in this city that I have ever been afraid of losing."

Bettina Haile did not say anything for about four seconds, and then she said, "**That is the whole of it, and you have got it in a week and I have got it in nine years, and I got it in a room with a police officer in it, and you have got it standing at a counter, and I have been slightly sick about that all day and I am not going to tell you which day.**"

"I will not write it down."

"I know. That is why I said it."

"Then I am going to tell you one thing that is not in the box and is not in the file and is not on any form, and then I am going to go and serve your queue, and I am giving it to you because you have been careful with me for four days and because I am tired."

"Go on."

"When I put a name in that box it is because somebody in this building has written, on a piece of paper, a thing that means a name in that box, and there is a date on it and a signature at the bottom of it, and it is a piece of paper that somebody could be questioned about, and I have never had to produce one in nine years because there has not been one." She squared the tray. "**So the name is not behind a wall. The name is behind one piece of paper, and there has not been one piece of paper, and if you want to know what has happened in this city for nine years then that is what has happened: nobody has ever written one down.**"

"That is not your fault."

"It is not my fault and it is not anybody's fault and that is the difficulty," said Bettina Haile, "because I have spent nine years being a careful woman in a building where the thing that protects people is a piece of paper, and the paper is not there, and I cannot make it, and nobody can make it, and everybody in four offices in this city has quietly decided that the paper is somebody else's."

She went back behind the partition, and the woman at the head of the queue came forward, and Elias Venn said thank you and was not thanked, and the bell on the door was not loud in that building.

---

The bench at about four o'clock was outside a bank on a corner, and it was not the bench he had sat on outside Harrow Lane, which he had noticed, and which he decided not to think about.

He had a page and a pencil and he wrote three lines, because there were three things he could still do, and under each of them he wrote what it cost, and he wrote it in a hand that was not neat.

> **1. Wait.** The letter went on 12 March. It takes a company three weeks and it is not expedited by anybody. It will be answered or it will be answered to, somewhere in the second week of April. **Cost: three weeks, eleven credits already in an envelope, and an answer that will be about a building and will not be about a night.**
>
> **2. Be given a reason.** The ground of the refusal is that a name is a reference and a reference needs a proceeding. Therefore the office will serve me the moment I am a person with a proceeding, and a proceeding is not a thing I can invent, and it is not a thing I can buy, and it is not a thing that anybody in this city will give a member of the public for asking. **Cost: unknown, and the whole of the cost is that it is not a number.**
>
> **3. Become a person who has something yet.** Not a case. Something smaller. Something with a form attached to it, in a drawer, in a building, in a country, that says a man called Venn is waiting for something, and that is the only one of the three that gets a name out of a box. **Cost: to be a person who has something, in a city where a person who has nothing gets a polite answer in nine seconds, and to be that on purpose, having been told to do it by a clerk with a stamp pad.**

He read it three times and did not add a fourth line and did not underline any of the three, and then he wrote underneath, in a different pen, because he had two:

> *And the thing underneath all three of them, which I have not got a number for: a person who has not been given a form is a person this city has not finished with. Not one refusal in five months has been because of me. Every one of them has been because of a drawer.*

He put the pencil away.

At about ten to five a woman of thirty-four came out of the magistrates' building across the road with her coat on over the cardigan and unlocked a door and went in, and before she went in she stopped, and looked across at the eight hooks on the noticeboard through the glass, and looked at them for about two seconds the way you look at a thing you have been thinking about all afternoon, and then she went in and shut the door behind her, and the hooks were on the other side of it, and there was nothing on eight of them, and the ninth had a card on it saying that forms were available at the counter, and the counter was shut, and the woman who worked at it had gone home.
