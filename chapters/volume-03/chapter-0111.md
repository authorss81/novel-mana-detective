# Chapter 0111 — A Fee Is a Fee

The back room at 3 Tanners Row had a table, two chairs, a hotplate, a clock that was four minutes fast, and a carrier bag on the table with the door of the room propped open behind it because Otto Vahl was on the landing with the radio on and the radio was the price of the room.

Elias Venn came in at about twenty to nine with two credits in his hand and put them on the table and did not open the bag.

He had done the arithmetic on the bus and he did it again at the table, because he had begun in March to do arithmetic twice. Fifty credits in the till. Two credits out. Forty-eight. He had a repair on the bench in the back room that he had quoted at eleven, and a part on order from a man in Bell Street who wanted it on Friday, and the rest of it was rent and the rent did not care what a fee was.

He wrote three lines in his own book, and then a fourth, and the fourth was the one he had got up at eight-forty to write.

> *A claim reference is not a claim number. The certificate in the bag carries a claim reference. The counter will not serve me on a claim reference. A reference tells you which paper it is and a number tells you whose account it is on, and the second one is in a different building and on a different day and belongs to somebody with a right to be there.*

Then he sat with the pen on the fourth line for a while, and then wrote underneath it:

> *I am not going to buy the record of issue. Not because of the fee.*

---

The Proof Registry at 14 Harrow Lane opens at nine and by ten there were eleven people in it, and the public counter was on the right of the front room with a fan going and a rope across the last two feet of it, and the woman behind it was about fifty-four and had a cardigan over the top half of a uniform and was reading a document upside down because she had forgotten she was holding it.

"Can I ask a general question before I spend anything."

"You can." She turned the document round. "You can also spend, and I would rather you did one or the other, because the queue does not care which."

"I want to buy a record of issue," said Elias Venn, "and I have not got a claim number, and I am going to tell you what I have got instead, because I would rather you had the true version than the one I would have made up."

She put both hands flat on the counter.

"**A record of issue is the only document in this building that will tell you the truth about a certificate without telling you anything about the sequence,**" she said. "It has a number on it and no acts on it. It says a certificate was issued, and it says who issued it and out of which office and at what time and for what fee, and if you want to know what a person did on a night you will not find out anything at all from it, and I have watched four people discover that this afternoon. Two of them were told last week that a record of issue would help and they came in with a claim number and they went out with a piece of paper that says a piece of paper exists."

"How long is the afternoon."

"Two hours," said the woman behind the counter, "and I have watched it four times this month and it is always the same two hours." She looked at him properly for the first time. "You did not come in here with a question about what is on a record of issue. You came in here with a question about whether you can have one, and you have told me you have not got the number, and that is the whole of your question and I am going to answer it and you are not going to like it."

"I did not expect to."

"A claim number is required. **A member of the public never has one.**" She said it the way a woman reads out a price. "A claim number is on the account. The account belongs to a person, and the person is either a claimant or somebody's solicitor, and neither of those is you. You can have a copy of a paper on a claim number. You can have a record of issue on a claim number. You can have a great deal of paper out of this building on a claim number and you cannot have one thing without one, and the fee is two credits, and a fee is a fee, and a registry is not a shop, and I have said no to about four hundred people on the basis of that number alone in nine years and two of them shouted and one of them sat down on the floor."

"And the other half of it," said Elias Venn, "which is the half you said you were going to say. If somebody did have that number."

"Then I would hand it over this afternoon, and it would take me four minutes, and here is the strange part and it is the part that has made four people sit down on a pavement today. **A record of issue does not need the paper. It does not need the person. It does not need you to have been in the room.** It can be served for a night, and a premises, and a date, and that is the whole of what it says, and I have pulled one for an account belonging to a person who died in 2027 and one for a company that was wound up in 2028 and one for a ward in a building that four people in this city could not tell you the name of, and in all three cases I have never seen the paper it was about, and I would not have been permitted to."

"I have got one of those papers already. It is in my bag and it is not been out of it since Monday. It carries a claim reference and I have not got a claim number, and I would like to know what the difference is, because I have been told there is one and I have not been told what it is."

"A reference tells you which paper," said the woman. "**And I am going to explain the difference once and then I am going to stop explaining it, because I have explained it to nine hundred people and about forty of them have heard it.** A reference is printed on the paper and it is made of the same stuff the paper is made of. A number tells you whose account it is on, and the account is a book in a building you have not been in, and the number goes in a request, and a request is the only thing this counter will move on."

He did not take the bag out of his hand. It was in his hand, under his coat, and it had been in his hand since the fourth of March.

"Could I have the second one, if I had brought the paper with me, and not a number."

"No," said the woman, "and I am going to say the reason once and then never say it again. The paper is the thing. The number is the way in. **Bring me the paper and I will tell you things about the paper, and one of those things is not the number, and the number is in a different building on a different day and belongs to a person who is allowed to be there.** And I will tell you the harder half, because you have been honest with me and I have watched four people be dishonest with me about this today alone." She put her pen down. "The file is upstairs, behind a door that says closed, and it is not this counter, and a member of the public served here with a document in his own hand means that every word on that document becomes something the public is entitled to, and I have watched that happen four times this month, and every single time it has cost the person who asked more than it has ever cost the office."

Behind her, a man of about sixty with a folded bus ticket in his hand was handed a single sheet across the counter and read it standing up. He had the look of a man who had come in with two questions and had been given one of them.

"That one," said the woman, without turning round. "He is a ward. He has been at it since October. He wanted the paper and I have told him what I am telling you, and he has about three minutes to decide whether he wants to go up two floors, and he is not going to go up two floors, because the second counter is behind a door that says closed, and you go through when the woman in there is in, and she goes out at eleven for a tea, and nobody waits for a woman to come back from a tea."

"What is the second counter."

"Sequence Certification," said the woman. "Upstairs. That is where the paper is and that is where the paper is always going to be, and I have not got it and neither has anybody on this floor, and I am not being coy about it, it is upstairs and it is not this counter."

The man of about sixty read his sheet, folded it in half, folded it in half again, put it in his inside pocket, said thank you to nobody in particular, and went out. The bell on the door was the loudest thing in the building.

"He had a claim number," said the woman. "Everybody who gets one of those out of here has a claim number. There is no other door."

He went back down the street to the door and stood in the road and looked up at the first floor, because he had not looked at it when he came in, and there were four windows and one of them had a card in it that said CLOSED and the card was a piece of A4 with the word printed on a machine and sellotape at the corners, and somebody had written *back 11* underneath it in biro. At about eleven the card came off the window and a woman of about thirty came out onto a fire escape with her coat open and a cup in her hand, and went in, and the card went back up on the glass about ninety seconds later. Nobody on the pavement looked up. Elias Venn stood in the road for about four minutes and worked out that he had been in a building for fifty minutes and had been told that the thing he wanted was behind a door two floors above him, and that the door was open for about eight minutes every hour, and that the fee at the counter he had spoken to was the same two credits that the fee at that door was, and that neither of the two women had the power to make the other one open earlier.

So he wrote nothing down, because there was nothing there that was not on the inside of a building he had been in, and he went and had a cup of tea in a place that was not his and thought about the difference between a thing that is refused and a thing that is upstairs.

---

The front office of the police station on Corbie Street had a counter with a bell on it and a queue of one, and Petra Lund had a cardigan on over her uniform and a mug of something on the counter that she had forgotten about, and she saw him come in and put the mug down and did not say hello.

"You have asked me one on the Wednesday and one on the Friday and I said one each," she said. "That was two weeks ago and this is a Monday and you are here before half past three, and I have not had a free one this week, so this is the third and it is the last of the month."

"I know what it is," said Elias Venn. "I have come to spend it and I have come to say what it is first, because you told a woman in a laboratory on a Friday that you had run out of anybody else to tell, and I have thought about that for two days, and I would rather be honest than quick."

"Go on, then."

"A record of issue is served on a claim number and not on a claim reference, and I have the reference and not the number, and I would like to know who in this city is entitled to ask for the number."

Petra Lund looked at him for about two seconds and then turned to the old man, who had been standing there since he came in with a plastic wallet and a piece of paper gone soft at the folds.

"Yours first, sir, and I am sorry, and it will be four minutes."

The old man put the paper on the counter and said that a form had gone missing.

Petra Lund did not say *what form* first. She said, "You have had that since you came in," meaning the wallet, and she meant it kindly, and she took the paper off him and turned it round so that it was the right way up for her, and read it, and put it down and squared it to the edge of the counter.

"Now. This is form nineteen. Somebody has given you nineteen, and nineteen is for a report of a thing that has been lost. What have you lost."

"Her cat."

"And the cat's name."

"Dot."

"Right," said Petra Lund, and wrote *Dot* on the pad, and turned the pad round so that the old man could see it, which she did not have to do and which took four seconds. "And what form did you fill in to lose a cat, sir."

The old man looked at her for a while.

"That's the one I've come about," he said. "I've come about the other one."

Petra Lund put the pen down flat on the counter, and picked it up again, and said, in a voice that did not change at all, "Then say the sentence, and I will write down whatever you say, and it will be what you say and not what I would have written."

"It is about the form for the cat," said the old man. "I filled it in on the Thursday and somebody's took it out of the wallet while it was in my coat, and I want to know who."

Elias Venn stood four feet away with his hat in his hands and did not move and did not say anything. He watched a woman of thirty-three take down the whole of an old man's account of a piece of paper in his own words and then read it back to him, and he watched her not correct the tense, and he watched her leave out the word *stolen* because he had not said it, and he watched her put the sheet in a tray on her own side of the counter and write the tray number on the top corner of his copy.

"Thank you," said the old man. "You are very thorough."

"It is my job," said Petra Lund. She turned back to Elias Venn and picked up the forgotten mug and drank about a third of it cold and put it down again with a face. "Right. And I will tell you three things and then you are finished for the month."

"Go on."

"**Nobody in this city can give you that number, and everybody in this city can, and that is not a paradox, it is a route, and routes are made out of people and not out of paper.** It is a person with a standing to ask. It is not the police and it is not me, and if it was me then I would have to put a reason in writing and the reason would be a case, and you have not got a case, and I have looked, and there is a drawer in this building with about nine hundred forms in it and I have been through the whole of it twice in my head."

"Second."

"**Second, I am not going to be your way into it, and it is not modesty.** Every time in nine years that I have thought about bending one of these, it was for somebody who was in front of me and could see my face, and I have never once done it for a man who telephoned. If I ever do it for you it will be because you are standing in this room and not because you are on the telephone, and I would like that written down in your book so that you cannot come back in June and say that I had implied otherwise."

"Third."

"Third, and this is the one that is going to cost you something and I would rather you had it now. **If somebody does give you standing to ask, it will come out of a room with a door on it, and it will cost the person who gives it more than it costs you, and you will find that out on the day.** And if it does not cost them anything, then they have not given you anything, and that is the only test I have got, and it is not a form either."

"Petra."

"Go and find a bench," she said, "and do not tell me where."

---

There was no bench. It was about four o'clock and the lane behind Corbie Street had a wall on one side with the sun off it and a loading bay on the other and nothing to sit on, and he stood against the wall for about ten minutes until the door of the station came open and a woman of thirty-three came out into it with her cardigan over her arm and a cigarette she did not light.

"You have been standing there since quarter past."

"Since about ten to."

"You are not subtle and I have never thought you were," said Petra Lund. "Say it."

Elias Venn said it. He said that on Wednesday the sixth of March a police inspector with twenty-two years in had told him to ask somebody with a warrant and a coat who was allowed into a building, and that he had not, and that it was five days, and that he was going to have to say it to somebody who could write it down and he had not been able to think of who.

Petra Lund put the unlit cigarette back in the packet.

"**That is the correct decision,**" she said, "and I am not going to argue with you about it, and I have already argued with somebody else about it this week."

"Who."

"That is not a thing I am going to give you at four o'clock on a Monday outside my own front door," said Petra Lund, "and I would have said no if you had asked, and you did not ask, and I want that noticed." She pulled the cardigan tighter over her arm. "I have been told by somebody in a very good coat that a member of the public who keeps turning up at counters is a *pattern*, and I stood there and said no, because you are not a pattern, you are a man who has been turned away eight times in five months and has a page in your own book about every one of them, and I have seen the book because you left it open on my counter in February and did not notice, and I did not read a word of it, and I want you to know that I did not read a word of it, and that I noticed it was open."

"I did notice," said Elias Venn. "About a week later."

"I know. That is the difference between us and it is why I can stand here." She looked at the wall for a second. "I will tell you the thing I can actually give you and then I am going inside. If somebody does give you standing to ask, it will come out of a room with a door on it and it will cost the person who gives it to you more than it costs you, and you will find that out on the day, and if it does not cost them anything then they have not given you anything. That is the only test I have got and it is not a form either."

"Petra."

"Go and find a building with a claim number in it," said Petra Lund, "and do not go back to Harrow Lane twice."

---

He got back to Tanners Row at about nine and Otto Vahl was on the landing with the radio off and his door shut, which was the signal. There was a customer in the shop who wanted a radio brought round and did not want to pay for the collection, and Elias Venn did the thing he had learned to do in six months, which was to say the price and then say it again.

When the shop was empty he went in the back with the electric ring off and the ten-day clock going, and he took the notebook out of the inside pocket of the coat, and he turned to the page with Monday the eleventh of March 2030 on it, and he wrote one line.

> *11/3/30, 2 credits. Record of issue.*

Then he ruled a line under it, and he left the rest of the page empty, and he put the two credits back into his pocket, and the carrier bag went on the shelf behind the door where it had been since the fourth of March with the paper in it that had cost two credits and a signature and a name written in a ledger in Morrow Hill by a clerk who had never met him.

The till said fifty credits. He counted it because he had begun to count things, and he put it back, and he did not take two out.
