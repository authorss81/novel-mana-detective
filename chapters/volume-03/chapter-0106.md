# Chapter 0106 — Two Orders

The office was open on Wednesday night because the day worker went home at eight and the night worker came down at nine, and Elias Venn had a piece of paper in his coat with a telephone number on it and nothing else on it, and he had no appointment, and he did not pretend to have one.

He was at the bottom of the wooden stair at ten to ten with his hand on the handrail, and the handrail was on the left going up, and the building had four storeys, and he was not going to be able to say afterwards what made him put his hand on it.

Perpetua Ayodele was on the landing in a cardigan with her sleeves rolled, waiting.

"You told me you were not going to come back a third time."

"I came on Friday. This would be the third and I have told you I would not, so here is what I am going to do and you can stop me at the door and I will go away and that will be the end of it." He did not put his bag down. "There is a book on a shelf in this office. It has been in this building for twelve years and on nine December of last year a working happened in the hall of this building, and that book was on that shelf when it happened, and I am not a police officer and I am not going to pretend to be one and I am not going to touch anybody in this building, and I am not going to take one page out of that office or photograph one page of it or copy one word out of it. I would like to put my hand flat on an open page of it for about a quarter of an hour and see what order it is in."

"You are asking a woman of forty-four to let a man she met on Friday night do a magic thing to her office on a Wednesday night."

"Yes."

"And if I say no."

"Then I go away, and I do not come back, and there is a man in this city who is going to be told something about his own life by a person he has never met, probably in about four years, in a corridor, by a woman with a tray."

She thought about it for about as long as it took the man on nights to come down four steps.

"Two rules," she said. "You tell everybody in this building that you are doing it, out loud, before you do it, and I do not care which of them you tell, I care that you have said it. And if anything in that room changes while you have your hand on it, you take your hand off and you go downstairs, and I do not care what it is, I want you to be out of the building inside sixty seconds, and that is not a magic rule, that is my office."

"I agree to both."

"You have not asked what I would have to agree to if I said yes to the rest of it."

"I am not going to ask you for anything else," said Elias Venn, "and that is the third building in five months where somebody has offered me a condition and I have not asked for the rest of it, and I want you to know that I know what that is worth."

---

The office had a desk, a chair with a cushion in it, a kettle, a shelf of paperbacks with the spines in no order, a wall calendar from a veterinary practice with a picture of a cat on the month of March, and **nine chairs** against the far wall, four of them stacking chairs and five of them not.

He counted them on the step of the office door and he counted them again from the inside, and it was nine both times, and it was not eleven, and he was aware of the number the whole time he was in the room.

The night book was on the shelf above the desk, spine out, four inches of it visible, and it was a hardback ruled book with a vinyl cover and the word **NIGHT** printed on the front in the middle of a rectangle, and somebody had written a year on the front in ballpoint, and then crossed it out, and then written a year underneath it, four times, so that the front of the book was a small archaeology of somebody's New Year.

He said it out loud to the room. There were two people in the building he could see, Perpetua Ayodele in the doorway and the man on nights at the counter in the hall, and he said it to both of them and loud enough to carry down the stair.

"**I am going to put my hand on that book and I am going to read the order of a working in it, and I am not going to touch any person in this building while I do it, and I am not going to touch anybody in this building at all tonight, and I cannot do it to a person even if you asked me to and I would have to say no, and that is not a rule I have been given. It is the only one I have got.**"

Perpetua Ayodele said, "Say the second part again."

"I cannot do it to a person. I have never done it to a person and I never will, and I am telling you that in a room in front of two witnesses because in January a policeman's hand went towards a chair in a room and I stood in front of it, and I have thought about that every day since, and I am not going to be the man whose hand goes towards a chair."

"Right," she said. "That is the first sensible thing anybody has said in this office since 2027."

---

The book had four columns and then a fifth.

**In. Out. Rounds. Notes.** And the fifth, ruled off with a line down the side of it and headed in capitals in somebody's careful hand: **SECURED.**

The first four were every night for twelve years, the same hand for the first seven years and a second, slightly heavier, hand from about March 2027, and the fifth column was a time and it was nearly always a time, and it was the last man in the building saying when the building was secure.

He did not go to 9 December 2029. He found the page first and then he put his hand flat on the open page with his fingers spread and the heel of his palm off the paper, the way he had been taught to in September 2021 by a man who is not named in any file in this city, and he read it.

It was not a picture. It had never been a picture and he had spent five months telling people that in a shop and a kitchen and a laboratory and had twice been laughed at by a woman with four instruments. It was order. It came up out of the floor of him the way a room comes up out of a floor of him, and there were four things in it and they came in an order and the order was the whole of what he had.

**First: the door.**
**Second: a man's hands, and a man not being a man but a shape with a man's hours on it.**
**Third: a turn, in a hall, that made forty-one people put their hands flat on their own chests and feel that they had been somewhere already.**
**Fourth: something coming out of the shape and going back out of the door, and a sound like a shoe on grit.**

And it went out through the door, and it took a piece of the book with it, and the piece of the book that went was the only part of the night that was a person, and he came off it about ninety seconds later with the taste of metal and had to put both hands on the desk.

He did not write anything for about four minutes. Then he wrote in his own book, in his own hand, one line, and the line was: *9.52 p.m. Four acts. The door is first. That is not a door you open to let somebody in.*

He read the room for a while. There was a smell of tea and old paper and a man in the hall counting a till.

He went downstairs at about ten o'clock because he had ninety minutes to kill and a man of thirty-six asleep in a chair, and he sat on the second chair in the hall with his hands on his knees and looked at forty-one people sleeping on the floor and on mats and in two chairs, and worked out something he had not known, which was that **he could not tell how many of them were there, and that the reason he could not tell was that he was not allowed to count.**

He had counted chairs in four buildings in five months. He had counted nine chairs in this office twice and eleven in a first floor in January, and it had been the price of the counterturn, and he had counted the four in a waiting room on a Sunday morning because that is what a man in his position does with a room. And he could not count the people, because they were not furniture and nobody had asked him to and there was no instrument in this city that would let him, and a woman of fifty-eight off a linen run had said a version of it to him at half past eleven at night in a landing on the twenty-third of January, and had been right, and he had not thanked her, and he had written it down and had not understood it for a month.

A boy of about nine came in at about half past ten with a book and sat down on the floor with his back against the wall about four feet away, and read for about twenty minutes without looking up.

Elias Venn watched him do that for about a minute and then made himself look away, deliberately, and think about the fourth column of a night book instead, which was headed NOTES, and which had one line in it for every night for twelve years, and which on the ninth of December had been empty.

There was nothing in the notes column on the night of the ninth of December. Twelve years of a man's handwriting and one blank line, and the blank line was between the eighth and the tenth.

He went back up at half past ten with his hand on the handrail on the first landing, and he put his hand flat on it, and the next act happened again, and again.

It was not a vision and it was not a sequence and it was not a working, and he had told a police inspector in a corridor in January that it was none of those three things and had said it out loud and had been believed, and the belief had not stopped it. His hand went flat on the varnished wood at the turn of the stair and he came up the last four steps, and he came up the last four steps, and he came up the last four steps, and on the third time he got his hand open and off the wood and held it in the air in front of him with the palm up, and stood there in a first-floor landing of a building in Kettle Rows holding his own hand out like a man holding a plate he has just been handed.

Perpetua Ayodele was in the office doorway with a cup in each hand and she had seen all of it.

"Do not come in," said Elias Venn.

"I was not coming in."

"That is not what I meant and you know it is not."

"Then I will say the thing I actually meant," said Perpetua Ayodele, and she put one cup down on the floor of the doorway and kept the other, "which is that I have been in this building six years and I have seen a man on nights do that about four times and I have never once said a word about it to anybody, and he is not a stranger and he is not a colleague and he is on nights, and I have said nothing, and I have never even put it in a book, and if you want to know what I think it is then I think it is something somebody did to him in January and that it is going to be with him for the rest of his life, and I think he knows that, and I think that is why nobody has ever said anything to either of you about it."

"Four times."

"Four times that I have seen," said Perpetua Ayodele. "I am not counting it. I told you this morning that I am not writing numbers down in this building and I meant it about beds and I meant it about this as well."

He stood in the landing for about a minute with his hand still up.

"Thank you," he said.

"Do not thank me, I have not done anything."

"You have not," he said. "That is why I am thanking you."

She picked the cup up off the floor of the doorway and went back into the office, and he went in after her, and he said so out loud in the doorway, because he had promised to say things out loud in that building, and she said, "I know. I looked at it in January. I looked at it in January and I decided he had had a bad night and I wrote it down to him having had a bad night."

Then he read the fifth column for the ninth of December, and the entry was not a time:

**Up 23.10 · Back 23.40 · Door · Off.**

Thirty minutes. He was off the desk for thirty minutes on the night of the ninth of December, and in the security column of a building where he had worked for twelve years, in his own hand, he had written the word **Door** and nothing else about it, and the certificate that came out of the Proof Registry on the ninth of January does not contain the word Door, and does not contain the word hall, and does not contain the word forty-one, and says the words *self-directed* and *own person* and *premises* and stops there.

He read it again at a quarter past eleven, and put his hand on the same page, and it came up different.

**First: the shape with a man's hours on it.**
**Second: the hall, and the turn, and forty-one hands.**
**Third: the thing coming out of the shape and going out of the door.**
**Fourth: the door.**

And the fourth came before the first.

> **MARGIN — Both of these happened. Not in this order.**

He stood in the middle of a small office in Kettle Rows with his hand six inches off a book and a kettle coming to the boil behind him and understood that he had two readings of one night and that they disagreed about what the night was, and that neither of them was a vision of anything, and that a man in a laboratory had told him in November that two readings of the same working are worth more than one reading of it, and that she had not said that, and that he had invented it, and that he had invented it because it was true, and that he was standing in a room having invented the most useful thing anybody has said to him in four months.

Then the kettle went and the other thing happened.

---

It did not come from the book. That was the first thing he understood about it and the only thing he was certain of afterwards. It came in from outside the reading, out of the four inches of vinyl and the paper the book was made of and the twelve years of a man's handwriting, and it was somebody's, and it was not his, and it was not anybody he had ever met.

**A bench under a window with the light coming off the neighbour's wall. A jar with a lid, and something in the jar, and hands that knew which hand. A garden with a wall on two sides of it. The fourteenth of August, and then the twenty-eighth, and then the fourteenth again, and the two weeks in between, and a man who did not come in, and the not-coming was a Tuesday and then it was a Wednesday and then it was an ordinary Thursday.**

It was not grief for a person. It was grief for a **Tuesday**, which is a thing a person only grieves about if they have been counting, and somebody had been counting, and the counting was the whole of what came through, and it was about four minutes long and it did not stop when he put his hands on the desk and it did not stop when he knelt on the floor, and he went down onto the floor of a refuge office in Kettle Rows at about ten to midnight on a Wednesday night because his legs stopped being part of the arrangement.

He was on his knees on a lino floor with a kettle boiling behind him and a shelf of paperbacks in no order above his head, and he was crying about a garden he had never seen, in a fortnight in a year he was not born in yet, on behalf of somebody he could not name, and he could hear the kettle and he could not get up and he did not want to, because wanting to get up would have meant it was about him.

Perpetua Ayodele came in and switched the kettle off at about the second minute and did not touch him and did not ask him anything, and she pulled a chair over with her foot and sat down in it about four feet away and said, to the middle of the room:

"There is a woman asleep on a mattress in the store room and she is seventy and she has been awake since four in the morning and she is asleep now, and I am not going to wake her up and I am not going to let you wake her up, and I am going to sit here."

He said, at about the third minute, in a voice that was not his: "I am not going to be able to tell you what it is."

"No."

"It is not mine."

"I did not ask whose it was," said Perpetua Ayodele, "and I want it noticed that I did not ask, and that I am not going to, and that if you tell me later I will listen and if you do not tell me I will not have asked."

It went off at about four minutes, the way a wave goes off, and he was left sitting on a lino floor with a cold kettle and a bad taste in his mouth and about nine pounds of somebody's fortnight in him that he had not gone looking for and could not put anywhere.

He got up. He washed his face in a sink with a cold tap in it and it made it worse. He went back down the stairs and out into Anselm Street at about ten past midnight and stood under the lamp that worked.

He wrote two lines in his own book in the light of a shop window he was not going to go into, and the first line was:

> *27/2/30, 11.58 p.m. Took something out of that book that was in the woman on nights. It has gone. I do not know what it was and I am not going to find out.*

And the second line was:

> *I read it twice. The second time I was less sure than the first. Both were real. I have been five months telling people that a copy of paper is a copy of a thing, and a man in a laboratory told me in December that the comparison is worth more than either reading, and I did not listen, and tonight it cost me a fortnight in a garden in a year I was not born in, and I would do it again, and that is the part I am frightened of.*

He put the book away, and got the last bus, which was at twenty past midnight and which he missed by about nine minutes, and walked the twenty-two minutes home, and slept for four hours, and was hollow for a day and a half, and told nobody.
