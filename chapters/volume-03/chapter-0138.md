# Chapter 0138 — The First Friday In Four Years

The door at 18 Pellow Street opened about four inches at about half past two on Wednesday the twenty-fourth of April, the way it had opened about four inches at ten past ten on the twenty-fifth of March, and the woman on the other side of it was the same and was not any older and looked it.

"You have come with nothing," she said.

"I have."

"**In March you came with a prepared speech and got through two sentences of it and had nothing. In April you have come with nothing and said so before I had the door shut, and that is an improvement, and I would like it recorded that it is an improvement and not a practice, because I have not got a form for a practice.**" The door stayed where it was. "Go on then."

"What did they tell you not to write down," said the woman at 18 Pellow Street, and it was the only question she had ever asked anybody at that door, and she asked it in a hall with a runner worn pale in a stripe, in a house with a kitchen at the back whose door does not shut.

Elias Venn stood on a step with two bricks missing out of the top tread and found that he could answer it.

"Nobody told me anything," he said. "**I told somebody. In a corridor, at one in the morning, on Monday, and I am not going to say who, and the thing I have not written down is a man who asked me for one thing in a shop in February and got it, and everything after that is him and nothing else.**"

The woman looked at him through four inches of door for about four seconds.

Then she opened it about a foot and let him into the hall and shut it behind him, and did not apologise for the runner, and did not offer him anything, and went through to the front room.

There was a table under a cloth and a hardback book on it and a chair and a wireless on a sideboard and a lamp. The book was where it had been in March. He did not look at it, and she did not move it, and neither of them did anything at all about the fact that it was the third thing in this city in five weeks that had been left alone on purpose in a room with a man in it.

"Sit down if you are sitting down," she said. "I have not had a night off since Thursday and I am not going to make a performance out of a man in a coat."

He sat down.

---

"Now tell me what you actually came for, because you have told me the answer to my question and you have not told me the question's other half, and the other half is *why did you tell her.*"

"I do not know yet," said Elias Venn.

"That is a bad answer and it is the first one anybody has given me that I believe, so I am going to leave it." She sat down opposite him, on the chair with her back to the wireless. "**I have been thinking about you for four weeks, which is longer than I have thought about anybody who has come to that door, and I want to tell you what I have been thinking about, and then you can go.**

"You came in March and said a sentence in my front room that I recognised. **I did not tell you where I had heard it. I have not told anybody where I heard it, and I heard it in a kitchen in this city in about 2016, off a woman who was extremely cross, and I heard it about four years before that off a man on a hospital ward, and both of those people said it to me because they thought I needed saying it to, and neither of them was talking about a case.**"

"I did not know that when I said it."

"**I know you did not, and that is why I have been thinking about you for four weeks, and not about the sentence.** The people who said it to me wanted something. I have had a sentence said at me in this room by four people in four years and every single one of them wanted something, and one of them wanted me to open a drawer. **You said it and then you put your hand flat on the table and said that you cannot read a person and you were not going to put your hand on my book, and I have thought about that for four weeks, and what I have thought is that the sentence was the part where you were most like all the rest of them and the hand on the table was the part where you were not.**"

She got up and put the kettle on, which was an answer to a question nobody had asked.

"Have you got anything to tell me about a person," she said, with her back to him. "Because I am going to say a thing and then I am not going to say it again, and I have been holding it since about the eighth of April and it has got heavier."

"Go on."

"I could not take a person. I told you that in March and I said about eleven days and I did not tell you it was a Friday." She filled the kettle and set it on the ring. "**He came on the Friday. The fourth of April, in the afternoon, at about three, on a day when I have said for four years that anybody who comes to that door comes on a Friday or not at all. And I could not take him, and I told him I could not take him, and he did not argue with me about it, which I had not expected.** And then on the Friday after, the eleventh, he came back to say that he had got in somewhere, and he telephoned me on the Wednesday to say so, and he did not say thank you, and I would not have wanted him to.

"And on the Wednesday in between, the ninth, I came to a room over a shop on Corrie Street and I sat at a table and a doctor said eleven names out loud and I wrote them down, and I have been writing first names in that book for four years and there has never been anything in it but a first name. **On the ninth of April I wrote a date. Wednesday the ninth of April. And it is not a person's name, and there is no name in that book for that man, and I did not put one, and I want you to understand that I did not put one because I did not have one and not because I was careful.**"

"Why did you write a date."

"**Because it was a Wednesday and I have never once in four years written anything in that book that was not a Friday.**" She turned round. "That is all. It is not profound and it is not evidence. I have said for four years that it is always a Friday and I have never worked out why, and it is the only thing about this house I would like to understand, and on the ninth of April a Wednesday went into my book and I have not been able to put it out again, and I do not know what to do with a book that has one day in it that should not be there."

He did not look at the book.

"I am not going to ask to see it," he said.

"I know you are not going to ask to see it, and that is the second time you have not asked me for the thing in this room, and the first was the book and this is a smaller thing and it is about a day and you have known about that day since March and you have not asked me a single question about it in six weeks." She poured the water. "**Both of those are the same man and I would like you to know that I have noticed and I am not going to make a speech about it, because a woman of sixty-three who has been doing this for four years has stopped being impressed by anybody who is careful.**"

---

They drank the tea standing in the kitchen, because there was one chair at the table and the other was under a pile of post she had not opened, and the door at the back still did not shut.

"Can I ask you for one thing," he said, "and it is not a question."

"You can ask me for one thing."

"**Write it down,**" said Elias Venn.

She put her cup down on the draining board.

"Write what down."

"Whatever it is that you have just said, and whatever it is you have worked out about a Wednesday, and the Friday, and about a book. Write it down. In the book, or on a piece of paper, or on the back of something, and put it in the drawer where you keep the post. **I have spent five months going round this city looking for a piece of paper and there has not been one, and there is not going to be one, and the reason there is not going to be one is that everybody who could write one has not been asked to and does not know they are allowed.**"

She looked at him.

"I keep a book," she said, "and I have never once been asked to put a thing in it that was not a person's name, and nobody has ever written down anything for me, and I have thought about a column for it about twice in four years and both times I thought about it at about two in the morning and both times I decided against it because I could not think of a heading."

"**A date is a heading,**" said Elias Venn. "You have already got one. Wednesday the ninth of April, and the man's first name if you ever get it, and the word *Friday* against the other days, and that is a column and it is a form and it is in your own hand and nobody in this city can take it off you, and I have spent five months trying to get a piece of paper out of four buildings and I would like one thing to have come out of this month that was not taken out of me."

She did not say anything for a while. There was a clock in the hall and it went and neither of them looked at it.

"Tell me one thing about the ninth of April," she said. "Not the names. I am not going to say a name to you in this house and neither are you."

"The doctor read them out and you wrote them."

"Yes. One at a time, in a room, with about nine people in it, and a woman in a cardigan who had been on that desk for nine years and who told me on the Tuesday, in a corridor, that the pen on the notice had come off its string at every one of the seven reviews she had been at the desk for and at no other time since, and that every one of the seven was a man from the trust or the company who wanted to write on a form. **I have thought about that for two weeks and I have not been able to stop thinking about it, and it is not about the pen.**"

"What is it about."

"It is about the two books." She put her hand flat on the table, on the cloth, on the side of the hardback book and not on it. "**I have got a book under this cloth and it has four years of first names in it and no surnames and no addresses, and I did not start it and I have never been able to explain it to anybody, and on the ninth of April I sat down at a table over a shop and I wrote eleven first names into a different book at a doctor's request, and between those two things I have done something to myself that I cannot undo and I have been sat here for a fortnight working out what it was.**"

She got up and turned the wireless on very low and turned it off again, which was a thing to do with her hands.

"**The people who come to that door come.** That is the whole of the difference and it has taken me a fortnight. I never sent for one of them. Nobody sent me one of them. Somebody in this city was in a bad enough night to walk four streets and knock on a door that a man in a good coat has never once been able to find, and they came, and I wrote down what they told me and that was all. **The people in the Corrie Street book were asked.** A doctor had a list in her head since about 2021 and a woman of fifty-five with a cardigan and a badge, nine years at that desk, wrote them out one at a time, and neither of them had been asked, and I was not asked, and I wrote them down anyway, in a room, in nine minutes, because I was asked to do a thing with a pen.

"So now I have got a book with people who came to me, and a book with people who were read out, and they are in the same city, and **in about two years somebody in an office is going to go looking for a list of eleven first names and they are going to find a woman of sixty-three in a terrace, and either they are going to think I have the same eleven people in the other book or they are going to think I have put two lists together, and there is not one thing on this earth I can do about that except never write the two books in the same hand.**"

"You have written them in the same hand."

"I know," said the woman at 18 Pellow Street, "and I have been sitting in a kitchen for a fortnight knowing that I have written them in the same hand, and that is the second time since March that a man has been in this house and worked out something about me in about four minutes that I have not worked out in a fortnight, and you are going to say that this is not a coincidence and I am going to tell you that I do not believe you and you are going to believe me, and that is how it should be done."

---

"Right," she said, after a while. "**I am not promising you anything, and I am not doing it tonight, and I am not doing it because a man in a good coat told me to in my kitchen. I am doing it if I do it, and I will do it in my own time and in my own words, and if it is ever asked about in about two years I am going to say that nobody asked me and that a man said write it down, and those are both true, and I would like both of them to be written down in that order.**"

"That is fair."

"I have not finished." She turned the cup about a quarter turn on the board. "**You came in here and you told me the answer to my question, and your answer was about a promise you made to a man in a shop, and you have not told me a word about the man, and I have not asked you for a word about the man, and that is not me being careful. That is me telling you that a person who has been careful for six weeks and then is not careful for one night in a kitchen is telling me something about themselves and not about him.** And I want to say this and then I want you to go, because it is the end of a Tuesday when I have not had a night off since Thursday and I have a woman coming at eight.

"**Do not do it again in a corridor at one in the morning.** Do it in a room with a light on and a person in it who is going to have to remember it in about a year. That is all. That is the whole of what I have got and it has taken me four weeks to work out that it is about you and not about him."

He said that he was sorry, and she said that he was not to be, and neither of them said the word, and she took both cups through to the kitchen and put them in the sink, side by side, with the handles on the same side, and neither of them washed them, and he went out into a street four streets off a parade in Kettle Rows at about ten past four in the afternoon.

She did not offer to walk him to the door, because he had not come in through a door that anybody walks anybody to. He went down the two steps with the two bricks out of the top tread and along a terrace of nine houses with the doors alternating and the gaps in the railings every two doors, and there was a washing line in the third yard with a sheet on it and a child's bicycle against a wall in the sixth.

Behind him, on a table in a front room, there was a hardback book with a cloth over it, and on the cloth there was nothing at all: no pen, no paper, no second book, and no place on the cloth where any of those had been, and there had not been a place on it since March, and he did not turn round to look at it, and it was not his.
