# Chapter 0107 — He Signed a Paper to Be Left Alone

The piece of paper came back on Thursday at about two in the afternoon, delivered to the shop at 3 Tanners Row by a boy of about eleven on a bicycle who wanted to be told whether he was supposed to bring one back.

"You bring one back," said Elias Venn.

It was clipped to a board under a fire notice for four days and it came off the board and across a hall and down a street on the back of a bicycle, and it was the same piece of A4, folded once, and the four letters and the time were in the top corner, and on the back of it there were two words in a hand that leaned right and pressed hard.

**I will.**

Underneath, in the same hand, smaller, and not in the first person's writing:

> **Chip shop on the corner. Seven. Not before. Not after.**

He read it twice on the shop counter with the till at fifty credits and a customer waiting, and then he put it in his inside pocket next to a plastic wallet, and he did not photograph it, and he did not write the two words in his own notebook, and he did not write the words on the back either, and the reason he did not write them down is that a man had given him a piece of paper and the man had not asked him to keep it, and Elias Venn had spent five months learning what it costs to be the man who keeps things.

---

The chip shop on the corner of Anselm Street had a counter, four stools, a glass front with the fog of it already on the inside at ten to seven, and a man in his fifties behind it with a chipper and a habit of saying the price before anybody asked. There was one other person in it, a woman of about thirty with a child asleep on her arm, and she left at five past seven, and the door went, and the bell went, and it was the last Thursday in February and the light outside was the colour of a coin.

He was already there, and he had been there for about forty minutes, and he had a cup of tea in front of him that had been cold for some time.

"Eleven, love," said the man behind the counter.

"He has it," said Ruben Sallis.

"You have not touched it."

"I have not touched it," said Ruben Sallis, "and I have been sitting here for forty minutes and I would like to be honest with you about why, which is that I read the back of that paper about eleven times on Wednesday night and I could not decide whether to write anything on it, and then I did, and then I could not decide whether to go, and I have decided to go, and I have decided to go about six times."

Elias Venn sat down on the second stool and put his hands on the counter, and did not take a notebook out, and did not have one out, and the man behind the counter saw that and went and did something unnecessary to the fryer.

"You did not write a telephone number on it," said Ruben Sallis.

"No."

"You wrote your name and a number and the word *Anselm Street* with a line under it."

"That is all of it."

"That is all of it and I have had it four days and I have asked four people whether it is all of it." He turned the cup about a quarter turn. "I have been a night porter in that building for twelve years and I have read about four thousand pieces of paper in my life, and every single one of them had something on it about me, and your piece of paper does not have anything on it about me. Not a case number. Not a building. Not a sentence about what I did." He looked up. "Why is that."

"Because a piece of paper with a man's reason on it is a thing a person finds in a flat in a house, and I am not doing that to a building of forty-one people for one man of fifty-four."

"That is a very quick answer for a man who has been doing this since September."

"That is a very old answer," said Elias Venn, "and I have never once been the first person to give it."

---

He asked one question, and it was not the one he had come with, and he had known that on the bus.

"Mr Sallis—"

"Ruben."

"Ruben. **Did anybody ever tell you what a record is?**"

The man looked at the counter for a while. Behind the counter the fryer went off with a noise and neither of them reacted to it.

"No," he said.

"Not in a course, not in a form, not in a conversation."

"No."

"What do you think a record is."

"I think a record is about you," said Ruben Sallis. "Everything with my name on it is about me. There has never been anything else." He put his hands flat on the counter, the way a man does when he is about to be accurate about something. "I have been asked what my name is about nine hundred times in twelve years. I have been asked what my address is about four hundred. In 2028 somebody said *Ruben, put your name on this* and I put my name on it, and I did not ask what it was for, and nobody has ever asked me to explain myself and I have never had to, because I have never once been asked a question about a Tuesday."

He turned the cold tea about another quarter turn.

"You have asked me one question and it is not the one you came with, and I want you to know that I have noticed that, and I have decided I do not mind, and if you tell me the other one now I will answer it, and if you keep it I will tell you about the ninth of December."

"Keep it," said Elias Venn. "Tell me about the ninth of December."

---

It took him about forty minutes and it came out in the wrong order and Elias Venn did not correct it once.

He had been on that night four years and he was on nights, ten until six, and it was a Tuesday, and the ninth of December. There was a man shouting in bed nine at about half past ten and he was not the sort to shout at nothing, and Ruben Sallis went out of the office into the hall, and the hall was as it always is on a Tuesday because it has been a Tuesday for twelve years, and he was in the hall for about half an hour and he could not tell anybody in that building what happened in it, and that is the sentence he had been carrying since January and he said it exactly like that, flat, twice, as though checking that it was still true.

"Who was in the hall."

"I do not know."

"You said you were in it for half an hour."

"I was in the room," he said, "and the whole of that hall was asleep in it, and I know my way round that hall in the dark because I have walked it every night for four years, and I know how many are in it because I count them, because if I do not count them and one of them is not there in the morning then I am the man who did not count. And I counted them that night and I did not see a soul, and that is what I have been saying since the twelfth of December and I have said it to three people and all three of them were kind to me about it and not one of them has ever come back with a different answer."

"What happened."

"There was a woman in the room."

He said *in the room*, and stopped, and looked at the chip shop window, and Elias Venn waited.

"There was a woman in there and she had a form," said Ruben Sallis, "and it was not a police form because I have seen a police form, and it was not a Rowan form because I have signed about four hundred Rowan forms. And she asked me things, and she asked me a great deal, and I answered her, and I answered her honestly because I have never in my life done anything else on a Tuesday night. And at the end of it she had two pieces of paper and she gave me one of them and she gave me the other one and **she asked me to sign the form and she did not ask me a single question about the other one, and I have thought about it for eleven weeks and I believe I signed the one she did not ask me about, and I want that written down in a book somewhere, because if I am wrong about that then the other eleven weeks are a different thing.**"

"Which one did you sign."

"I signed both," said Ruben Sallis. "That is what I am telling you. I signed both. I am not telling you I signed the wrong one. I am telling you that one of them was asked for and one of them was not, and that is the whole of it, and eleven weeks later I cannot tell you which is which, and neither can she, because I do not have her name and I have asked four people and three of them have told me they do not know her and the fourth one told me to go away."

He picked the cold tea up and put it down again without drinking it.

"They will both have my signature on them," he said. "That is the thing I cannot get past. There is a piece of paper somewhere with my name on it and I have never read it and I have never been asked to and I have never wanted to, until about the middle of January."

---

"Why did you sign the one you did not ask about."

Ruben Sallis looked at the man behind the counter, who was cleaning a chip scoop and had been cleaning the same chip scoop for four minutes.

"The trust came round in the January of last year," he said, "with a slip of paper saying eleven of the porters had been put on something, and I was one of the eleven, and they paid for it and it was two days and I did it in a room in this building that I am fairly sure was a meeting room upstairs, and it was on a Tuesday and a Wednesday and we had a certificate at the end of it that said we could be used as witnesses in a dangerous working. Eleven of us. **I have used it exactly once, for the December, and I did not know that was what I was signing for until the January, when the certificate came, and I read about four lines of the paper it was on and then I stopped and I have not read the rest of it and I have not been able to bring myself to read the rest of it since.**"

"You knew it was a course in December."

"I knew it was two days. I did not know it was a witness. And I want to say something about that and I want to say it properly, because I have been thinking about it in a bed in the afternoon for eleven weeks and I have got it to the place where I can say it." He put both hands on the counter. "**A man will sign anything that is put in front of him in a room if he has been told for eleven years that his name is the only thing anybody wants from him. That is not a defence. I know it is not a defence. I have read it somewhere that it is a defence and I am telling you now, in this shop, that it is not, and that I worked it out myself in about a fortnight and then I have not been able to do one single thing about it since, and that is what I have actually been doing with my time.**"

"You did the ninth of December and then the certificate came out on the ninth of January."

"Ninth of December, I signed. Eleventh of December, a man from the police wrote it down in a book and read it back to me. Ninth of January, it came out." He said it like a man reading a bus timetable. "A month, and a certificate, and not one of those three dates has ever been explained to me by anybody. **And the trust came round on the sixteenth of January** and told us eleven of us had been re-interviewed, and by the eleventh of February three of the four of us who were still there had gone, and they put the contract out to tender in February, and the beds close on the thirty-first of March, and there is a review on the ninth of April, and I have read about a hundred and forty of those beds and I have been in that building every night for four years and I could not tell you one of the people in the hall that night by name, and there was a woman in the hall who I have never seen before or since, and I have told all of this to three people and one of them was the police and I told him in a room with a door and he wrote it down in a book."

---

Elias Venn put his hands flat on the counter.

"Mr—"

"Ruben."

"Ruben, listen to me carefully, because this is the only time I am going to say it and I am going to say it once. **I am not a police officer.** I have a certificate number in my head that I am not allowed to write down, I am not allowed to apply with it, and the only reason I have said anything to you this evening is that I have not got one single thing I can do for you and I would rather say that than come here in a month and pretend I have."

"I worked that out on the Friday when you counted my chairs," said Ruben Sallis.

"And I have not written one word of this down. Not a sentence, not a time, not a place. There is no notebook on this counter and there is not going to be one, and if I have written anything tonight then it is in my own book at home and it is about me and it is not about you, and I will show you neither and I will show you both if you ask me to in a year, and I am not going to lie to you about which of those it is going to be."

"Then what is the point of you."

"**The point of me is that I am a person who can be in this building without a form,**" said Elias Venn, "and there is nobody else in this city who can be in this building without a form, and I have been that person since the third of November and I have not wanted to be it once, and I cannot think how anybody gets anything done at all."

Ruben Sallis laughed, which he had not expected to do, and it was not a cruel laugh.

"Ask me what you came to ask," he said, "and then go away, and then come back if you want to."

"You will not be asked anything else. Not tonight and not ever, unless you ask me."

"Then there is one thing," said Ruben Sallis, "and it is not a question, and I have thought about it for four days and I have not got a better way of saying it. **Nobody is to be told where I am.**"

The chip shop had a fryer going and a clock over the door and a man with a chip scoop.

"Not the trust, and not the police, and not the woman on nights, and not anybody at the clinic if you have not asked me about a clinic, and not the woman who writes on the board. **Nobody.** If they send me a letter I will not answer it, and I do not care what it says, and I have thought about that for four days and I have decided I would rather not be found than be found with somebody standing in the room where I sleep. That is not a big decision. It is the only decision I have made in eleven weeks and I have made it about six times a day."

Elias Venn did not ask him why. He had four reasons he could have asked about and three of them were about money and one of them was about a woman's voice on the ninth of December, and all four of them belonged to Ruben Sallis and none of them belonged to him.

"I am not going to tell anybody where you are," he said, "and I am not going to write it down tonight, and I am not going to be clever about it. I have promised three people something in five months and I have kept two of them and I know exactly what the third one cost somebody, and it was not me."

"Which third one."

"A man of thirty-nine who lived four doors from a man of fifty-eight in a street of first-floor rooms," said Elias Venn, "and he stood at the end of his own corridor on a Tuesday night in January because a police inspector telephoned that building and asked him one question, and he said yes, and he has not been told since then whether it was any use to anybody, and he is not charged with anything, and that is the correct answer and it is the correct answer because there is no sheet in this city that says a patient has been told what the work is for. So I know what it costs to keep a promise like that, and I am telling you I know."

The clock over the door said a quarter to eight. Ruben Sallis picked up the cold tea, drank about a third of it, grimaced, and put it down.

"Two days' notice," he said. "If you want to talk again, write it on that book in the hall, the one on the table by the door, the one with a name in it. Put a day on it, and a time, and I will be there in the evening, and nobody else reads that book, and if somebody starts reading it then I will not come and you will know why."

Elias Venn went out into Anselm Street at ten to eight and the lamp over the door was on and the bell went behind him and he stood on the pavement for a moment with his hands in his pockets and a piece of A4 folded once in the inside pocket next to a plastic wallet, and he did not write the words on the back of it down until he got home, and when he got home he wrote two words in his own book and nothing else, and the two words were not a name.
