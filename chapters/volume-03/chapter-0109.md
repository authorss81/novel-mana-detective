# Chapter 0109 — The Comparison

He had been into the Proof Registry at 14 Harrow Lane on Monday morning at about a quarter to ten with two credits and a signature, and it had taken nine minutes, and he had come out with a page in a bag, and he had then spent the whole of Monday afternoon and the whole of Monday evening not thinking about it.

On Tuesday morning he went back in at twenty past nine with the bag, and the counter was a different woman, and she had a cardigan on and a stamp pad and about four hundred pounds of stamping in it.

"There is a distinction and I have been told about it by a counter assistant and I want it from your side of the counter."

"Go on."

"A certificate is the thing. Four acts and a box and a signature and a fee and a date. A record of issue is a piece of paper that says a certificate was issued, and it has a number on it and no acts on it."

"That is right," she said. "And there are two other things you have not asked about and I am going to give you both of them because you asked the right first question. One: a copy of a certificate is two credits and a record of issue is also two credits, and the two fees go into two different things, and one of them is a legal expense and one of them is a registry expense, and in about 2014 somebody in finance noticed and could not think what to do about it, and it is still like that."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is that a record of issue will tell you two things about a certificate that the certificate will not tell you about itself. It tells you the office it was issued out of and it tells you the time. **The certificate carries the date and not the time.**"

"Why would a document not carry the time it was made."

"Because a sequence is an order," said the woman, "and an order has no clock in it. And if you printed a time on the front of a page with four acts in it, then somebody somewhere would start reading the four acts against the time, and about nine people in this building have tried that in the last ten years and the answer is always the same, which is that a working is not obliged to happen when a document says it happened." She stamped something that was not the bag, out of habit. "I am not going to ask you for the claim number, because you have not got one and a member of the public never has. And if you come back with the same number I cannot serve you again, because a fee is a fee and a registry is not a shop, and I have said no to about four hundred people on that basis and two of them shouted."

"I am not coming back with the same number."

"Then I hope you never need to."

---

He got to Saint Orra Reach at about half past nine and stood outside the door of the laboratory for about a minute and a half before he knocked, which was new, and which was because he had a carrier bag and two of the things in it were not his to bring.

Mara Quill opened the door and looked at the bag and then at him.

"You have written something down that I am allowed to see and you have written something down that you are not, and you have brought a sheet of paper, and the sheet of paper is the thing you are not allowed to bring, and I am going to decide in about four seconds whether to let you in and I want you to know that the deciding is mine."

"The second one," said Elias Venn.

"Then give me the bag and do not tell me what is in it until the bench is clear, and then I am going to make tea, and you are going to drink it, and you are going to sleep for forty minutes, because you have the face of a man who has not slept since Wednesday night."

He slept for fifty minutes on a bench in the corridor outside the linen office with a paper cup of tea going cold beside him, and she let him, and she came and got him at eleven o'clock and did not mention it.

Then she said, "Right. What have you got."

He gave it to her in the wrong order, which was his habit, and which she corrected twice.

"**You read a night book in a refuge in Kettle Rows on Wednesday night at about ten to ten, in place, and you read it a second time at a quarter past eleven, and you told me on Friday by telephone in four sentences and you did not tell me one word of what you actually got.**"

"I got four acts."

"You got four acts and two orders, and the two orders are different, and you have not told me that either, and you said to me on Friday telephone *she said the comparison was worth more than either reading*, and I want to know why you said that, because I did not say it to you in November and I have been annoyed with myself since Friday night, because a sentence I have not said has been in your head for three days doing work in it."

"You did say it."

"I said that a plate records residue and does not decide what residue means. I said two plates can disagree and that the disagreement is a fact. I did not say the word comparison, and I want it entered that I did not." She pulled a stool round. "Say the two orders out loud. Both of them. In order. And do not explain them."

He said the first one out loud. She wrote nothing and her hands did not move.

He said the second one out loud.

She wrote two lines on a sheet of paper, one under the other, and turned it round so that he could see it, and said nothing about it.

"That is not one working," she said.

"No."

"That is a working, and a thing somebody did to the account of the working. **You read a genuine four-act working on Wednesday night and on the same page you read a different order of the same four acts, and both of them were real, and you have spent three days assuming one of the two readings was your mistake.** It was not your mistake. A door cannot be both first and last. One of the two is right as an order, and your first reading says the door is first and your second says the door is last, and there is a physical thing on that page that makes both of them true at once, and it is not a mystery and it is not a faculty and it is the oldest idea in my trade. **A working can be run in an order and reported in another, and the reporting is done by a person, and it is done with a pen, and it leaves no residue at all.**"

"The second reading is a report."

"The second reading is a report, and you read it because it was written into the page by a man in a room, and your faculty cannot tell the difference between a thing that happened and a thing somebody wrote down, and that is not a fault in your faculty. **It is a fault in every instrument in this city, including mine, and there is nothing on the bench in this room that can tell a selection from a fabrication, because a selection leaves exactly the same residue as a truth and a fabrication leaves more.**"

She stood up and went to the shelf and stood with her back to him for a second.

"That is the answer to a question you have been carrying since about November, and I want to say it out loud and then I want to sit down. **A certificate does not have to be a lie.** A certificate can be made out of a completely true thing by taking one act out of it and moving two, and the moving can be done by a person with a pen in an office and no working at all, and there is not one instrument in this city or any other that will ever show you that it was done. Which means a certificate that is a selection and a certificate that is a lie produce the same physical record, and **the only way anybody will ever find out which one is which is a person.**"

"Elias Venn," he said.

"Yes," said Mara Quill. "Yes. And I know what you are going to do with it, and I am not going to let you do it in this room, and I have not said so yet."

---

"I am coming with you tonight," she said, "and I am going to tell you why, and it is not to help you."

"Fine."

"It is not fine and I have not finished. In November I made a rule and I have told nobody I made it and you have not asked me about it, and the rule is this. **I do not work on an object that has been in contact with a person who is still in the building.** Not on Tuesday, not with a consent, not with a doctor in a corridor. A door in a refuge stairwell has forty-one beds behind it and the residue on it is not a document's residue, it is a hall's, and I said in November that I would not do it and I have not done it since." She put her hand on the bench. "So there are two ways. Either you do it on your own tonight, and I never hear about it, and I do not lose a night of sleep, and you come out of that building with a fourth order and nobody in the world able to check a word of it. Or you ask a man who is paid for that building whether you may put your hand on his fire door after eleven o'clock, and you do it with a witness, and I am the witness, and I get to say out loud what I am and why I am there."

"I would like the second one."

"I know you would, which is why I am saying it out loud, and I am saying it because if I did not say it out loud then in about a year one of us would have done something on a bench in here without the other knowing, and that is the way this ends." She picked up her bag. "Go and ask the contract officer. Not by telephone. In person, today, and do not tell him what you want it for until he has said no or yes to you being in the building at all."

---

Gareth Prothero was in the office at 2 Anselm Street at about twenty past three on the Tuesday afternoon with the same folder under his arm and about six people in the hall, and he listened to the whole of it standing up, which took longer than Elias Venn had allowed for, and he said no four times before he said yes.

"No to a stranger in the office. No to a stranger in the hall. No on a Tuesday, and no on a Wednesday either, and I do not care what is on the door. No at nine o'clock at night in a building I am paid for with a hall of people asleep on the floor of it."

Then he said: "What exactly do you want to touch."

"The fire door at the top of the stair. The one with the bar across it."

"Why that one."

"Because it is the only thing in that building on the ninth of December that anybody went through."

Gareth Prothero stood still for about four seconds.

"God," he said. "All right." He went to the office door and shut it, which he had not done all afternoon. "I am going to write you something on our paper and I am going to date it and I am going to put my name on it, and you are not going to photograph it, and you are not going to keep it, and I am going to have to tell the trust's solicitor about it on Friday and I have already decided that I do not care, and that is the first time in eleven years I have decided that about anything."

He wrote it on the back of a delivery note and dated it and signed it and turned it round.

> *I consent to Elias Venn and to one other person placing hands on the fire door at the top of the stair at 2 Anselm Street on the night of 5 March 2030, after 11 p.m., for the purpose of reading a sequence, and to neither of them entering a bedroom, an office with a document in it, or any part of the building other than the hall and the stair.*
>
> *One condition: you will tell me what you find, and I will write it in my own book, and I will not put your name on it.*
>
> *G. Prothero, Contract Officer, 4.3.30.*

"You may read that aloud to a police officer if you have to," he said. "**I have been kept out of that building for eleven years by everybody who has ever had a reason to keep me out, including me, and I have never once been asked what is in it, and I am not going to be the man who says no to a question about a fire door.**"

---

They went up Anselm Street at about twenty past eleven on the Tuesday night and the lamp over the door was on and it was the ninth night in a row he had been on that street and the first night in his life he had arrived with somebody.

Mara Quill carried four instruments in a bag and had not taken the plate out of Saint Orra Reach, and she said that out loud on the pavement before they went in, because she had decided that anything said on a pavement in Kettle Rows at half past eleven would be said twice and ought to be said once.

"I am not certifying anything tonight," she said. "There is nothing on this earth I can certify. I am going to measure, and I am going to be a second pair of hands, and if he says an order out loud I am going to write it down and say *that is not what my plate says* or *that is what my plate says*, and those are the only two sentences I am going to say, and you are not going to like the second of them half as much as the first."

"I know."

"You do not know, but you will."

The hall had about thirty people in it, on mats and on the floor and in two chairs, and there was a boy of about nine reading against the wall, and the fire door at the top of the stair was propped open with a wedge of wood, because it had been propped open since about 2019 and everybody in that building knew it and nobody had ever fixed it.

He put his hand flat on it.

It was not like a book. A book is a record and a book lies gently; a fire door has been run through eleven times an hour for eleven years and there was no gentleness in it anywhere. It came up out of the floor of him and it was over in about four seconds and it was the only one of the three readings that was not arguing with itself.

**First: the hall, and the turn, and a row of hands going flat on a row of chests.**
**Second: the shape with a man's hours on it, standing, and not doing anything at all.**
**Third: the thing coming out of the shape.**
**Fourth: the door.**

Four acts, in that order, and the door last, and the door not a door anybody opened to let somebody in.

He took his hand off and put it flat on it again, and got the same four, and Mara Quill said: "That is what my plate says. That is the whole of what my plate says and I have been standing here for ninety seconds and I have got a third independent number for the same four things, and none of the three of us is allowed to say *I have proved it*."

"What have you got."

"The door is last," said Mara Quill. "In a stairwell. Which is the most ordinary sentence in the world and which neither of us would have believed in January."

---

They did the comparison on a bench in the hall at about twenty to midnight with a torch and a carrier bag and eleven people asleep and a boy of about nine who put his book down and came and looked and was not asked to leave.

Three columns in his own handwriting, taken at 9.52 and at 11.15 and at about twenty-five past eleven, and the fourth column was not handwriting at all.

**Column four was the certificate.** It was a single sheet of A4 with a Registry header, a claim reference block, four printed acts with a ruled box beside each one, a certification line, a signature, a fee line, a date and no time, and it was lying on a bench in a refuge hall in Kettle Rows at twenty to midnight in the middle of March because he had paid two credits for it on Monday morning and put it in a bag.

"That is the whole of the trick," said Mara Quill. "Say it slowly and say it out loud, because you have not said it and you have not written it down and you have not slept, and I want to hear you say it before I say it."

Elias Venn put the torch down flat on the bench so that the page was lit from the side.

"I read four acts out of a night book on Wednesday and four acts out of a fire door tonight," he said, "and the two of them agree. Door last. Hall first. The turn second. And in between them a shape with a man's hours on it that is not doing anything, and a thing that comes out of the shape, and that is four, and all four of them are real, and both of my readings say the same four in the same order, and I have been calling one of them a mistake for three days and it was not a mistake." He put his finger on the certificate without touching it. "**And this has three.** It has the hall, and it has the turn, and it has the release. It has left out one, and the one it has left out is the one that would have shown a second person standing in that room, because the shape with a man's hours on it is the only part of that night that was a person, and it has kept the other three in and moved two of them, and the one it has put last is a release, and a release goes on a certificate, and it is in the wrong place, and the order of the three is not the order of the four."

"Go on."

"**This is not a false certificate,"** said Elias Venn. "Every act in it happened, on that night, in that hall, to that building, and the man it names did not do any of it and every word of the paper about the man is true and none of the words is about him. Somebody took a true thing, moved two acts, dropped one, and printed it, and it cost two credits and a signature and about four minutes, and there is no working in it at all, and there is no magic anywhere in this hall except the thing that happened in it in December."

Mara Quill did not say anything for about ten seconds. A man on a mat turned over. The boy of about nine said, "Is that the police?"

"No," said Elias Venn.

"Cool," said the boy, and went back to his book.

Then Mara Quill said, out loud, to nobody in particular, in a hall where thirty people were asleep:

"**Then there is no instrument.** There is no instrument in this city, and there is not going to be one, and I have spent eleven years trying to build one, and the thing I have just been told is that I have been trying to build a thing that cannot be built, because a true thing with an act taken out of it is indistinguishable from a true thing, and the difference between the two is a person, and the person is always going to be the one who is harder to find than the paper."

"Or easier," said Elias Venn.

"Yes," she said. "Yes. That is the whole of it and it is worse, because paper does not change its story when you have not got a warrant, and people do."

---

Then she made him agree to two things, out loud, in a hall, at half past midnight, and she wrote them on the back of a delivery note with a date on it, and it was not a protocol and she said so four times.

"First: **I do not do this again on an object that is in contact with a person who is still in the building.** I have said that in November and I have not said it out loud since, and I have said it out loud tonight, and you heard me, and there is a woman of forty-four who works days in that office and she was in it on Wednesday night and I have not spoken to her and I am not going to."

"Agreed."

"Second: **you do not read the same object twice again.** Not for a month, and not at all if the first two readings disagree, and not on a night when you have been awake. If two readings agree I will look at them, and if they do not agree then what you have is a man with two accounts and a sheet of paper, and that is a police matter and not a laboratory matter, and it is a matter for a person who can compel testimony, and that person is not me and is not you and is a woman at Vine Street." She wrote the date at the top. "We have got a rule. Two of them. **A rule is not a protocol.** A protocol is a document that two people sign, with a heading and a version and a clause about what happens when one of us is wrong, and I have not written one and I have not got anybody to write one with, and I am not going to write one with a man who has a certificate number in his head that he is not allowed to write down."

"Then who."

"I do not know," said Mara Quill. "And I have thought about it since Christmas and the honest answer is that it is not you and it is not me, and it is going to be somebody who is frightened of losing a job, and there is exactly one of those in this city I can think of who is competent, and she is forty-seven and she works on the fourth floor of a building on Verge Row and she has been the person who finds things out twice in her life and both of them cost her somebody."

She folded the delivery note and put it in her coat.

"Two credits and a signature," she said. "And a man on a bench, and eleven weeks of my life, and a fortnight in August in a year you were not born in, and about four hours of sleep on a Tuesday. That is what it cost and I have written the hours down because I am a person who writes hours down." She stood up and looked at the hall. "And on Monday a woman at a counter in a corridor telephoned me because she was not permitted to give a member of the public a name and she wanted somebody to know that she had refused him properly. I have known her a fortnight. She is not a police officer and she is doing a police officer's job on a Wednesday and nobody has asked her to, and I would like it noticed that she rang me and not you, and that I think that is the correct decision and I think it is yours to notice it and not to answer it."
