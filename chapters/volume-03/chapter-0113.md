# Chapter 0113 — Concourse B

The ground floor of Concord Hall had a floor of grey terrazzo with a brass line across it where the doors had been during the war, and on the right of the hall, past a fire notice, there was a public hardback register on a table with a card in front of it saying that it was a public record and that enquiries about any entry were not accepted, and about ten feet from that table, behind a partition of plywood and glass, there was a door with **Concourse B — Licensing** on it, and the door was open.

Elias Venn had been in the hall three times since the twenty-fifth of February. He had been given the same three things on each visit, free, without being asked, by three different people: the wire rack by the radiator, the noticeboard, and the fact that general questions were answered and specific ones were not.

He had not opened the ninth module and he was not going to, and he had a line in his own book about why, and the line was that a man who has not asked him for anything is not a man he is entitled to go and read about, and the second half of the line was that if he ever broke it he would have to write down the day he broke it.

Inside the door there were two desks, four chairs, a filing cabinet with a padlock on it, a wall of course dates going back to the year the building opened, and a woman of about fifty-five who had a cardigan on over a blouse and a stack of about forty booklets on the corner of her desk with the top one squared off.

"Constance Vail. Wednesday the thirteenth of March. I am not police and I am not the claims floor and I have nothing to do with your case, and I have not said that to anybody for four years and I say it in the first ten seconds because it saves about forty minutes."

"I am a member of the public," said Elias Venn, "and I have a question, and I am going to start by telling you what it is not."

"That is the correct start and about one man in ten does it." She put her pen down. "Go on."

"It is not about a person. I am not asking about anybody in that book on the table, and I am not asking to see the list of who has been on your course, and I want to be clear that I know you would say no and that I have decided not to ask rather than be told no."

Constance Vail looked at him for slightly longer than a person looks at a stranger.

"Go on with the question, then, and I will tell you why that was the right way to come in."

"I have read that a certificate comes out of a district office in this city and that a registered witness is somebody who has been on a two-day course. **I would like to know what a course reference is made of.**"

"That is a good question and it is on the printed card by the register and nobody reads the card." She turned round in the chair and pulled a leaflet off the pinboard behind her without getting up, and it was the green printing, the two-year-old printing, and she turned it round and put a finger on the corner of a block on the front of it.

> **WT/2/4**

"Two letters, a slash, a digit, a slash, a digit. **The first two letters are the funder's code. The first digit is the number of days. The last digit is the number of times a year the course is delivered.** So that one is a two-day course, run four times a year, and it was paid for by somebody whose code is W-T, and the funder's code is what makes a person eligible for the course at all, because the course is free to a person whose employer has paid for their place and it is four times the price to anybody else, and about nine people a year pay for their own place and I have taught all nine of them."

"Four times a year," said Elias Venn. "So this year."

"February, May, September, November. Second and third day of the week, residential, two nights, and about six of the twenty-two people who come live in the building." She wrote the four on a pad and did not give it to him. "There are twenty-two places. There have been twenty-two places since 2021 and there were sixteen before that and there were ten before *that*, and I have run this course about forty times in twenty-two years, which is fewer than it should be, and it is fewer because of 2020 and because of two years when there was no building to run it in."

"About forty," said Elias Venn. "And twenty-two places."

"About forty, and twenty-two, and the funder's box is on the enrolment form and it is the fourth box down and it is the only box on the form that anybody has ever asked me about, and it has been filled in four hundred times in twenty-two years, and **nobody has ever once asked to see the four hundred.**"

"Who fills it in."

"The employer does. That is the whole design and it is a good design." She said it without any irony at all. "A person comes who works for a body. The body has paid for a place. The person signs a form, and the fourth box down has a code in it that the employer put there, and the form goes into a tray on my desk, and the tray goes to a clerk in a different building in a different week, and that clerk types it into a register which is on a table in the hall for anybody to read. And that is it. That is the entire mechanism and I have taught in it for twenty-two years."

"Which clerk."

"That is not a question I can answer and I am not going to enjoy being asked it," said Constance Vail, "and I am going to tell you why, and it is because **a course is not a register and a register is not a class, and the two are separate documents kept by separate people under separate delegations, and I have got neither of them.** I have got a stack of booklets and a wall of dates and the attendance in my own head, and the register is a public book in that hall with a card in front of it that says enquiries are not accepted, and the woman who types it into the register does not work in this room and I do not know her surname and I have worked in this building for twenty-two years."

Elias Venn wrote four months, and a number, and a word, on a pad of his own and put the pad away.

"Number forty," he said. "The number of times. And twenty-two places. That is my question, answered, and I am not going to ask you a second one because I have had one."

"That was not a question," said Constance Vail, "that was two facts, and I gave you them because you told me what you were not going to ask, and in twenty-two years about nine people have done that." She picked up the stack of forty booklets and squared it again, which she had not needed to do. "Can I say a thing, and you can write it down or not."

"Please."

"I have never once been asked what happens to one of them afterwards." She said it to the middle of the desk. "**Twenty-two years, and I have thought about that about four times, and I have never written it down, and I am not going to now, because if I write it down then somebody has to answer it, and I have a payroll of two and a course that runs four times a year and I have never found a drawer that answers it.** So I have thought about it four times and I have thought about it standing up, mostly, at about eleven o'clock at night, and I have never had anybody come back and tell me, and it is possible that they have all been fine, and I want to be fair to the four hundred, because I have taught four hundred people to tell the truth about forty minutes of somebody else's night, and I would like to know that the truth held, and there is no form for that either, and I have checked."

Elias Venn took the wire rack's copy of the compendium down on the way out. It was the current printing, three credits, and it went into the carrier bag with the paper that had cost two credits, and he noticed himself doing it and stood in the hall for a second with the bag in his hand, because it was the second time in three weeks he had put a thing in that bag that he had bought in a building, and the first time had been a certificate, and a bag with two documents from two counters in it was a bag that was starting to be a file, and he had not gone looking for a file, and he wrote one line about it that night and did not write what the line was.

---

The canteen in the municipal building across the courtyard was open from eleven to two and had a servery with a woman of about seventy behind it and about nine tables, and at one o'clock on Wednesday the thirteenth of March there was one man in it.

He was about fifty-two and had the cuffs of a coat he had not bothered to button and a cup of tea with the skin on it, and Elias Venn sat down opposite him because there was one table with room and it was that one, and the man looked up and said, "Fire's not on."

"I know," said Elias Venn. "It has been off since Tuesday."

"Since Tuesday," said the man, approvingly. "Then you'll want to know they turn the whole thing over at about ten past on a Friday and everybody stands about it."

"You work here."

"Nights. Twelve till six, eleven years." He turned his cup round. "You'll not find that in a book, and I'm not saying it for a reason. I was on a course in 2028 and I'm the only person in this canteen who has been on one, and if I tell you that then I'm a man who has been on a course, and there are about four hundred of us, and two of the four hundred ended up in this building's basement and the rest of us are the ones who came back."

"Have I met you before."

"No. And you'll get a lot out of that, because I have never been anywhere else."

Elias Venn put his hands flat on the table, and it was a thing he did now, and he noticed himself doing it.

"I am not going to ask your name."

"Ta."

"I am not going to ask you to repeat anything you have said. And I have not got a notebook out and I am not going to, and I am going to tell you why I am telling you, because I have found that a man who only says the useful half turns up a second time and is not believed the first." He turned his cup round too. "There is a document in this city that is true in every line and does not describe what happened, and the difference between the two is a person, and there is no instrument that will ever show you the difference, and I have spent six months finding out that the last sentence of those is not a thing you can put in a drawer. And I am not a policeman. I have got a finding against me in December and I am not a constable and I am not a doctor and I have no business in any building I have been in this year. So I am a man in a canteen."

The man drank about a third of his tea and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand.

"Twenty-two years in this city on nights and I have never had anybody come and sit down and *explain*," he said. "They have come and asked. Different thing."

"What were you told, when you did the course. About afterwards."

The man put the cup down.

"Nothing," he said. "**Nothing, and that was the arrangement, and I understood the arrangement and I signed it.** They give you the booklet on the first morning and there is a bit in it about what you're agreeing to and about giving your account in your own words, and there's a bit about after, and what's in that bit is that a registered witness is a person whose account is available to be looked at. That's it. That's the whole of after. And then the woman says *that's the course, that's the certificate, keep it* — and everybody in that room of twenty-two had a laugh, and I laughed, and I have not laughed about it since."

"You have not been asked about afterwards."

"Once. About four months after. A woman from the employer came round with a clipboard and asked whether the course had been useful." He looked at his cup. "And I said yes, and that was it, and she was pleased, and I have not seen the clipboard since, and I have thought about the clipboard maybe twice in four years and both times I have thought about the word *available*."

Elias Venn did not write it down. He could feel the not writing it down sitting in him like a stone.

"The part I have not been able to get past," said the man, "and I have got about four years to get past it, and I have not — is that somebody had to pay for me to go. **The course is free to a man whose employer pays for it. A man whose employer pays for it is a man whose employer knows he went.** And I did not think about that on the Tuesday in January when I was in a room, because I was thinking about whether I could be useful. And then in about February I was back on nights doing the same round I'd done the night before, and the same round I did last year, and there was a woman on the desk at about two in the morning who said *hello love, you did that course then*, and she did not say it unkindly, she said it exactly the way you'd say it to anybody."

He turned his cup round a third time.

"So they know," he said. "And if they know, then whoever sent me is on a list somewhere, and if I am on a list then the list has got my employer's name on it, because that is how the money went, and **that means there is a piece of paper in this city with my name on it and my employer's name on it, and I have never seen it, and I have thought about it every night for about four months, and I have still got the certificate, and it is in a drawer at home, and I have taken it out about six times.**"

"That is the course," said Elias Venn. "And I am sorry, and I am not going to soften it, because you have just told me the thing you have not told anybody and you have told it to a stranger in a canteen, and the only thing I can do with it is not to hand it back to you smaller."

The man looked at him.

"Say that again."

"The course puts your employer's name in a chain that leads to a piece of paper with your name on it, and it does it to about four hundred people, and it does it four times a year, and it is not a secret and it is not anybody's fault, and it is a *box* on a form." He tapped the table once with a finger, not meaning anything by it. "And that is why nobody can find the person who did the thing to the person it was done to. Because the person who did it was paid for it."

The man sat with that.

"Two o'clock," he said. "That's the thing about the servery, the coffee's off at two. You want to know about the sandwiches."

"I want to know about the sandwiches."

"Four credits. The cheese ones are the best and the ones with the egg are a lie." He got up. "You'll not see me about this again, so if you've come down here for something you haven't got it, and I'll not be offended."

"I have got it," said Elias Venn. "Thank you."

"Aye. Course is like that."

---

He went back into Concord Hall at about ten past three, because the canteen was in the same building and the register was a public record on a table by the counter, and a public record on a table by the counter is the only thing in this city he is allowed to read as often as he likes.

He did not ask anybody anything. He stood at the table with his hat in his hand and read the funder column of about forty rows, going down the page and not touching the book, and the column is narrow because almost nothing is ever written in it, and it is a column of bodies.

A housing charity he had a name for. A registered operator with a name and a premises. A health board. A civic committee with a minute number and no name. And, on the third row of the page he was looking at, a code and a floor and a series.

**44-31.**

A claim series. In a funder column. On a public register. Where a body that pays for a place ought to be.

He read it twice and did not put his finger on it and did not go and ask a woman of about forty at a counter whether a claim series has ever been a funder, because that is a specific question and he has got one general question a week at best and he was not going to spend it on that, and because a man of about thirty-nine had been reading public registers since February and had learned that the second time you look at a public thing is worth more than the first and the third time is worth nothing, and this was the second time.

He wrote nothing in the hall. He walked out into the courtyard, and the canteen had one table with room on it, and there was a sandwich on a plate in front of the chair opposite him with the paper still round it and a fork laid across the paper, and the man of fifty-two had not wanted it.

Elias Venn sat down and ate it, and it was a cheese one, and it was the best thing he had eaten in about five months, and there was a pencil on the table that came with the place, and he wrote five lines on the back of the servery ticket in his own hand.

> *Rowan Trust. Harlowmere Rest and Rehabilitation Ltd. A health board. A civic committee, minute number, no name. 44-31.*

That is the whole of the list and it took him eleven minutes to write and about four minutes to check against his own head, and he did not tick anything, and he did not write a building against any of the five, because he had promised himself on the step of a building in Kettle Rows in October that he would not be a man who stands in front of a name with a question, and because a list of five bodies with no address on it is not a conspiracy and is not a network and is not anybody, and it is five bodies that have each paid for somebody to sit in a room in Concord Hall for two days, and four of the five of them have a building and the fifth has a series number, and one of the four buildings is a room in a building where a man of fifty-four has worked for twelve years, and the course is run by the fourth.

He put the ticket in his pocket. At ten past five the bus came out of the arcade and he got on it, and he wrote nothing on the bus, because the five lines were already done, in a canteen, with a plate in front of him and somebody else's fork, and he had sworn on the step of Concord Hall that he would not go back and check one of them.
