# Chapter 0133 — A Word That Does Not Fit

Alaric Nunn was in a corridor on the ground floor of a police station on Corbie Street on Wednesday the seventeenth of April with a folder under his arm and a coat over his shoulder, and it was half past ten in the morning, and it was a Wednesday.

"You have not been in since March," he said.

"You told me not to come on a Saturday."

"I told you not to come on a Saturday, and you have not come on a Saturday, and there have been two of them since March." He put the folder down on a windowsill and did not open it. "What do you want, and if you have come to be refused again then say so now and I will enjoy it, because I have had a poor morning and there is a man from the Crown in upstairs with a printout of my timesheet."

"I want one thing about the week of the twenty-fifth of January."

"Then you will not get it from me and I will tell you why in one sentence: I was not there, I do not hold that week, and I do not hold anything at all, because the matter closed on the ninth of January and everything that belongs to it went into a store in a building two miles from here with a post on a form instead of a name." He leaned on the windowsill. "**What you have actually come about is a telephone call, and I am going to tell you what I know about telephone calls, and then you are going to leave, because I have a folder and a bad morning.**"

"I did not say where the call was."

"You did not say anything at all," said Alaric Nunn, "and that is why I am still talking."

---

"In this city," he said, "a place for a man is obtained with a piece of paper.

"Not a warrant. Not a court order. **A letter.** On a letterhead, with an address at the top and a signature at the bottom, and it does not have to say anything much, and it does not have to be a police letter, and in fact a police letter is worse than useless because a police letter has to have a case number on it and a case number is a thing that can be traced back to a decision, and what you want is a piece of paper that says an office is asking on somebody's behalf.

"That is all it is. **It is not a threat and nobody has ever threatened anybody with it. It is a way of saying that somebody is responsible for the person, and a person who is responsible for a person is a person who can be telephoned, and a home that cannot house somebody because nobody will take responsibility for them is a home with an empty bed in it, and an empty bed in this city is the only emergency anybody in this building ever gets called about.**"

"How many such pieces of paper are there in this city."

"**About six that are worth anything, and I am not going to tell you which six, and I want to be exact about the reason I am not going to tell you, which is not caution.** It is that I do not know. I have been in this force twelve years and I have seen four of them and I could not tell you which buildings two of them came from, and the two I know came out of this city, and the other two I have never found out and I have not looked, because looking is the job of somebody who is going to do something about it, and I am not going to do anything about it, and a man who looks is a man who has decided something."

"And the force."

"**The force gives one out and does not minute it.**" He said it flatly. "It is a request and it comes from a duty desk and it goes on a telephone and a woman in a resources office writes it in a box with a date in it, and nobody writes down the name of the person who rang, and I have known that for nine years and I have never once asked about it, and you are the first person in four months to come into this corridor and ask me a question about a form."

"Nobody rings you up."

"**About nine people have rung this station since the fourteenth of March and every one of them told me they were not police and would prefer it not to be known, and four of them asked me the same question you have just asked me, and I have said no to all nine in about six words each.**" He took the folder off the sill. "The rest of it is not mine. Whatever the rest of it is, it is written in a diary in a different building, and the diary is not the police diary, it is a ward book, and there is one page of it, and I have not seen the page and I am not going to, and if I told you the building you would be at that building on Friday and Friday is the day that building empties a diary into a store."

He said it as a fact about a building. He did not say he was warning anybody.

Elias Venn looked at the folder under his arm and did not ask about the folder, and Nunn watched him not ask, and something went across his face that was not gratitude.

"**That is the fourth time,**" Nunn said. "February, March, April and this afternoon. I have stopped counting it as a favour and started counting it as a professional disagreement, and I want to say something to you about that while I have the chance, and then I am going to upstairs."

"Go on."

"I have been in a corridor on this floor for eighteen weeks." He did the arithmetic as he said it, out loud, the way a man does a thing he has done several times. "**I have four lines in a book that I bought for a pound in Calder Street in 2019, and a telephone was in a room on the twelfth of December, and about four people were in that room, and I have said the word *room* to a radiator in this corridor about nine times in eighteen weeks and there was nobody to say it to until a man in a good coat turned up in February and would not take the book.**"

"You do not have to tell me that."

"**No, and that is the point of telling you.**" He shifted the folder. "There is going to be a hearing one day, or there is not going to be a hearing one day, and if there is one then those four lines go in front of somebody and I have known that since December and I have made my peace with it. What I have not made my peace with is the other thing."

"What is the other thing."

"That in eighteen weeks nobody has asked me to give evidence, and nobody is going to, and I have worked out why." He looked at the floor. "**Because the department does not have a case. And the reason the department does not have a case is that the only person in this city who could put one together has spent four months failing to, and every week that goes on I get a little more hopeful, and I have not said that to a living soul, and I am saying it to you because there is nobody else in this building I would say it to and because you are a member of the public, which makes it a thing I have said to somebody and not a thing I have said inside my own head.**"

He went upstairs. There was a lift and a stair and he used the stair.

---

Concord Hall, the ground floor, on a Wednesday at about two in the afternoon, and the same counter and the same woman, who was about forty and had been in her chair for eleven years, and who had a card in front of the register that said *A public record. Entries are made under delegation. Persons may read this register. Enquiries about any entry are not accepted.*

"You were in here in February," she said.

"I was in here in February."

"Your line is still in the book. I do not get rid of those." She put her hand flat on the cover. "**Are you asking about a person or about a person.**"

"I am asking about a person."

"Then I have to write it down while you are standing here, and I am going to write it the same way as I wrote it in February, and then I am going to turn the book so that you can read it back, and if you do not read it back I am going to be annoyed with you."

She wrote it and turned the book, and he read it, and it said what it said in February with the day changed and one word different in it, and he read it all the way to the end because she had told him she would be annoyed.

"Do you have a card reference for yourself? You gave one in February and you did not use it and I have thought about that perhaps four times in two months."

"I have not used it."

"You do not have to tell me why," she said, "and I would rather you did not, and the reason I would rather is that in nine years nobody who has given me a card reference has ever come back and used it, and the four times somebody has, the four times it was somebody who was already in the book."

He had not used it because a card reference is a thread, and a thread is a thing you pull once.

He asked for the page.

She found it in about four seconds, which told him that a person who has never asked to see a page is not a person who has to be searched for.

**RIDDELL, GAYNOR. SC/2/4. Two-day residential, delivered at Concourse B, Licensing, ground floor. Registered 9 May 2022. Funded by Standish Care Limited, Calder Street.**

And then, in the status column, the narrowest column in the book, which had been the narrowest since about 2019 because somebody in 2019 had decided it should be:

nothing at all.

"**She is not lapsed," said the woman at the counter. "I want to be careful, because that is the question everybody asks and it is the wrong one, and a status column that is blank does not mean a person has gone. A status column that is blank means there is nothing to put in it.**"

"Then what goes in it."

"**Words.**" She said it the way she said everything about that book, which was as if she were defending it. "The column is two and a half millimetres wide and there is a printer in this building with a template that has been the same since 2019, and in 2019 the longest thing anybody had to say in that column was *reissued*, and *reissued* is eight letters and it fits. **I have watched that column be made narrow and I have watched the words get longer.**"

"Longer than reissued."

"**There are nine other blank ones on those pages,**" said the woman at the counter, "and I have counted them about four times in nine years, and I have never once been asked about them by anybody, and I have wanted to say this next sentence since about 2019 and I have never had a reason to say it to a stranger on a Wednesday afternoon, and here is a stranger on a Wednesday afternoon. A word that does not fit in a two-and-a-half-millimetre column does not get written. It gets not written. And then the column is blank, and a blank in a status column is not a thing anybody can be shown, and I have a feeling — and I am putting it to you as a feeling — that there are people in this city who are extremely pleased about that."**

"Have you looked?"

"I have looked at every one of the nine and I have not looked at what is behind them, because behind them is a file and a file is not this." She closed the book over her thumb. "**And I will tell you the other half, because you have not asked me for it and you have not asked me for any of it, and that is now twice in one afternoon.** The rule is not secrecy. There is no rule. The page is a page and anybody may read it, and there is a card in front of it that says so. The rule is that when somebody in this building has a reason to write a word in that column and the word does not fit, the honest thing to do is to widen the column, and widening the column is a change to a public record, and a change to a public record requires a delegation, and the delegation is not mine, and in nine years I have watched the column be narrow and I have watched the words get longer and **nobody has ever asked me for a wider column, including the eleven people who work here.**"

"Whose delegation is it."

"**That is the whole book and nobody in it knows, and I have looked, and looking took me two years.**" She let go of the cover and stood up straighter than she had come in. "A delegation is not a person in this city. A delegation is a line in a schedule somewhere that says *Licensing may amend the public register in the following respects*, and there is one of those, and it is four lines long, and I have read it, and two of the four lines have never been used in nine years and the other two are about correcting a person's name when a person has changed their name, which is the only thing anybody has ever wanted out of this book. **So there is a facility, in print, in this city, for amending a public register, and it has been used about nine times in nine years and every single one of those nine was a name, and not one of them was anything else.**"

"And nobody has used it to widen a column."

"**Nobody has used it for anything at all, and I have been eleven years in this chair and I have watched about four hundred people walk past that book without touching it, and the ones who touch it are the ones who want something out of it, and you are the first person in two months who has touched it and walked away.**"

He did not copy it. He did not write the reference in his own book, and he did not write the name, and when she asked him at the end whether he wanted the page marked, which she offered to do with a slip of paper and a pen, and which would have taken twenty seconds and cost nothing, he said no, and gave the reason, because he had learned in the last four months that a reason given once is a thing a person can defend later.

"A gap in a column is not a record," he said. "**It is the absence of one. And I have spent four months trying to get an absence into a file in this city and there is no box for it anywhere, and if I take a slip of paper off that counter with that word on it then in about two years somebody will be able to say a man in a good coat was interested in that person, and there will be no way on this earth for me to say what I was interested in.**"

"That," said the woman at the counter, "is the first sensible thing anybody has said at this counter in about a year, and I have heard about nine hundred things, and I would like you to know that it is also the only sentence said at this counter in nine years that describes what this counter is."

He said thank you and was going, and stopped, and opened the book again at a page about nine back, which he did with two fingers and without asking, and she watched him do it and said nothing.

There was a name on that page in the alphabetical run, and a course reference, and a registration date, and in the status column a word that was not blank and was not *reissued*.

He looked at it for about two seconds.

He knew the woman. He had sat in a kitchen in another district in March and she had put a folder on the table before he had spoken and had said *this is true and I want that said first, out loud, before anything else*, and she was twenty-nine, and she had had no shift since the second week of February, and everything she wanted that year was to be a person who had a gap.

He closed the book over his thumb, the way she had, and he did not ask for the page, and he did not write anything, and he did not go back to that page, and when he looked up the woman at the counter was watching him with the particular expression of a person who has been in a chair for eleven years and can tell what a person has just decided not to do.

"Do not tell me about it," he said.

"I was not going to," she said, "and I have never told anybody about anything, and that is not a boast, it is the job."

He thanked her and was going, and the chair by the end of the second row was still there, and it was the chair he had been put in in February, and it was empty.

"You're going," said the woman at the counter, "and you have been in here for forty minutes and you have asked to see one page and half of another and you have not asked me for one thing off this book. **In nine years, you are the first person who has come back into this room for a different person.** Do you know how many people come back into this room at all? Six. I could tell you all six and I am not going to, because I have never been asked for one of them, and that is the only reason I have still got a job."

He said that he was sorry, and she said that he was not to be, and he went out into the ground floor of a municipal building at about ten to three on a Wednesday afternoon with nothing in his own book about any of it, which was the first time in four months that he had come out of a building in this city with that.
