# Chapter 0123 — A Post Is Not a Person

The front office on Corbie Street was on a Wednesday the way it is on a Tuesday, which is to say that there were four people in it and one of them was eating something out of a paper bag and the log was on a spike and the spike was a different colour from the log it had held in March.

Petra Lund was thirty-three and had been in that building nine years and she had a rule, and the rule was that a general question was free and a member of the public got one, and she was not going to give it to him on a Wednesday morning because she had already given it to him on the sixth of March and the month was not over.

He bought nothing. He had never bought anything in that building and he was not going to start.

"I am not asking a question," he said. "I am asking you to point at a road."

She put the paper bag down.

"North," said Petra Lund. "**And I am going to say three things and then I am going to go back to my spike, and none of them is a question and none of them is a favour, and if you come back to this counter on Friday I will not have anything for you.** One. You cannot have the name of that building off me and you did not have it in March and you are not having it now, and I have said that to you once and I am not saying it twice. Two. North side, past the tram shed, and there is a road with a civic store on it and a civic store has got a counter and a counter has got a man on it, and that is the whole of the directions and I did not have to look them up. Three."

She stopped, and looked at him, and he waited, because she had said three.

"Three is that a man came in here on the twenty-second of March and asked me whether anybody had ever asked a question about a Friday, and I said no." She picked the paper bag up again. "**I did not say yes and I did not say no, and he did not notice that I had not answered him, and he is welcome to come back on Friday and ask me properly and get a proper answer, and I am telling you that it is not going to be the same answer, and that is all I have got and it is not even an answer.**"

He wrote that down on the back of his left hand in biro, which he had not done since March, and it came out as three words and a smudge, and he did not ask her what she meant, because he had worked out in five months that when a woman in this city said *that is not an answer* she was telling him the shape of the answer and expecting him to go and find the rest of it himself.

The tram shed was where she said it was. The road past it had a civic store on one side with a blue sign and a ramp and a bin store at the end, and the store took deliveries on a weekday and the loading bay was empty at about ten to one, and the doors were two of them, one for people and one for trolleys, and the one for people had a bell on it that somebody had wrapped in cloth.

---

The counter was at the back, past nine rows of shelving, and the man behind it was about forty-four and had a tie on and was eating a sandwich in four pieces which he had put on a sheet of wax paper and had folded up around the fourth piece, and the fold was tidy and it was the tidiest thing in the building.

"Can I help you."

"I am a member of the public," said Elias Venn. "I want to look at the index of transfers in."

The man put the sandwich down.

"Right," he said. "**Sit there. Not there — there. The chair by the end of the second row, because that is where the light is and because the chair by the window is where anybody who wanted to be looked at sits.** I have had two hundred and something of you in this chair since I started and about forty of them have tried to take a photograph of the index with a telephone and all forty of them have been asked to stop by a man who did not enjoy it."

"What may I see."

"Any day in the last seven years, and any premises, and you may read it and you may not copy it, and you may not have a page, and I will not turn the book for you if you have got a wet hand, and if you want to write anything down you write it on your own paper and not on ours." He was already going down the row. "**And before you start: there is no charge for looking and there is no charge for asking and there is nothing you can do in this room that I have to write a form for, and that is a deliberate arrangement and it is the only deliberate arrangement in this building.**"

"Deliberate by whom."

"Not by me. Ask me again in an hour and I will still not know." He came back with a hardback book the size of a telephone directory and put it on the table with the spine away from the person sitting down and the pages open at a marker made of a strip of card. "Premises and date. Go."

Elias Venn read.

The book was ruled in five columns and the first two were a form number and a date, and the third was a premises, and the fourth was headed **RECEIVED BY**, and the fifth was headed **RUN** and carried either a letter or a number, and most of the lines had a letter in it.

The twelfth of December 2029 was on two pages. On the first of them there were nine lines and on the second there were fourteen, and the fourth line from the top of the first page read:

> *F-2214/09 — 12.12.29 — 2 Anselm St Kettle Rows (residential, contracted) — R/O 3 — 14*

He read it four times and it was the same four times.

"**R/O 3**," he said. "What is that."

"Received by. Post three." The man had gone back to the shelf and was doing something with a box. "It is a post and it is not a name and I am not going to be asked for a name because there is not one in the book, and if I looked one up for you I would be inventing it."

"What is post three."

"Whosever it is on the day it is in." He put the box down. "**And that is the whole of what I can give you and I have given it to about nine people in four years and it is the same nine words every time, and the ninth one asked me to put it in writing and I said no, because there is a difference between a post and a person and if a post goes in your notebook as a person then in about two years you will be at somebody's door about a thing that rota did.**"

Elias Venn did not write it down. He had the page in front of him and he wrote four things in his own book afterwards, in a hand that got worse as it went, and the four things were the form number and the date and the premises and the number in the fifth column, and he left the fourth column out, and the man watched him leave it out and said nothing about it.

"You have not asked me the obvious thing," the man said.

"What is that."

"Who was in the room."

"Yes."

"**I can tell you which letter a transfer is on, and I cannot tell you who was in the room, and both of those are in the same book, and they are in different columns, and the column I can read you is the one that is not a person.**" He came and stood at the end of the table. "About four people are permitted to be in the building when a form like that one is signed, and the building that gets signed in is not this building, it is the one the file is going to, and the signatory signs it in the corridor outside that room and then carries it down the stairs. That is the arrangement. That is not a secret, it is a set of stairs. I have signed about nine hundred of them and I am not going to guess at a fourth name for a stranger in a chair, and if somebody in this city ever wants to know who was in a corridor on a Wednesday evening in December 2029 then the answer is in a rota, and a rota is a document, and a document is a thing you ask the owner of for."

"And a rota is not a thing a member of the public can ask for."

"A rota is not a thing anybody can ask for except the people in it," said the man. "You would have to be a person in it. You are not in it. I have been in this building for eleven years and I have not asked for one either, and that is not because I do not know what I am allowed to ask for."

"You do know."

"I know exactly what I am allowed to ask for," said the man, "which is nothing, and I have known it for eleven years, and there is a difference between not knowing and knowing, and it is the only difference in this building and I have got quite good at it."

---

A woman of about seventy-two came in at about half past one with a plastic folder and a piece of paper with a number on it, and the man said, without turning round, "Sit there, the same chair."

"It is a charge," said Elias Venn.

"It is a charge," said the man. "**Go on.**"

He went and came back with four inches of card in a plastic sleeve, and he put it on the table in front of her and turned it round, and it was a photograph of a shelf, and there was no photograph of a person on it anywhere.

"Eleven forty-one," he said. "That is your number, and you may look at the top sheet only, and if there is a page with a person on it in the middle of it then that page is somebody else's business and you will not see it, and that is not me being careful, that is the law and it has been the law since before I came here."

The woman had her hand on the plastic sleeve and did not pick it up straight away.

"They said Wednesday."

"They said Wednesday and it is Wednesday and it is eleven forty-one, and I have never once known a Wednesday to fail." He went back to the shelf. "**And I will tell you what the top sheet is, because you have not asked and everybody who sits in that chair does not ask. It is a list of what the file is, and how many sheets, and which of them are about you. Your name is on one of them and it is not the top one, and that is the way it has been since I started, and I have never had anybody query it and I have never had anybody write down that they had noticed it either.**"

"Thank you," said the woman, and read it, and put the sleeve down carefully the way you put a plate down.

Elias Venn sat in a chair by the end of the second row of shelving and watched a person of seventy-two walk out of a civic store at about ten past two on a Wednesday afternoon with four inches of card in a plastic sleeve, and understood something about the building he was sitting in that he had not understood in March.

In that building a person is not a person. A person is a number, and a number is on a piece of paper, and the piece of paper has to have been written by somebody in a building with a stamp pad, and the only thing in the world that can make you a person in there is a charge against your name, and a charge against your name is not a thing anybody is given. It is a thing that happens to you.

He had nothing. He had a row of a book that he had read four times and a post number and a form number and four things in his own handwriting, and there was no piece of paper anywhere in the world that said Elias Venn was waiting for something, and there was no box for one.

---

He got the Friday out of him at about twenty past two and it took nine seconds.

There was a board on the wall by the counter with a week on it and seven columns and about a hundred and forty buildings down the side of it, and each of the columns had a letter at the head of it.

"That is the collection round," said the man. "Vans. Whatever has been listed for removal from a building goes out on the day that building is on. And the north of this city has been a Friday since before the war, and the reason it is a Friday is that the round goes out and comes back on the Saturday and the paper is unloaded on the Monday and the office that listed it wants to be able to say the paper went out on a Friday, and that is the whole of the reason."

"Nobody has ever asked you for that."

"Nobody has ever asked me for that," said the man, "**because a van is not a person.** I have watched two hundred and something people come through that door and not one of them has ever looked at that board, and you have looked at it, and you are the first, and I would think less of you if you had not asked me why rather than who."

Somewhere four streets south of a tram shed there was a blue door with a sign on it and a yard behind it with the bins in it, and about a dozen people had come through that yard door in four years, all of them on a Friday, and a man behind the counter in there had never once worked out why it was a Friday, and it was because a van went out of a building on the north side of this city on a Friday afternoon and came back on the Saturday, and the paper on it went out of a premises, and a letter went to a person because a piece of paper had gone somewhere in a van, and four buildings had known that for eleven years and not one of them had ever said it out loud to anybody, because there is no box for it and because nobody had ever needed to.

And then, underneath that, and this was the part he stood in the corridor about for a minute afterwards with his notebook shut in his hand:

The line in the index for the twelfth of December 2029 was not on a Friday.

The twelfth of December 2029 was a Wednesday, and the entry in the fifth column on that line was not a round at all, it was a number with a tick beside it, and the man in the store looked at it for about two seconds and then said the thing that took the whole of the weight out of the morning.

"**Fourteen,**" he said. "That is not a round. Fourteen is a hand-carried transfer. **That is somebody carrying a file down a corridor with a form, at a quarter to seven in the evening, on a day that is not on my board, with about four people in the corridor, and the signature is the signature of whoever was holding it, and it is not a signature of mine because I do not go in at that hour and have never gone in at that hour.**"

"How many of those are there."

"Nine in eleven years that I have seen and about forty that I have not, because I do not keep the ones I am not in the building for." He put his hand flat on the board. "A round takes what has been listed. A hand-carried one is for something that could not wait for the Friday, and every one of the nine I have seen was somebody's decision, and not one of the nine was mine, and I have never once known why."

"And the person who signs it."

"Signs it and carries it and puts it on the shelf and the shelf is in a room and the room has a door and about four people are allowed in that room and I am one of the four this week." He stopped. "**And you are not going to ask me which room, and I am not going to tell you, and it is a different building and it is about two miles from here, and that is the last thing I am going to say to you today.**"

Elias Venn stood in a chair by the end of the second row of shelving on a Wednesday afternoon in March and found out that the whole of the ordinary arrangement in this city was a Friday and a van, and that the one night he was interested in had gone in on a Wednesday, by hand, ten minutes after somebody had said four words into a telephone, and that a thing like that is not a conspiracy and does not need to be, and is not going to be in a book, and that there was no way whatever to stand at the bottom of that corridor and ask who was on it, because the answer to that was a post, and a post is a word and not a name, and a word is not a door.
