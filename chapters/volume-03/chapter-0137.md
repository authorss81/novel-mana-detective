# Chapter 0137 — Not Relevant To The Account

The basement of Concord Hall was down eleven steps and along a corridor with a concrete floor and a light that had a crack in it, and at the end of the corridor there was a door that said **EXTRACTS**, and the door was open, and there were two desks in the room and about nine hundred manila wallets on shelves along one wall, and the shelves had numbers on the ends of them.

The woman at the first desk was forty-nine and had been doing it nineteen years and her name was Ena Whitlow and she asked him what it was before he had got to the second desk.

"If it is about an extract, I am the person. If it is about a copy, it is a different floor. **If it is about whether somebody is allowed to see one, that is the man upstairs and I have not got anything for you.**"

"It is about an extract."

"Then sit down, because the ones who sit down get an answer and the ones who stand get a form." She put her hand on a tray. "What record."

"Any of them. I want to know how one is made."

"**Then that is the best question you have had down here in about four years and I am going to answer it before you ask it, because I have been waiting nineteen years and nobody has ever asked me and it has started to look like a thing nobody can be blamed for.**"

---

"It comes off the record," said Ena Whitlow, "and it is two pages, and I make it, and that is the whole of my trade.

"You put in an order. The order is a sheet with boxes on it. The record — a box with the number. The account — a box. The officer who wants it — a box, and that is a name, because an officer is a person and a person is somebody who can be telephoned about it. The purpose — a box, and that is a free box and I have seen it written *general* about four hundred times. The date. And my initials in the corner, which are mine, because the thing is made by me and I am not hiding that.

"**And there is no box on it that says the witness. There is no box that says what the account said. And there is no box anywhere on that sheet that anybody could write a question in.**"

"How long."

"Ninety seconds if I know the record, four minutes if I do not." She said it as a person who has been asked how long a thing takes. "**The two pages are the front and the last and the bit in the middle that says the act, and the parts I leave out are the parts that are not about the account, and I know what is about the account because it is on the order, because the officer has written what he wants, and the officer's writing is not a suggestion, it is a description of two pages, and I make those two pages.**"

"Who decides what is not about the account."

She put her pen down.

"**You have just asked me the only question I have, so I am going to answer it properly and then I am going to say the rest of it, and I have been saying the rest of it to myself in this room for nineteen years and it is a terrible thing to have said to nobody.** The officer decides. I do not decide. I have never decided. **And I am not going to be able to tell you that in a way that makes it sound like anybody's fault, so I am going to tell you what I am.** I am a person who is extremely good at a thing, and the thing is cutting a record down to two pages, and I have been cutting records down to two pages since I was thirty, and I have done about nine hundred of them, and the better I have got the fewer words I have had to read, and I have never once in nineteen years asked a single officer why a page came out, and I have never once in nineteen years asked a single witness whether they wanted to see what was left in.

"**And I want you to sit there and understand what I am, because you have the face of a man who has come down eleven steps to find somebody guilty and there is nobody down here. There is a room and a tray and a woman who is fast.**"

"I believe you."

"You should not, and that is not a criticism of you, that is a criticism of the stair." She picked her pen up again. "So the order comes in, and I cut it, and it goes back up in a tray, and the officer puts it in front of a person and asks him whether he agrees with it. **And the reason you are here on a Tuesday morning and not on a Friday afternoon is that everything about that is on a Wednesday and a Thursday in about four offices, and there is no room in this building that anybody could put a camera in.**"

She got up and took a wallet off a shelf and opened it on the desk without being asked, because she had read the shape of the question and had decided he was entitled.

"**That is an extract. That is the top of it. Two pages and it is a real record and everything on it is true.**"

"Then anybody can have one of those."

"**Anybody can have one of those in about ninety seconds, if they can fill in an order and if an officer wants one, and the officer is the part that is not a form.**" She put the wallet back. "And yes, before you ask it: you can have an extract of a record of issue. **A record of issue is a record. It is the only kind of paper in that building that is about an office's own act, and an office's own act is a record, and a record can be extracted, and I have made four of them in nineteen years and all four were about money.**"

Elias Venn sat very still in a chair in a basement in a municipal building with two records of issue in the inside pocket of his coat, in his own handwriting, neither copied, neither photographed, neither in a file, and understood that the piece of paper in his pocket was not a receipt and was not a curiosity and was a **record**, and that a record can be cut down to two pages in ninety seconds by a woman who is fast, and that no warrant in this city could have got him either one.

He did not say any of that. He said, "Thank you," and got up, and she said, "You did not ask me the other thing."

"What other thing."

"**You have been in a chair for twenty minutes and you have not once asked me whether I would be willing to leave out a different page, and every man who comes down these eleven steps asks me that inside a minute, and the ones who do not ask it are the ones I think about at four o'clock.**"

"The question I came with has to be produced by a piece of paper and there has not been one piece of paper in this city for nine years," said Elias Venn, "and if I do not produce it then I am not entitled to ask you that, and you would be right to say no."

Ena Whitlow looked at him for about four seconds.

"Then I will say the thing I would have said if you had asked," she said, "and then you can go up the stairs, and I am going to be annoyed for the rest of the afternoon. **I have never been asked what I leave out. Nineteen years. And if somebody asked me today, this week, in this room, I would not be able to tell them, because I do not know what I leave out. I make what I am given. I have never had the whole of one of these records in my hands in nineteen years and I have never asked to.**"

"I am not going to ask you."

"I know you are not," she said. "**That is why I have just told you.**"

He went up the eleven steps and across the ground floor, because the second counter of the Proof Registry is on the same floor and it is the one with a card in the window that says CLOSED, and *back 11* in biro on the glass, and he had been in that building three times in nine weeks and had never once gone to it.

The door behind the second counter was open at about a quarter past one, and a woman of about thirty came out onto a fire escape with a cup in her hand and stood in the air for about two minutes and went back in, and the card went up on the glass about ninety seconds later, and nobody on the pavement looked up.

He stood on the pavement in the middle of Morrow Hill at about twenty past one on a Tuesday afternoon and understood that there were two counters in that building and that he had been sent to the one that talks, and that the other one is the one that signs, and that between them they had made a very small and extremely complete machine, and that neither of them was hiding anything from him, because there was nothing to hide and because nobody in that building had ever once been curious about the other half of what it did.

He did not go back in. He had been refused twice in four minutes on the same ground by the same answer, and a third refusal would have been the same answer again, and there is a point at which a man who keeps going back to a counter has stopped doing an enquiry and started doing a grievance, and he had made that mistake in February at a records store and had been asked to leave it.

---

The public counter at 14 Harrow Lane was on the ground floor and the woman of about fifty-four was behind it in a cardigan over a uniform, reading a document upside down, and she looked at him and put it down.

"You have had a record of issue for a night in Kettle Rows and one for a ward in Wexford Row, both in your own hand, both in March, and I have neither of them in front of me and neither of them has been in this building since the day you wrote them out at that table."

"You have kept them."

"**I keep everything for about four months and then I do not, and that is a retention period and it is on the front sheet of a file and not inside it, and I have said a number that is not an answer to you before and I am not doing it twice.**" She squared a tray. "What do you want."

"The name of the registrar who countersigned the second one."

She did not hesitate. He had expected her to hesitate and she did not, and that was the answer.

"**No,**" said the woman at the counter, in about eleven words, and then she stopped, and then she said, "That is the same answer I would have given in February and it is the same answer I gave you the other day, and the reason it does not change is that it was not mine to begin with, and the person you are asking about is not me."

"It has not changed in a month."

"It has not changed in nine years." She put the tray down. "**And I am going to say the rest of it because you are the fourth person in a year to come to that counter and be refused and the first three got angry and you are not going to, and the rest of it is this. A registrar's name is not a secret. It is on the paper. It is on the register and it is on a form anybody may inspect and there is no form in this city that says keep this name quiet. What I cannot do is show a member of the public what a name is for, and to show what a name is for you have to show a proceeding, and to have a proceeding somebody has to be entitled to start one, and you are not a party to anything and you have not got an instrument and there is no form for a man with a piece of paper who cannot say what the paper is for.**"

He had a ground. He had had it since the eighth of April, when a woman with twenty-six years' service had told him that the right to know who was in the room with you belongs to the person in the building and not to him, and he had carried it about in a book for a fortnight, and there was no use in it at all.

"Suppose I could show you," he said. "Not today. Suppose in three weeks I could put a sheet of paper in front of you that said what a name is for."

"Then I would read it and I would give you the name in about four seconds, and I have always said that, and nobody has ever done it." She looked at him steadily. "**And I will tell you what I think, for nothing, because you are the only one of the four who has not shouted: I do not think there is a piece of paper coming. I think there is not one piece of paper in this city that has been written since about 2011 about the thing you are actually asking about, and I think four offices have quietly agreed that it is somebody else's, and I have said that out loud to three people this year and two of them were from this building.**"

"That is the fourth time somebody has said that to me."

"Then you have been in this city for a sensible number of months," said the woman at the counter, and went back to her document, upside down.

Concourse B was on the ground floor and the door of it was open and there were eight chairs in it and about five of them had a coat on the back of them, and he was on the threshold and did not go in and did not count them, and Constance Vail was at the front with a folder and a list, and she had been teaching since before the building had a car park.

"You have not come for the list," she said.

"No."

"**Then I can give you one thing and it is the only thing, and I have been a trainer for twenty-two years and I have never once been asked it, and you are the first person to ask me about a page and not about a class.** The course is taught out of that manual. The manual is the course. There is no second document and there is no lecturer's version and there is nothing I hand round in a room that is not in there in print, and it is on a wire rack three inches from the Schedule for three credits, and about nine people a year buy their own place.

"So if a person is ever shown that page before they sign something, then it has come out of the rack, and the rack is not a secret, and I have taught every delivery in this room since about 2008 and I could tell you which years I taught which paragraph off which printing."

"Could you."

"**I could, and you are not getting it written down, because the moment it is written down it is a document and I have spent twenty-two years in a building where documents have a delegation and I am not going to be the woman who starts one.**" She put the folder down. "But I will say this, once, and then I am going to teach. **In twenty-two years I have never been in a room when a witness was shown that page before he signed anything. And I have taught about four hundred people to hold a sequence in their heads for two days and then go home. And if that ever happens — if a person anywhere in this city is shown that page before he is asked to agree to something — then that is not the course failing. That is the only thing in twenty-two years that has ever worked.**"

She looked at the clock on the wall.

"The next delivery is in May," said Constance Vail. "Twenty-two places. Eleven of them are booked and I have four names on this list that I have never spoken to and I will speak to all four in the first hour, and that is what I am for."

---

Elias Venn went up the eleven steps and out into the ground floor of a municipal building at about ten past three on a Tuesday afternoon with two things he had come for and one of them had cost him a page, and the other had cost him nothing at all, which was worse.

The bin in the corner of the basement corridor was a steel one on a frame and it was about two-thirds full of paper, and every piece of it in it was a page that had been taken out of a record, and he looked at it for a while.

"That's the waste," said Ena Whitlow, from the doorway. "Two hundred a week, roughly, and about nine in ten of them are one sheet. **And I empty it on a Friday, and I have emptied it on a Friday for nineteen years, and it goes into a sack in the yard and the yard men take it on a Monday.**"

"Do you ever look in it."

"**No,**" said Ena Whitlow. "**And I have thought about that more than I have thought about anything else in nineteen years, and I have decided that not looking in it is the job, and that the reason I have decided it is that the alternative is a woman in a basement who has read nine hundred records, and I would not be a person anybody could sit in a room with.**"
