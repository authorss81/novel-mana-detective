# Chapter 0110 — Nothing

He got to 2 Anselm Street at about ten to nine on Wednesday morning with nothing at all, which was the first time in five months that he had gone anywhere with nothing at all, and it took him until about eleven to work out that he had done it on purpose.

The office door was open. The list was on the desk. There was a cup of tea on the desk with a plate under it to protect the wood, which was a thing Perpetua Ayodele had done every day for six years and had never once mentioned.

"Can I ask you a question and get an answer from you, or is it one of the ones where you get nothing."

"It depends on the question."

"How many of the forty-one beds were full on the night of the ninth of December."

She did not answer, and she did not look away, and she picked her pen up and put it down again.

"No," she said.

"Right."

"Not because I will not tell you," said Perpetua Ayodele. "Because I do not know, and I have been thinking about that question since Friday, which is a ridiculous thing to have happened to me, and I have worked out the reason I have been thinking about it, and it is this. In four years nobody has ever come in here and asked me for a number. Not you, not the trust, not the council, not a man from the housing association who came in the summer of 2027 and looked at a fire door for twenty minutes and did not look at anything else. So if eleven of them had been told there would be a list, and there is not a list, and I have gone round the building looking for it for two days in my own head, and there is nothing, and **I have been the day worker on this building for six years and I did not know that until this morning.**"

"Which means nobody was told."

"Which means I cannot say they were not told," said Perpetua Ayodele. "It means I have got nothing, and you have got nothing, and if I give you a number it will be a number I have made up out of a Tuesday in December, and I am not going to do that, and there is a man of thirty-four asleep in the third chair and if he wakes up while I am inventing a figure in front of a stranger I will not be able to work here again."

"That is exactly right," said Elias Venn.

"I know it is exactly right. I have known it was exactly right since about eleven o'clock yesterday morning and I have been waiting for you to come and ask me so that I could say it out loud to somebody." She turned the list over and showed him the back of it, which was blank. "**The only record of who was in that building on any night in twelve years is the fifth column of a night book, and the fifth column of the ninth of December says *up 23.10, back 23.40, door, off*, and that is four words and a time and it is the best thing anybody has got, and it is better and worse than a number, and I am not writing a number on the back of this list and if you write one in your book I will know and I will ask you for it.**"

"I am not going to write a number."

"I know that as well." She put the list back. "Now. Have you come to say you have not got what you came for."

"I have come to say I have not got what I came for," said Elias Venn, "and I have got two other things, and I am going to say both of them because I have learned in five months that a man who only says the useful half turns up a second time and is not believed the first time."

---

She listened to both of them standing up, with her arms folded, in an office with nine chairs against the wall.

"The first one is that the certificate is a selection and not a lie, and I have got a piece of paper that says so, and it is two credits and a signature and it is in a bag and I am not showing it to you and I am not going to be asked to."

"I am not going to ask you."

"I know. That is the second thing." She unfolded her arms. "The second thing is that I have promised a man that nobody is to be told where he is, and I said it on Thursday in a chip shop in about nine words, and I am telling you that I have said it, and I am telling you that I have told you for about one reason, and the reason is that you have been in this office three times and you have not asked me for a thing that was not mine to give, and if I am wrong about the reason then I have made a mistake and I would rather have made it in front of you than behind you."

"You have," said Elias Venn. "I have. I am telling you for the same reason and I want it on the record that it is the same reason and not a better one."

"Good," said Perpetua Ayodele. "Then the two of you can be wrong in the same building and that is at least something."

---

He telephoned the front office at Corbie Street at ten past four in the afternoon from a box on the corner of Corrie Street, because the box was there and because he had a rule about telephones in the last five months that he had never told anybody about and which was that he did not make them from his own shop.

"Can I ask a general question."

"You have asked one on Wednesday and one on Friday and I said one each, and you have now asked for a third, and the answer to the third is going to be no."

"Then I shall put the phone down."

"You shall not," said Petra Lund, "and I have been thinking about you all week and I have a thing to say and I am going to say it and then you can have your no and I will not feel bad about it." There was a pause with about nine sounds in it. "I have read what happened in January. All of it, in the papers, four paragraphs and a photograph of a building on a parade. I know about a man being suspended and I know about a certificate and I know about a door, and I do not know the half of what I have been told, and I have a drawer in this building with about nine hundred forms in it and I have worked out something and I am going to tell you what it is and then you can tell me I am talking nonsense."

"Go on."

"**There is a form for everything.** Not a good form. Not a form anybody designed with a person's head on. A form for the thing, every time, including the thing you would not think of. I have got one for reporting a stolen bicycle. I have got one for a person who is in a cell and cannot get a message to somebody. I have got one for saying that a form has gone missing. There is a drawer in this building that is a **map of every single thing this city can do to you and be written down about, and every one of the nine hundred is numbered and every one of them has a box somewhere on it for a thing that is not the thing.**"

"Like what."

"Like the reason. On the bicycle form there is a box for *how the bicycle was taken* and a box for *where it was last seen by the owner* and there is a box for *was the owner drinking*, and I have been putting *no* in that box since 2021 because I do not ask that question any more, and the box is not gone, and somebody put it there." She stopped. "So when a man comes in here and asks how a person withdraws an account, and I give him the answer, and the answer is a form, and the form has a tick already in it, that is not a conspiracy and it is not anybody. **It is just a drawer. It is nine hundred forms and every one of them was written down by somebody sensible, on a Tuesday, about a different thing.** And there is no drawer in this city with in it a form for *a person's account being different from a document*, and there never has been, and I have looked, and I have been in nine of the eleven buildings in this city that have a drawer in them, and the one I have not been in is Harrow Lane, and I am not going to be able to be in Harrow Lane because they are not open to the public on a Wednesday afternoon without an appointment and I cannot make an appointment because I am not a member of the public and I am a **counter assistant** and there is a difference between those two things in this city that comes down to about eleven pounds a year."

He stood in a box on the corner of Corrie Street with a telephone in his hand.

"Ask your question," said Petra Lund.

"Can a member of the public buy a copy of a dangerous-working certificate."

"You have already bought one."

"I have."

"Then you have your answer, and I have not been a help." She let about a second go past. "**And that is the difference between a certificate and a record of issue, and you are going to need it in about a week and I am going to give it to you now because you have asked me two questions in two weeks and I have run out of anybody else to tell.** A certificate is the thing. It is a page with four acts in it and a box and a signature and a fee and a date, and you can walk into Harrow Lane with a claim number and buy one for two credits, and it will be true in every line, including the four acts, including the release, including the night, including the premises, and it will say the sequence is complete. A record of issue is a different office and a different building and it is a piece of paper that says a certificate was issued, and it has a number on it and no acts on it, and it costs about the same, and it is the only thing in that building that will tell you the truth about a certificate without telling you anything about the sequence."

"And if the certificate is a selection of a true thing, then the record of issue is true and the certificate is true."

"The record of issue is true and the certificate is true and one of them is a lie by what it leaves out, and I have been at this counter for nine years and I have never been able to work out how those three things go together, and I am not going to work it out on a Wednesday at twenty past four with a box on the corner of Corrie Street." Her voice did not change. "**But I will tell you the thing I would like said out loud, because you have asked me questions properly for two weeks and nobody has ever done that, and it is this. I have got a drawer with nine hundred forms in it and every one of them is a person doing their job.** And if you go and find out that somebody has done something wrong, then you are going to find out that a person did it, and that person is going to have a name and a job and a reason, and about nine of them will be frightened, and one of them will be comfortable. **And I do not know how anybody finds the uncomfortable one and I have thought about that for four months and it is the only thing I have thought about.**"

---

He rang Nadia Kest at four twenty-five from the same box.

She said hello in a way that meant she had been expecting it, and then said, "Right," and then waited, which she had never done to him before.

"Nadia Kest. Wednesday the sixth of March. I have not got a case number and I have not got a name and I have got a piece of paper in a bag with two credits' worth of paper on it and a rule in a laboratory with a date on it."

"That is a lot for a man with nothing," said Nadia Kest. "Go on."

"What have you got?"

"Nothing yet," said Elias Venn.

There was a pause on the line long enough that he could hear a door close on the other end of it and a corridor change.

"Say that again," said Nadia Kest.

"Nothing yet."

"You have said that to me before."

"I have."

"You said it to me in a lane behind Corrie Street in November and I let it go, and you have said it to me twice since, in a car park and on a telephone, and I have let it go three times, and I am not going to let it go a fourth time and I want you to hear exactly what I am about to say because I am saying it for the record and not for me." Her voice did not rise. "**I am not going to ask you. That is a decision I am making on purpose and not a thing I have forgotten about, and I have made it about you four times now, and I have never told you I was making it, and I am telling you now.** And here is the other half of it, and it is for the record and it is not a threat and I am not going to repeat it. **This is the last time that costs you nothing.** If you ring me and say *nothing yet* a fifth time, then I will not ring you and I will not meet you and I will not write to you, and I will not do it out of spite, and I will do it because I am a police officer and there is a man in this city with a certificate number in his head that he is not allowed to write down, and if I have told you four times that I will not ask and then I have found out what he has been doing, then I have spent four months of a person's trust on a man who was going to use the fifth one to tell me anyway."

"I understand."

"I am not certain that you do, and I am not going to check, because checking is asking." She let a second go. "**But I am going to tell you one thing that I am permitted to tell you, because you have not asked me for it and I have decided that a man who is not going to be told anything deserves to be given something.** On Friday a detective sergeant of twelve years went to a Deputy Chief and asked to be relieved of a case that closed on the ninth of January, and he gave a reason that was about his own conduct, and it was not his conduct, and I know that because I have been the person who reads those reasons for twenty-two years and I have never once been able to do anything with any of them." She paused. "He is not suspended. He asked for it and it was not granted and he is still in a corridor at Corbie Street and he has a rota that ends on Friday."

"Friday the seventh."

"Friday the seventh," said Nadia Kest. "And I am not going to tell you anything else, and I have not told you his name, and I have not told you that he is a man who wrote four lines in a hardback notebook in a second-hand shop, and I do not know how you know any of that and I have decided that I am not going to find out, and I am going to put the phone down now."

"Elias."

"Yes."

"You asked her," said Nadia Kest, "you asked me at a counter, and you asked her at four o'clock on a Wednesday, and I have sat here and listened to a man describe a form to a telephone and it is the best description of a form I have ever heard, and you have been in eleven buildings in five months and you have knocked on nine doors and you have been refused eight times and it is the ninth one that has just told you the thing that matters, and I have been trying to work out whether that is luck or whether that is you, and I have been trying to work it out for two days and I have got as far as not knowing, and I am not going to resolve it in a corridor, and that is a thing I am allowed to say out loud because I am not an officer at this end of this telephone." She did not put the phone down. "So I am going to say this instead, once, and then never: **whatever you have got that you are not telling me about, there is about a fortnight in it for somebody who can help you and about four years in it for a man on a good coat on his own, and I know which of those two I would rather be on, and I have known since about a week after you lost your case.** Ask somebody. Today. Not me. Somebody with a warrant and a coat who is allowed to go into a building. And when you do, ring me and say the word *asked*, and not anything else, and I will know exactly what it means, and I will not ask you one further question about it for as long as I live."

"Understood," said Elias Venn.

"Good," said Nadia Kest. "I have to go and open a box on a floor that shuts at five. It is four hundred boxes and I have the key to the room and I have not been in it since January, and it is the fourth box of somebody's correspondence I have been sent to look through this month and I have found a cat in one of them, which nobody believed me about for nine days. Ring me when you have asked. Not before."

He put the phone down in a box on the corner of Corrie Street at four thirty-one in the afternoon of Wednesday the sixth of March 2030, and stood there for a moment with the coins in his hand, and then went and had a cup of tea in a place that was not his, and wrote nothing down.

---

He got back to 3 Tanners Row at about ten past seven and Otto Vahl was on the landing with a cup of his own and a plate of crusts, and he looked at the good coat and said:

"Shop's shut an hour since. Bench is yours and I'm sixty-eight and I'm going to bed."

"I am all right."

"You have got a face on you," said Otto Vahl, "and I have been buying off you for four months and I have not asked you one question and I am not going to start, and I am going to say one thing and then I am going to bed, because I am sixty-eight." He put the cup down on the cloth by the keys. "Whatever it is, there is a man in this city who can go into a building and it is not you and it has not been you since September, and I have watched you stand on that step in every weather we have had since October and I have said nothing, and the nothing has been a job. Do not make it a second job. Go and find the man with the coat that is not a good coat and give him your coat and let him go in, and then stand on this step and count the stairs and let somebody else hold the other end of it."

Then he went to bed, and Elias Venn sat on the bench in the back room with the electric ring off and the ten-day clock going and a carrier bag with a piece of paper in it that had cost two credits and a signature.

He took the notebook out of the inside pocket of the coat, and he wrote one line, and then a second one, and the second one was not about a certificate, or a form, or a building, or a man of fifty-four in a chip shop.

It was a note of a thing he had promised, in a chip shop, on a Thursday, in nine words, to a man who worked nights, and he wrote it down for the first time in five months, in his own hand, with the time and the day on it, and **it was the first thing he had written down that he was not going to do**, and he noticed that while he was writing it, and he sat with the notebook open on his knee for about a minute and a half and did not decide anything at all, and then he shut it and put the ring on and did the thing he had not done since the twenty-second of January, which was to count the stairs.
