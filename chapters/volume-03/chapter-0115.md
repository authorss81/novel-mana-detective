# Chapter 0115 — A Woman Who Did Not Say No

This is not the same case and it is not the same district, and neither of those two facts is going to be used for anything in the next two days except to stop him from confusing them, and he wrote that in his own book on the bus at half past eight in the morning and underlined it, which he had not done to anything since November.

He had come because of a canteen. On Wednesday a man of fifty-two who worked nights in a municipal building and would not give his name had said, out loud, to a stranger, that he had still got his certificate in a drawer at home and had taken it out about six times, and Elias Venn had spent Thursday and most of Friday walking round a city looking for anybody who had ever been glad of one.

He found her at about twenty to ten in the morning in a kitchen four districts away, sitting on a table with her boots off, eating out of a paper bag, and she was twenty-nine years old and she had a folder of her own with an elastic band round it and she had brought it with her to a kitchen because she did not leave it in a van.

"Before I say anything," she said, "I am going to say three things and you are not to write anything down, and then you can ask me things."

"Go on."

"One. I do not know who you are and I have not asked and I do not want to know. Two. There is no case. I have never been in a police station in my life and I am not going to be one. Three." She put her hand flat on the folder. "**This is true. I want that said first, out loud, before anything else, because you are going to go somewhere in your face in about four seconds when I say it and I would rather you did it now than later.**"

Elias Venn had been in a laboratory at half past twelve in the morning nine days earlier and he had heard a woman of thirty-five say a version of that sentence standing up, and he had gone very still inside and had felt the whole of the six months he had been doing this come up under him like water, and he sat down on the other side of the table, because it was the only thing in the room he could do about it.

"Say the next one," he said.

"There is not a next one. Those were the three." She laughed. "You have gone the colour of the wall. All right, I have got one more and it is the one I actually want."

"Go on."

"**You are the only person in nine months who has come and asked me a question about it and it has not been a doctor.** Every single person who has wanted to know anything about that folder has been a doctor or a person from a place that wanted it for a box. And the doctors have been very kind. And I want to tell you the thing and I want to tell you it once and then I want to go back to my chips, because I have got a shift at ten tonight and I am not going to be doing it and that is the whole of what I want out of this."

"Tell me."

She told him.

---

Her name was Tamsin Rudge and she was twenty-nine and she worked nights at a shelter in that district, on the desk from ten at night to six in the morning, four nights, and she had worked there for two years and eleven months. In the second week of January a certificate came out of a district office in her own name, and it said that on a date in the previous October she had performed a complete dangerous working on her own person, and every word of it was true, and she had done it, and it took about twenty minutes, and it was hers.

"I did not know it was a certificate until somebody rang me," she said. "I thought it was a letter about my hours."

"And the certificate was for the working you did."

"It was for the working I did. **It is the only true document I have ever had in my life.** I have never had a passport, I have never had a bank, I have had a driving licence in a plastic wallet for eleven years and it is a lie half the time. And that piece of paper in that folder is true, and it is about me, and it is the only true thing anybody has ever given me, and it is ruining me."

"How."

"Two ways, and the second one is the one that has taken my shifts." She put the elastic band back on the folder. "I go to a place on Wednesdays. It is not a hospital. It is a service for people whose jobs have got into a state, and it is free, and I have been going since January, and they are lovely, and I have got a folder of my own there as well, and in that folder it says *event of 14 October, self-directed, no injury, no absence*. And that is all true. It is true in the same way. **And I am a person who has had an event.** That is what I am now, on a Wednesday, in a room, to a woman who is very good at her job. And she is not being unkind. She is being accurate. There is no other word on the sheet for me."

"What word is there."

"There is no word for me," said Tamsin Rudge. "**There is a word for an event.**"

"And the shifts."

"The second week of February a woman from the charity I work for came in and sat in the office with the folder open on the table — not the Wednesday one, mine, the real one — and she was very decent about it, and she said that they had had the certificate back from the funder, and that after an event it was their duty to look at whether a person was doing nights, and she was sorry, and would I consider a change to the pattern." Tamsin Rudge turned her cup round on the table. "**And it is not about whether I can do nights. I can do nights. I have done nights since I was nineteen. It is that the certificate says I did a dangerous working on my own person in October and did not stop, and did not report it, and was off no work, and that is a fact about a person and not about a shift, and once it is a fact about a person then the roster is not mine any more.**"

"What do you want."

"My roster," said Tamsin Rudge. "Ten to six, four nights, Thursday to Sunday. I have had it for two years. It is in that folder. There is a letter in it from January about the *access* arrangement which I have not got a word of and I am afraid to open in front of anybody."

"You want the paper to stop being true."

"I want the paper to stop existing," she said, "and it will not, and I know that, and I am not asking you to lie to anybody. I am asking whether there is a thing you can put in a box." She looked up. "Somebody said to me in January, a woman, about ninety seconds on the telephone, from the company that paid for it, and she was pleasant, and she explained what the certificate was for. **That is the only time anybody has ever explained anything to me and she did it in ninety seconds and I have thought about it every day since.** And she was pleasant about it. That is what I cannot get over. She was *pleasant*."

---

He did not take a statement. He did not offer to help. He told her both of those things in the first minute and meant them, and she looked at him and said, "Right," and moved her boots further under the table.

He spent the middle of the afternoon in a building with a counter on the ground floor and a queue and a smell of floor polish, at the offices of a county authority about four miles from the kitchen, and the woman on the counter was fifty-eight and had been there thirty-one years and her name was Philomena Rusk and she had a form nineteen in front of her.

"I have got a question and it is a specific question and I have not got a form, and I am going to tell you that up front because I have learned that a person who only says the useful half turns up a second time."

"Then I am going to like you," said Philomena Rusk. "Go on."

"**A person wants a record of theirs to stop being true, and the record is true.** Is there anywhere in this building with a box for that."

Philomena Rusk did not answer for about eight seconds, which was longer than any of the counters he had been to in six months.

"There is a box for a record that is wrong," she said. "Every record in this building that can be challenged has a box on a form for saying the record is wrong, and there are about four of those forms and I have had all four out of the drawer in my head in about six seconds and I can tell you what every one of them asks. They ask what the record says. They ask what the person says it should say instead. They ask for a date. And in all four of them there is a box with two ticks in it, and the ticks are *the record is wrong* and *the record is incomplete*, and in thirty-one years I have never once seen anybody tick *the record is correct and I do not want it*."

"That is the second half of it. The first half is: what does somebody do, in practice, if the record is right."

Philomena Rusk put her pen down flat on the counter.

"In practice they ask for it to be **restricted**," she said. "Which is a different thing and it is a real thing and it is what everybody does, and I will tell you what restricted means, because it is the only thing in this building that is worse than a lie. **Restricted means it is still true and it is still on the account and it still gets looked at by anybody who has a reason to look at it, and the only thing that has happened is that it is not printed on the front.** And every person who has ever asked me for that has wanted it because of a doctor or a family or a landlord, and every one of them has got it, and not one of them has ever said the word *unwanted* to me, and I have been waiting thirty-one years for somebody to say that word."

Elias Venn stood at that counter and felt the last two thirds of a week fall away from him, because on Tuesday a woman of thirty-nine in a car park had told him that there was one road into a number and it was a person with standing to ask, and on Wednesday a woman of fifty-five in a hall had told him that she had thought four times in twenty-two years about what happened afterwards and had never written it down because if she wrote it down somebody would have to answer it, and here on Thursday afternoon a woman of fifty-eight told him that the third of those is the answer and it is *restriction*, and it is free, and it takes a form, and it does not help.

"Thank you," he said.

"You did not get anything and you are welcome," said Philomena Rusk, and then, as he was going: "I am going to say one thing and then I am going to serve the next person. **The form for that is not in this building.** It is in the building that holds the account, and there is one of those for every organisation in this county, and I have sent eleven hundred letters to eleven hundred of those buildings in thirty-one years and I have never in my life sent one asking for a record to be unwanted, because nobody has ever asked me for it, because the question does not occur to anybody, and I do not think it is a stupid question. I think it is a question that gets asked in about eleven years, by somebody, and I would like to still be here." She looked at the clock. "It is twenty past three. If you want to know what a person looks like when they have been told that there is no box, then come back at four o'clock and watch me say it, because I have got about nine of them a week and I have got very good at it and it never once makes me comfortable."

---

He went back to the kitchen at about ten past four and Tamsin Rudge was still on the table with her boots under it, and she had a second cup of something from the machine, and he sat down and did not have any news and said so in the first sentence.

"Right," she said. "What have you got."

"I have got a woman of fifty-eight telling me there is a form for a record that is wrong and there is a form for a record that is incomplete and there is a form for a record that somebody does not want, and that the third one is in a different building, and that nobody has ever sent it, and that she would like to still be there when somebody does."

Tamsin Rudge put her cup down.

"Right," she said again. "So you have come back to tell me a thing that is not a thing."

"I have come back to tell you a thing that is not a thing," said Elias Venn, "because you are the only person in six months who has said *true* to me about a piece of paper and I did not know what to do with it, and that is the whole reason I am in a kitchen four districts away and not in the case."

She looked at him for a while.

"And you have not written anything down."

"I have written in my own book that I met you, and your first name, and the date, and that it is a different case in a different district, and that I have not written a single other word about you, and I have torn that page out and put it in my own pocket, because I have had one promise in nine words to one man in nine months and I am not going to have a second one that I have to keep to a stranger in a kitchen."

"That is the most decent thing anybody has said to me since January," said Tamsin Rudge.

Then she said: "You have got about an hour and forty minutes before I have to go and change, so ask me your one question, because I can tell you have had it in your pocket since the chips and you have not asked it because you did not want to be the man in the good coat."

He asked it. He asked it in a kitchen in a district four miles from where he lived, at about twenty past four in the afternoon on Friday the fifteenth of March 2030, to a woman of twenty-nine sitting on a table with her boots under it, and it is worth putting down exactly, because everything he has done for six months has been about who is allowed to ask what.

"**Did anybody ever sit you down and explain what that piece of paper was going to be used for?**"

Tamsin Rudge did not have to think about it.

"Second week of January," she said. "A Tuesday. She telephoned me at about eleven in the morning, which I thought was polite, and her name was a name and she was pleasant, and she said, *we have issued the certificate and it is a record that you performed a complete working on your own person, and what it means for you in practical terms is that the work counts as work, so there is no gap in your record, and that is what it is for.*" She put her hands flat on the table. "**Ninety seconds. And I have thought about it every day since because of the gap.**"

"Which gap."

"That is the question, and nobody has ever asked me it, and I have asked it of two doctors and one of them understood me." She looked at the window. "**It does not count as a break in service. My record is unbroken. And that is the sentence, and I have never in my life been so glad to be told I was all right by anybody.** And then I have been a person with an unbroken record for eleven weeks, and the thing I have wanted since the second week of January is to be a person who had a gap. To have had a bad October that I am still recovering from and that nobody can pin on anybody, and a fortnight off, and a note, and a doctor who says *you will not go back to nights for a while* — and I have written all three of those in my own book at home as if they had happened, and I have known every single time I have written them that they had not, and I have not been able to stop."

Elias Venn sat in a kitchen in a district four miles from where he lived and did not say a single thing for about a minute.

"That is the worst one I have heard this year," he said, "and I have heard a man tell me he signed two pieces of paper in a room in December and cannot say which, and I have heard a woman tell me she counted eleven chairs and did not ask one of them."

"Is it bad?"

"It is the same bad," said Elias Venn. "**A piece of paper that is true, in a place where nobody thought anybody was going to read it.**"

She got up at about six and put her boots on and put the folder under her arm with the elastic band round it, and she did not thank him, and he did not tell her he was sorry, because he had thought about both of those in the last ninety seconds and had decided against them on the grounds that neither of them would survive being said in a kitchen.

He walked her out to the bus stop because it was on the way and because it was raining a very little. There was a shelter with a bench in it and a timetable with the last one gone off at eight, and a shop opposite that shut at half past five and had a man in it counting a till.

"I am not going on the bus," said Tamsin Rudge.

"You said you had a shift at ten tonight."

"I have not had a shift since the second week of February," she said. "**I have had a shift at ten tonight on four nights a week for two years and eleven months and I have not had one since February, and the difference between those two sentences is a piece of paper in this folder, and I have tried eleven different ways of saying that to five different people in nine weeks and you are the first one who has not looked at my feet.**" She got her coat out of the back of the shelter bench where she had left it, which meant she had been there before and had left it there on purpose. "I am going to walk. It is forty minutes and I have done it twice this week and I am not going to do it in front of a man who has come four districts to tell me there is no box."

The bus came at about ten to seven and she did not get on it, and she stood on the kerb and watched it go and did not wave, and then she turned and walked off up the road with the folder under her arm and the rain not falling hard enough to be worth mentioning, and Elias Venn got on the bus, and the fare was four credits, and he had a till with fifty credits in it and a carrier bag on a shelf at the back of a shop in Tanners Row and eleven credits in an envelope in a coat pocket that had gone to a company in a post box, and he sat on the bottom deck with his hat in his hands and watched a woman's back get smaller and did not write one word down for the whole forty minutes.
