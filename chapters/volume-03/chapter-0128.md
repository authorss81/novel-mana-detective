# Chapter 0128 — The Office That Does Not Take Appointments

The office of a Deputy Chief did not take appointments from a member of the public, and it did not take telephone calls from a member of the public either, and a man who telephoned it from a box on a corner at about ten past four on Wednesday the third of April was put through to a civilian assistant, who took his name and his telephone number and said that the office would consider a written request on its own merits and that a written request was the only thing it considered, and that there was no form.

There was no form. He had known there would not be. He had asked a clerk in a courthouse three weeks ago what the name for it was, and she had looked, and there had not been one.

He telephoned Corbie Street at about eleven and asked for Nadia Kest and was put through to her on the fourth ring.

"I cannot do this," she said, before he had got a word out. "**And I am not going to make you explain why you are asking, and I want that said first so that you do not spend the next ten minutes deciding whether to.**"

"You told me to ask somebody. I asked somebody. I got an address. I got a Friday and a van and a man who has been sitting in a corridor for seventy minutes since December. And then a man in an office in this city is the only person who can reopen it, and I am telephoning a police station instead of the office because the office will not speak to me."

"I know what the office will and will not do to you. I have been in that building for twenty-two years." A corridor changed on the other end of the line. "**You do not need me to get that number. You have probably had it since February. What you want from me is a sentence to say in a room, and I am not going to be it, and I am not going to be angry about saying no, and here is why: the last four times I have been a route for a person who had no route, three of them ended with somebody in a building being told that a police force was interested in them, and the fourth ended with a man being suspended in a different city in a different decade, and I have been the woman who made both of those happen, and I am not doing it again for a man who has been extremely careful.**"

"You do not have to do anything."

"I know I do not have to do anything, and that is the whole of what I have to say to you and I have said it in one sentence, and I am not going to say the word *asked* back to you and I am not going to be one of the five things you have been carrying, and you have not asked me about Sunday and I have not asked you about Sunday, and we are going to leave that exactly where it is." She stopped. "Write it out. Eleven lines is usually enough. **And leave the man's name off it the way you left it off the letter, and do not tell me why you are leaving it off, because I have worked it out in about four seconds and I do not want to be holding it.**"

"You have worked out—"

"Ring me if it goes badly," said Nadia Kest, "and do not ring me if it does not."

---

The request was eleven lines and it was in his own hand and it went to a registered address on the morning of Friday the fifth of April in an envelope with his own name and his own address on it, and the envelope had a man's name nowhere on it, and there was a sentence on page two about a night in December 2029 and a file in a store and a form with a signature on it and about four people in a corridor, and there was no name of a night porter in it anywhere, and on Monday the eighth of April at eleven o'clock in the morning he was shown into a room on the second floor of a building in Kettle Rows with a table in it and four chairs and a window that gave on to a wall about four feet away, which was the single most ordinary room in this city and which he had been in a version of in three other buildings since February.

There were four chairs and he counted them without deciding to and it was four and he wrote nothing down.

Deputy Chief Aveline Orr was fifty-three and had been in the force twenty-six years and she was not a large woman and she had a folder in front of her that was about four inches thick, and she read the first page of his eleven lines with a pencil in her hand and then put the pencil down and did not pick it up again, which he noticed, and which told him that she had not made a mark in eleven lines and that she was not going to.

"You have written eleven lines," she said, "and there is not one of them that I could refuse on the ground you would like me to use, and I want to tell you that before I say anything else, because you have come in here braced and I would rather you did not spend the next hour braced."

"I have come in here braced because the last six months have been refusals."

"Then this will be one," said Deputy Chief Orr. "**In about four minutes, and I am going to give you the reason before the four minutes, because the reason is the only part of this that is any use to you and the four minutes is not.**"

She put her hand flat on the folder.

"I have read the file," she said. "**On the fifteenth of February, four days after your man wrote his letter, because a Deputy Chief reads a file that has a withdrawal on it, and it is not a favour and it is not curiosity, it is the job, and I would have read it if the letter had come from a solicitor.**"

"Then you know what is in it."

"I know exactly what is in it, and I am going to read you the list, because you have not got the list and it is the whole of what I have, and it is four lines long, and you are going to be angry with it." She did not look at the folder. "A witness statement, dated the eleventh of December 2029, with a police signature at the bottom. A compiled extract from certified material, produced at about ten to ten at night on the eleventh. A form headed in words you have not got in front of you, which is a witness's agreement to a certified record before issue, with a signature in an agreement box and four printed act boxes beside it. And a file transfer form, signed for at about a quarter to seven on the twelfth."

"Four lines."

"**Four lines, and not one of them is a record of a night, and not one of them is a record of a person, and there is nothing in that file about what was done to anybody in that building on the ninth of December because what was done to anybody in that building on the ninth of December was not written down by anybody, at the time, by anybody, in any of four buildings.**"

Elias Venn sat in a chair in a room on the second floor of a building in Kettle Rows and understood that he had spent six months trying to get a document into this city that described a night, and that the woman across the table had the file, and that the file did not have it in it, and that this was not anybody's failure and was not a cover-up, and was the ordinary working of a machine that had four parts and a form for each part and a signature at the bottom of each part and a place for the event in none of them.

"You can reopen it," he said.

"I can reopen it. I have had the power to reopen it since the fifteenth of February."

"Then why have you not."

"**Because the last time this department reopened a matter on a member of the public's word was in 2031, it is 2030, and I am not going to do arithmetic with a man in a room.**" She stopped. "I am going to be serious and then I am going to be fair, and the fair part comes second and you are not going to enjoy it, and I have found in twenty-six years that if you give a person the serious part first they can hear the fair part, and if you give them the fair part first they stop listening."

"Go on."

"Reopening this matter means a Deputy Chief's signature goes on a piece of paper that says a decision taken on the ninth of January 2030 was wrong. **That decision was not mine. It was not one person's. It was four buildings and about eleven people and it was taken over three weeks and it was, in the way this department does things, ordinary, and defensible, and I have read it twice and I cannot find the place where it was done wrongly.** And reopening it puts my name on the front of a process that does not end with my name on the front of it. It ends with a hearing. And in that hearing the first question will not be *what happened on the ninth of December*. The first question will be *why was this closed*, and then the second will be *by whom*, and then somebody — not me, somebody in a room I do not sit in — will say *and what else did that office close in nine years.*"

"How many."

"**Four hundred and nine,**" said Deputy Chief Orr. "Not all of them are the same and about forty of them are not like the other three hundred and sixty. I have got that number in a drawer in this building and it is not a secret, and there is no piece of paper in this city that says what the nine years were, and you have spent six months finding that out about one night and one man and one building."

"The registrar said the same thing in March."

"The registrar said it in March and she has been saying it for nine years and she is right and she has been right every single time and nobody has ever written it down," said Aveline Orr. "**Do not use that sentence at me. I wrote it down in about 2011 and I put it in a briefing and eleven people read it and it did nothing, and I have been watching things not happen on the strength of a piece of paper for nineteen years and the piece of paper is the only thing I have ever had.**"

---

"**You have not asked me your question,**" she said. "You have been in here for nineteen minutes and you have asked me four questions, and none of them was the one you came with."

"What is the one I came with."

"You came with two records of issue in your own handwriting, a number off the back of a man's hand in a laundry, a letter you posted yourself with eleven coins in it, a page in a notebook with four pairs of hands on it, and a sentence you have been carrying since Friday." She said it without any triumph in it at all. "I know about four of those because they are in the file or they are in a building I have asked about. **And you have not asked me whether anybody can be found, and that is the question you came with, and you have not asked it because you do not have a ground.**"

"I have got a ground."

"**Then say it and I will tell you whether I have got a better one, and I have got one, and it has been got since the fifteenth of February.**"

"A person in a building has a right to know who was in the room with them."

Deputy Chief Orr put her pen down on the table, on its side, which was the first thing she had done with her hands that was not a document.

"That is a good ground," she said. "**That is the best ground anybody has put in front of me in about four years and I want you to hear the second half of it. It is a good ground and it belongs to the person in the building. Not to you.** You are a member of the public in this city. I have refused about nine people in this month and one of them was a man with a document in a good coat who was very close to the line, and you are not the worst thing that has come up these stairs, and the difference between you and the other nine is not the coat and it is not the care, and I want to say what it is exactly, because you have earned an exact answer and I am going to give you one."

"Tell me."

"If a person in that building goes to a magistrate and says *there was a man in a room with me on the ninth of December and I have been told nobody will say who*, then there is a proceeding, and a proceeding has an officer attached to it by a court, and that officer is entitled to a warrant, and a warrant is a piece of paper that makes me open a door that I cannot otherwise open. **The right to know who was in your room is a real right and it belongs to her and not to you, and if she never uses it then nobody in this city is entitled to open that door, and that is not a failure of the system, that is the system, and it is the only part of the system I have ever been able to defend.**"

"And she does not know."

"**She does not know that she is entitled to ask, and neither did you, and neither did the woman of thirty-four in a courthouse who looked for a form for it in March and there is not one, and I have had four officers in twenty-six years come to me and say the words *a person is entitled to know*, and every one of the four said *and therefore I should*, and every one of the four was wrong, and two of the four are still in the service and one of them is in this building.**"

Elias Venn did not say anything for about a minute.

"Can I ask you one thing that is not about the case."

"You can ask me one thing."

"You said you have known since the fifteenth of February that an officer kept private notes about that night."

Deputy Chief Orr did not move at all.

"**Yes,**" she said.

"And you have not asked to see them."

"**No. And I will not, and here is the reason, and it is the whole reason I have let you sit in this chair for twenty-five minutes.**" She put both hands flat on the table. "If I say what is in them, then I have read them. And a Deputy Chief who has read an officer's private notes is a Deputy Chief who has caused them. **And every note that officer writes after that is written for a Deputy Chief, and not for himself and not for a court, and there is a sergeant in a corridor in Corbie Street who has been sitting on four lines for sixteen weeks who would take them out of that drawer and burn them, and he would be right to, and I will not do it to him for a man I have met once.**"

"You know about the sergeant."

"I know that an officer in this force kept something of his own on a matter of mine, and I have known since the fifteenth of February, and I have never asked, and I have never needed to, and there is a reason for that which is that **I did not know there was anything to ask.**" She stopped. "**That is the one thing you have said to me this morning that I had not already thought about, and I am going to be honest with you about it, because you have been honest with me for twenty-five minutes and I have not been fair to you once. Nobody has ever come in here and refused to ask me the question, because everybody who comes in here wants something from me, and a man who has been refused by four counters in six months and who says *I am not going to ask you what is in that man's notebook* is either a saint or a man who has found out what asking costs, and it is not going to be the saint.**"

---

"Then there is a date," said Deputy Chief Orr, at about twenty to twelve, and stood up.

He stood up too, because she had.

"The ninth of April, two o'clock, the rooms on the ground floor at Corbie Street. It is not a hearing and it is not an inquiry and it is a review of a contract, and the contract ended on the thirty-first of March, and it has a hardback book on a table and a box on a notice, and both of them have been empty since the fourteenth of January."

"A review."

"A review. And I will be in the room, because I have read the file and a contract in my district has ended and a building has closed and there are people in this city who have come out of it, and I do not sit in on a review as a matter of routine and I have never in eleven years."

"You have read the list of people who are going to be there."

"I have read it on Friday. There are names on it that are not staff, and there are four names on it that are not the trust and not the company and not the police, and I have not put your name on it, and I am not going to tell you why."

"You know why."

"**I know why and I am not going to say it, and it is the only thing in this room I have that is worth the air in it.**" She came round the table and stood beside the chair. "A man who puts a name on that list is a man who has decided the last name in a book. And the last name in that book is not an appointment. It is a person. And a person has to ask."

Elias Venn picked up his eleven lines off her table and squared them, because you do not leave paper on a Deputy Chief's table unfolded, and because his hands wanted something to do that was not a question.

"A corridor, then."

"No."

"I will not be in the room. I have not come for that. I will be in the building."

"That is not available either." She said it without any particular weight. "**The list of who is on the list is pinned in that corridor, and you are a member of the public and there is no corridor in this city for you, and I am not going to be the last person to explain to you that there is no corridor.**"

"Right," said Elias Venn.

He went to the door and she was behind him and opened it, because it was her door, and he went out into a corridor with a stair at the end of it, and then stopped, because she said his name.

"Mr Venn." She was in the doorway with the folder under her arm. "**I have turned away four people in good coats in this room in twenty-six years and I have turned away about four hundred without one, and I would like you to be wrong about which of those two you are, and I am aware that I have just asked a man I have refused to do the work of my week.**"

"I have been wrong about most things since February," said Elias Venn. "It is a fairly steady business."

Then the lift came and went and he went down in it with eleven lines of his own handwriting in his hand and a folder under his arm with a lease in it that said that no key was required at any hour of the night, and he had gone into that room with two records of issue and a number out of a laundry and a promise he could not keep and a form that did not exist, and he had come out of it with a date and a sentence, and the sentence was better than anything he had said in six months, and the date was the ninth of April at two o'clock, and he was not going to be in the building, and there was not one single thing in this city he could do about any of it except turn up.
