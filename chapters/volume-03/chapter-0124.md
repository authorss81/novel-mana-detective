# Chapter 0124 — The Rota Face Up

Alaric Nunn was on a Thursday, which was the whole of the arrangement, and he had made it in a corridor in February and had told a man in a good coat about it before he had been asked for anything, and he was the only person in this building who had ever put a date on a condition of his own.

He was at a desk in a corridor with a coat over the back of the chair and a hardback notebook on the desk with a rubber band round it, and he was forty-seven and had been in the force twelve years and had a sister in Calder Street and a rota that went to the end of the month.

"Close the door."

"There is no door." There was a door, and Elias Venn shut it, and the corridor went on being a corridor outside it.

"**Right. Before you sit down. I am going to say a thing and I want it to be the first thing, and if it is not the first thing then I am going to stop.**"

"Go on."

"I am not giving you that." He put his hand flat on the notebook. "I have had a man in a good coat in this corridor in March and one in February and I have had about nine telephone calls since the fourteenth of March from people who have told me they are not police and would prefer it not to be known, and every one of them has wanted the book. I am not giving you the book. It is mine. I bought it in Calder Street in 2019 for a pound off a man who was having a clear-out and it is the only thing in this city that says what happened at ten to ten at night on the eleventh of December, and it says it in my hand, and **the day it is in a stranger's coat it is a document, and a document about a man who signed a box.**"

"I was not going to ask for it."

"You were going to ask for it inside a minute and I am saving us both the minute." He took his hand off it. "**And I will tell you why I have said no to nine people and I have never explained it to one of them, which is that I have been a policeman for twelve years and there is exactly one thing I have ever wanted and it is to be in a room with somebody who can be cross-examined, and if the book goes out of my hand then the four lines in it are a thing two men in coats have between them, and I am a man who kept a book, and a man who kept a book is a man who was not asking anybody anything.**"

The corridor went on being a corridor. Somebody went past at the other end with a folder under one arm and did not look in.

"Read me one line of it," said Elias Venn.

"No."

"One line. From about four feet away, and nobody touches it, and it is not copied and it is not photographed and it does not leave this building tonight, and if you read me one line then I know the shape of the thing I have been carrying and if I am wrong about the shape then I have wasted a morning and you have lost nothing that anybody could take off you."

Alaric Nunn thought about it for about eleven seconds, which was longer than Elias Venn expected, and then he turned the book round on the desk, put his thumb on the page so that a line and a half was showing, and read it out loud at the volume of a man reading a bus number.

"Nine forty. Extract produced by second officer from compiled sequence. Witness read it three times. Witness stated twice, that is not what I said. Witness then signed the agreement box."

He shut it and put the rubber band back on and did not let go of it.

"**Now you have got it and you have not got a copy, and I am going to say the thing that nobody has ever asked me for, and I have been waiting since the twelfth of December and it has been eleven weeks and about four days, and I have thought about it every night since, and I am saying it to you because you are a member of the public and there is nobody else, and I am aware of exactly how that sounds.**"

"Then say it."

"Say it out loud, and not to a woman at a counter." He pulled his hand off the notebook at last. "**On the evening of the twelfth of December I was told the matter was closed. It was said to me by telephone. The telephone was in a room. There were about four people in the room and I was not one of them and the room had a door, and I have said the word room out loud about nine times in eleven weeks in this corridor, on my own, to a radiator.** And that is it. That is the whole of the eleven weeks. A man in a good coat came in February and asked me about a bed and I told him about a telephone because I have not been able to do anything else with it, and I have not been asked about it once by anybody who could do anything, and I have not told my sister, and I am not going to tell my sister, and if you put that in a book I will tell you where you got it and I will be telling the truth when I do it."

"Who was in the room."

"No."

"You have just said—"

"**I am not going to say and you are not going to make me, because I have thought about it and if I say a name then that name is a man who is finished, and I have not got a case number in front of me, and I do not know what I would be starting.**" He said it without heat, and then, with a great deal less: "And I have been asked that exact question in this corridor by two men in six weeks and both of them asked it in the first ten minutes, and I have said no to both of them, and I would have said no to you this morning and I have said something else, and I would like you to notice that I have noticed."

Elias Venn sat on the end of a desk opposite a police sergeant of forty-seven and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have been worse than the silence, and because the man had been alone in a corridor for eleven weeks with a radiator and a rubber band and a sentence he had never said to anybody, and had said it to a stranger in a good coat because the alternative was to say it to nobody ever again, and because that was not a decision to trust him and it was a decision to stop.

---

A woman of about twenty-eight came along the corridor at about half past twelve and put a clipboard down on a desk and said, "There is a call for the front desk and they do not know who it is."

"Fine," said Alaric Nunn.

"From a company."

"**Then it is not for me.**"

"It is not for anybody, it is just a woman on a line saying a building's name."

"Then it is a company that cannot get to its own post," said the sergeant, "and it is not a police matter and I am not a postmaster, and if I take that call and I am wrong then the file on my desk has been interrupted by a stranger's post and there is a rule about that too, and I have never in twelve years been glad that I broke it and I have been glad twice that I did not."

She went. Alaric Nunn got up from his chair, went into the room at the end of the corridor that had a table in it and two chairs and a window with a blind that did not shut, and stood at the table.

"Elias Venn, come here."

He came.

"Four people were in that room," said Alaric Nunn. "You are going to ask me how I know, and the answer is that I was in the corridor outside it for about an hour and ten minutes waiting to be told to go in, and there was a bench, and I have been in this building for twelve years and I know that corridor better than I know my own kitchen, and I can tell you that a person who comes out of a room and goes past me at a quarter to seven and then goes back in within four minutes has been in a room that somebody has decided is closed. **That is all of it. I have got a bench and a four minutes and a door that I could not open, and there is no instrument in this city that will open that door for me, and there is no instrument in this city that will open it for you either, and I have stopped waiting for one and I have started doing the other thing.**"

"What other thing."

"Go back out to the desk," said the sergeant, and went out to the desk, and took a sheet of paper off the spike, and did not put it down yet.

"**Before that. The seventy minutes.** I have not said that out loud either, and I have thought about it more than I have thought about the room, because the room I cannot do anything about and the seventy minutes I can do something about, and what I can do about it is say it, and I would like somebody to have the order of it in a room." He put his finger on the desk, not on anything. "I was in the corridor at ten to seven. At about ten to seven somebody in a room I was not in said four words to somebody, and the four words went into a telephone, and the telephone was in that room. **At about a quarter to seven a file went into a store, and it went by hand, and it was signed for, and it was not on anybody's round.** And at about ten to eight they opened the door of the room I was waiting outside, and I went in, and there was a piece of paper on a table, and I signed it, and it took about four minutes, and I came out at about ten past eight and went home and my sister had my brother's tea."

"So the decision was made before the paper moved."

"**The decision was made before the paper moved, the paper moved four minutes after the decision, and then I waited seventy minutes for a door.**" He said it as a man laying out a row of figures he had gone over on his own for eleven weeks. "That is the whole of the order of it, and it is the only thing I have got, and here is why I have kept it. If a person tells you a thing was closed and then a file goes into a store, that is a file being put away. If a person tells you a thing was closed, and a file goes into a store four minutes later, and then a sergeant waits seventy minutes outside a door for a piece of paper he is going to sign, then **the file went into the store because somebody decided it should, the decision was not the sergeant's, and the sergeant was not told what the file was for until he was handed a thing to sign about it.** And in eleven weeks I have not found one box on one form in this building that says *here is the order of those two things*, and I have looked, and there is a form for eleven kinds of thing and one of the eleven is a reason a file is being kept and not one of the eleven is a reason it went."

"Have you written that down."

"No, and I have thought about it every night for eleven weeks, and there is a piece of paper on this desk with a rubber band on it, and if I write that down then the only word in it is mine, and there is about a two per cent chance in this building that my word survives contact with a supervisor."

He looked up.

"**That is not a rule and it is not a grievance. That is me being a man who has been outnumbered by paper for twelve years, and I am not going to insult you by pretending I am braver than I am at half past one on a Thursday.**"

---

"Now sit down," said Alaric Nunn. "I am going to do the only thing I have wanted since December, and you are going to let me, and neither of us is going to enjoy it, and that is the entire point."

He put both hands flat on the desk, palms down, the way a man does when he has decided to stop moving them.

"Question me properly. Stand there and ask me four things in a row, and let me answer them in order, and do not let go of a thread and do not be kind about it, because I have had four months of people being careful with me and it has been the worst four months of my working life. **I have stood in a room in this building with somebody who can be cross-examined once in twelve years, and it was on a Tuesday in October, and he was about to be disciplined, and he asked me four things in a row, and I have thought about it every week since.**"

Elias Venn did not move for a moment.

"Who decided the matter was closed."

"I do not know. I was told. It came down the line at ten to seven and there is a call sheet in the front office for the twelfth of December and I have asked for it twice, and I am a sergeant, and I have been refused twice, and both times the answer was that it was a supervisory line and I was not on it."

"Which supervisor."

"That is the second thing I do not know, and it is the thing I would trade the notebook for, and you are not getting the notebook."

"Who was in the room."

"About four people. I have said that four times today and I have said it to you twice and I am not going to say it a fifth time, and I have thought about saying it for eleven weeks, and it is the sentence I have said to a radiator the most."

"What was in the file."

"A compiled extract from Registry material, an index card, a witness statement with my signature at the bottom, and a form headed with something I have never seen on a piece of paper in my life, which is a form for a witness to agree with a certified record before issue, and I have never seen it before and I have been in this force for twelve years." He put his hands further apart. "That is four things, answered in order, and that is what I wanted, and I would like a moment."

Elias Venn stood against a filing cabinet in a corridor on Corbie Street on a Thursday afternoon and gave a police sergeant of forty-seven a moment, and it went on for about forty seconds, and neither of them said anything in it, and the building went on around them with a telephone ringing in a corridor and a door on a latch and a bin being emptied somewhere below.

"Right," said Alaric Nunn, eventually. "**Now you have done it and I am not going to thank you, because if I thank you then this was a favour, and it was not a favour, it was a Thursday, and I asked for it, and that is the whole of the difference and it has taken me eleven weeks to be able to say it out loud in a corridor to a man in a good coat.**"

He put the sheet of paper down on the desk face up. It was a rota, printed in a grid with a name against each day, and it was his own week.

"**That is my week,**" he said. "The days I am in are on it. The days I am not in are on it, and they are the days I am not in, and anybody who comes to this building and wants to ask me anything can look at that and come back on a day when I am here. I did that in January. **I have never told a living soul that I did that, and I am not going to, and I am telling you about it because you are going to find out about it in about eleven days from somebody else and I would rather you heard the sentence from me.**"

"Which somebody."

"**A woman you have not met**, who has been putting a pad in a book in a courthouse for four years, and who I have never seen." He squared the rota again. "She is going to write down where I am on a Tuesday and I am not going to stop her, and that is the whole of what a man in my position can do about a corridor he could not open, and it is a rota and it is a pad in somebody else's book, and it is eleven weeks late, and I would do it again on Monday."

Elias Venn did not touch the rota and did not write the days down, because the days were the whole of the gift and a man who copies a gift into a notebook has made it a document, and he had watched a man in a tie do that to himself in a chair in a store the day before.

"You are going to ask me about the room," said Alaric Nunn, sitting down, and putting his hand on the notebook again.

"No," said Elias Venn.

"Good," said the sergeant, and put his thumb on the page, and stopped there.
