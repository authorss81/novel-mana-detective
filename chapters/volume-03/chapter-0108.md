# Chapter 0108 — What He Wrote Down Twice

The front office at Corbie Street had a bell on it and a queue of nobody in it at ten o'clock on Monday morning, and Petra Lund was on the desk, and Elias Venn did not ask her for anything, and she said so before he had got to the counter.

"You are here on Monday, you are not asking, and you have got that face people get when they have been somewhere they should not have been on a weekend." She put her pen down. "I am not saying anything. Say what you have come for or go and have a coffee, and I have got a coffee machine that has been broken since November and I will not be sorry if you take about eleven minutes to decide."

"I have come to walk through a corridor," said Elias Venn.

"Then walk through it."

He walked through it, slowly, in the direction people walk, and it took him about six minutes, and he counted, because he counts things now, and there were nine doors off the main corridor and a stair at the end of it and a bench that four people could sit on and a fire extinguisher and a vending machine, and there were about thirty people in the building between nine and half past ten.

He was on his way out at a quarter past ten when a man of forty-seven came down the corridor with a hardback notebook under his arm and a coat over it, and the coat was the wrong coat for the weather — it was a funeral coat, dark, and it had been to a funeral, and he had not put it on for that reason today, and Elias Venn knew what that looked like because he had been the man in the good coat in every corridor in this city for five months and he could see it from the outside now for the first time in his life.

The man went past, and turned back about six paces, and came back.

"You counted the chairs in the hall on the Friday," he said.

"Yes."

"Then I am going to answer a question you have not asked me, and I am going to do it here, and in about four seconds somebody is going to come down that corridor and then I will not do it." He took the notebook out from under the coat and held it against his chest with both arms. "**I took the statement. I am a detective sergeant, I have been in this force twelve years, and on the eleventh of December last year I went to a building on Anselm Street at about eight in the evening and I took a statement from a man who works nights in a building where forty-one people sleep, and it is in a file, and I have not opened the file since the twenty-second of December.**"

"Who is the file with."

"Corrie Street, and it is not a matter anybody in this building will be able to help you with, and I am not going to ask you to go and ask, and if you go and ask you will be told the case is closed and you will have wasted a morning." He looked at the stair. "**What I am going to tell you is the reason I have kept this.** A message came on a telephone at twenty-five to seven on the evening of the twelfth, and it was one line, and it said that the case was going up and to hand it over at the end of the day, and it came from an inspector, and I have never been told which one, and I have looked. And the file went into the records store at about a quarter to seven the same evening, and there is a signature on the form that went with it, and I do not know whose signature it is and I have not got a way of finding out, and I have had eleven weeks. And the thing about a store is that a store is a building, and a man who has been in a building for nine years does not get to see a file, and I have not seen that file since the twenty-second of December and I have not been asked about it once, and I have been waiting for eleven weeks for somebody to ask me about it, and on Friday a man in a good coat stood at a counter and asked a question about a bed and I found out about this through a woman in a corridor on Monday, and I am not going to tell you her name because she told me not to and I am not going to be the reason she is in trouble."

"Who told you on Monday?"

"Somebody who was told what he was doing by somebody who read a letter out of a tray on Wednesday," said the man. "That is all I am going to say about that and I have already said more than I should have."

A door opened at the end of the corridor and somebody came out of it.

"Half past twelve," said the man of forty-seven. "There is a car park behind this building and I have got a court at eleven and then I have got a sandwich, and I am not saying any of it in a corridor. And I have a rota and my rest day is Saturday, and you are not going to do anything on a Saturday, and if you do it on a Saturday then you did it to me and not to the file."
---

They went out into the car park behind the station at half past twelve because he would not stand in a corridor and Elias Venn would not insist, and there were six cars in it and a woman reversing out of a space by a skip and neither of them went to the car, and it was the same Monday, and it was the only two hours of that week in which either of them said anything at all.

"Right," said the sergeant. "Alaric Nunn. I have been twelve years and I am forty-seven and I have a sister who sees me on Sundays and I am not going to be talked into anything by anybody. That is the whole of my preliminaries and I am going to say them once."

"Go ahead."

"I bought that book in a second-hand shop in Calder Street in 2019 for about a pound and I have written in it since," he said, "because I was told in my second year that a notebook is not a document, and I believed that, and then about nine years later I found out that a notebook is not a document and a book is a document, and I have never worked out what that makes this."

He did not open it.

"I am not going to show you the page," he said. "I am going to tell you what is on the page, in my own words, from about eighteen inches away, and then you are going to tell me whether that is worth anything, and if you say it is worth nothing I will put the book in my pocket and go home and that will be the end of it, and I have been carrying it about for eleven weeks and I am about eleven weeks past the end of what I can carry about."

"Tell me."

"The eleventh of December, about eight in the evening, Anselm Street, a first-floor office with nine chairs against the wall." He said it the way a man reads a form out loud. "I took a statement from a night porter. He is fifty-four. He gave me an account of the night of the ninth in about eleven minutes and it was a good account, and I want that on the record before I say anything else, because it is the part nobody at my station has ever asked me about. It was better than most statements I have taken in twelve years. He was frightened and he was not performing being frightened, and he told me the times, and he told me that he had gone along every row of that hall and had not seen anybody, and he told me that he did not know why a woman had been in a room with him."

"All right."

"At about twenty past eight I was telephoned. Not by him. By the case's second officer, and he had an extract of a sequence in front of him, and he came into the office and put it on the desk in front of me and told me the witness would need to see it." Alaric Nunn's voice did not change at all. "I did not have a reason on the planet to say no. It is a completely ordinary thing. It is the fourth or fifth time in twelve years it has happened to me and it has never once been a thing anybody has had to justify. And the witness read it, and he read it twice, and he said, *that is not what I said.*"

Elias Venn did not say anything.

"**That is not what I said.** Five words. And then the second officer said something about how the extract had been compiled from the whole of the material, and the witness read it a third time, and he said, *no, it is not*, and then he picked up the pen, and he signed it."

"He signed it."

"In the box. There is a box at the foot of a page and the box is headed *witness in agreement with extract*, and he signed in the box, and the second officer countersigned, and I initialled the top of the page, and then I read the whole page back to him, which is the thing you do, and he listened to all of it." Alaric Nunn shifted the notebook under his arm. "And then I wrote in this, at the time, in this book, and I wrote four lines, and I have not shown the four lines to anybody in eleven weeks, and I am going to tell you what they say."

"Go on."

"**Nine forty. Extract produced by 2nd officer from compiled sequence. Witness read it three times. Witness stated twice *that is not what I said*. Witness then signed the agreement box. Read back in full. Witness made no further comment.** Nine forty is the time on the extract and not the time I wrote it down, and I wrote it down at about ten to ten in a corridor on the same floor, because I did not trust myself to carry it to a car park and back."

He stopped, and there was a lorry reversing somewhere and it took a while.

"That is all four lines are," he said. "That is not an opinion. That is not a case. It is not even a complaint. It is a man writing down, at ten to ten at night, in a hardback notebook he bought for a pound, that a witness said something twice and then signed the thing he had just said twice was wrong. **And then nothing happened for eleven weeks, and I am the only person in this city who knows those two facts are in the same room, and you are the second.**"

---

"Why did you keep it."

"Because I wrote it at the time and I did not have anywhere to put it." He said it simply. "A contemporaneous note by the officer who was present is not evidence of anything until somebody wants it to be, and there is nowhere in this force to put a note that says a witness disagreed with an extract, and I looked, and I asked, and in December I was told by a supervisor that the matter was closed and the certificate was issued and the case was up, and I said yes, and I came out, and I have thought about that supervisor's face about four hundred times."

"What was the extract compiled from."

"I do not know and I have wanted to know for eleven weeks more than I want anything." He said the next part carefully. "I asked the second officer where the compiled sequence came from and he said it was certified material from the Registry, and I have never seen a piece of Registry material in my life that is given to a witness to agree with before the certificate is issued. I may be wrong. I have been wrong about procedure before. **I am not asking you to believe me. I am telling you what I know, which is a statement, and I have spent eleven weeks finding out what a statement is worth, and the answer is that it is worth exactly what the officer who took it is willing to be cross-examined on, and that is a figure I cannot get anybody to look at.**"

Elias Venn wrote four lines in his own book, and did not write the four lines that had been read to him, because they were not his, and the difference between the two four-line entries was the whole of what he had learned in five months and it had cost him a fortnight in a garden in a year he was not born in.

"The certificate," he said. "When was it issued, and by whom, and did you ever see it?"

"Ninth of January. Out of the Registry at Harrow Lane, from a registrar-counsel whose office I could not tell you, and no, I have never seen it, and I have never seen a certificate in my life, and that is not a complaint, that is what I am telling you."

"You said you are not going to be the last one to know."

"I said I was not going to be the last one to know," said Alaric Nunn. "Not that I was going to be braver than I am. And I have to go back in about ten minutes and I am going to take the notebook with me, and you are not going to ask me for it, and if you do ask me I am going to say no and it is going to be the last thing either of us does today."

"I am not going to ask you."

"Thank you." He took the coat off his arm and put it on. "One thing, and then I am going, and it is a condition and not a favour and I have thought about it since Friday and it is the only thing I have that I would actually miss."

"Go on."

"I have a rota that ends on Friday, and I am not going to be the man in a file on a Saturday because a man in a good coat could not wait until Monday." He put his hand on the car park door. "**You want to do something with this, you do it on a weekday, and you do not do it on a day when I am on a rest day, and if you do it on a day when I am on a rest day then you did it to me and not to the file.** Are you going to say my name?"

"Yes."

"Then say it on a weekday, and say it at the front, and say it to somebody who has to write it down, and do not say it on a Saturday." He held the door. "And I have a sister, and I would like to see her at the weekend, and I have not thought about anything else since Saturday, and that is a man of forty-seven telling you the truth and not asking for anything, and I would like it noticed that I have not asked for anything."

He got in the car, and then put his hand on the roof of it and did not move for about ten seconds, and Elias Venn did not walk away and did not help.

"Eleven weeks," said Alaric Nunn, to the windscreen. "I have been a detective sergeant for twelve years and I have never once had a case that came apart in my hands at the end, and this one came apart at the beginning and I walked out of it, and every night since the twelfth of December I have gone home at ten past six and I have cooked my dinner and I have gone to my sister's on Sunday and I have not told one single person in this city that any of it is happening. **You are the first. You are going to be the second, or the thirteenth, and I genuinely do not know which, and I have known you for about two hours.**"

"I will not tell anybody your name this week."

"Good," said Alaric Nunn, and went.

---

He walked back into the building at about twenty past two and asked Petra Lund one more question, which cost him the good-faith of a woman who had given him two for nothing.

"I am aware I have had my one," he said. "I am going to ask it anyway and you are going to say no, and I want to say first that I know what I am doing and that I am not going to come back on Monday pretending it did not happen."

Petra Lund put her pen down on the pad, face up, so that he could see she had not written anything.

"Ask."

"Can a records store produce a file to the officer who took it, on request, without a supervisor."

"No," she said. "It cannot, and that is not a policy, that is a wall, and the wall is about nine feet high and about four inches thick and it is made of about a hundred and forty years of people who did not want anybody coming into a room with boxes in it." She turned the pad right round. "A file goes into a store and a file comes out of a store on a destruction run or on a charge, and there is no third thing. Not a request, not a telephone call, not a warrant — a warrant gets you the file into a building you are in. **You can have a file brought to you, or you can go to the file, and there is no version of this in which you and a box are in a room together without about nine people in a corridor knowing the date and the time.**"

"So the file is in a building."

"The file is in a building on the north side with a name on it that I am not going to give you because you have had your one and I am not going to have a conversation about the second one." She squared the pad. "And I am going to tell you the thing I actually want to say, and I have thought about it since Friday and I have decided to say it, and it is this. **Whoever put that file in that store on the twelfth of December at a quarter to seven is not somebody who hid it. Somebody signed for it.** There is a signature on a form, and the form is in the store with the file, and the signature is on a piece of paper, and a piece of paper is the easiest thing in this city to get hold of if you are a person who is allowed to be in the building where the piece of paper is written, and that is a completely different thing from a person who hides things, and I want somebody to be careful about the difference, because I have watched about nine people in nine years conflate those two and it is always the same nine."

"Who is allowed to be in the building where the piece of paper is written."

"About four people," said Petra Lund, "and one of them is me, and I have not signed a form for a store since 2026 because they took the run off me, and that is the one fact I have got that has a date on it and it is the only reason I have said anything at all."
