# Chapter 0134 — Two Hundred Pieces of Paper

The box was on the counter at ten past ten on Thursday the eighteenth of April and had been on the counter since half past eight, and the word on the front of it was still **UNCLAIMED**, in the same marker, in the same capitals, with the same slightly crooked C, and it had been on the front of that box since 2026.

Elias Venn had come in at twenty to ten, and for the first half hour he had stood at the end of a counter watching a laundry work, and it had been the most ordinary forty minutes he had spent since February.

There were nine machines in a row and only four of them going, because it was a Thursday, and a woman of about thirty-eight came in with a coat in a bag and a slip with a number on it, and Roy Tarrant did not take the number off the slip in front of her, and did not say the number out loud, and wrote it on a tag and put the tag in the bag and tied the bag and said *Thursday*, and she said *Thursday*, and went. And a man of about sixty came in and asked for a pair of curtains and was told they would be Friday, and said *no, I need them for the funeral*, and was told that they would be Thursday at four instead, and said *right*, and went. And nobody in that shop said one word to Elias Venn for the best part of twenty minutes, and it was not rudeness.

Elias Venn stood at the end of it and worked out that this was a business in which nobody needed anything from anybody, and that he had been in eleven of them since February, and that he had got eleven refusals and one of them, on the seventh of April, had been better than the other ten.

The reason he had come at twenty to ten and not at half eight was in his own book in one line, and the line said: *I have no instrument. I have no ground. I have no document that is mine. I am going to a shop on a Thursday to stand at a counter, and there is no version of that in which anybody is owed me anything.*

He stood at the end of the counter and did not open his book.

The reason he had come was in the form.

It was a side of A4 with a heading at the top and a box for a number of items and a box for a destination and a signature at the foot, and there was a heading for the destination and it was a word long, and it was not *destination* exactly, it was *delivery address*, which is worse, because a delivery address is a place a thing is going to be, and a destination is a place a thing is going to be and both of them want the same thing and neither of them asks.

There was no box for a name.

Elias Venn had come in on the eleventh of April and looked at that form for about four minutes and worked out that a man who has decided to say where two hundred pieces of other people's paper are going cannot do it on a form with no name on it, and had gone away with the problem. On the eighteenth it was still the problem and it was on the counter.

Roy Tarrant came out of the back with a cardigan on and a pencil behind his ear.

"You have been in here twice in five months," he said, "and both times you have not asked me the photograph."

"I have not."

"**I know, and I am not going to make a speech about it, and I would like you to know that a man who has been in here twice and not asked me the photograph has cost me about four pounds in trade and I have not worked that out yet and it may be a good bargain.**" He put the pencil behind the other ear. "Half eight is when they come. Half nine when they come. It is a Thursday and it is a Thursday about once a month and I have known it since January."

"Who are they."

"A company. It is not a removal firm and it is not a laundry and it is not a man in a van of his own, and I am not going to tell you what it is because I do not know what it is, and I have had a telephone conversation with it in January and a telephone conversation is not a thing you can put a name on." He put his hand flat on the counter beside the box, not on it. "**They rang me. On the twelfth. Which is the day after I rang them.**"

"You rang them in March."

"I rang a number in March. I have not rung a number since March. In April there was a box on the counter and a form and a date, and the date was the eighteenth, and the date was in an envelope with a form in it, and the envelope came through the post, and there was no telephone call at all, and the twelfth of April there was a call to say *we shall be there at half eight or half nine, will the box be ready.*" He shrugged. "**So they knew on the twelfth what I was going to do on the eighteenth without me having to do anything, and I have decided I am not going to think about how, because the last time I thought about how, in about 2027, I worked out that a shop is a place a person can be found, and I have been not being found in this shop for four years, and I am not giving that up for a telephone call.**"

There was a bell on the door.

He was thirty-one and called Sean Petrie and had a padded jacket and a clipboard and a docket book, and he had been doing the round for four years, and he came in and said good morning to the room.

"Morning," said Roy Tarrant. "It's on the counter."

Sean Petrie put the clipboard on the counter and read the form and read the box and read the form again.

"Right. How many?"

"Two hundred."

"**Two hundred what?**"

"Assorted," said Roy Tarrant.

"Then it's assorted." He wrote it. "And it's going where."

Roy Tarrant looked at the box.

He did not look at Elias Venn and he did not look at the window. He looked at the form, at the box under the heading that said *delivery address*, and he stood in his own shop at half past ten on a Thursday morning with a pencil in his hand and the answer in his mouth.

"There's a unit," he said. "Nine Calder Way. That's the address."

"Any contact name?"

"There's a name on the box."

"There's a name on the *box*," said Sean Petrie, "and I have got a box and not a name. I'll put the address and I'll put the box number if you've got one."

"There's no box number."

"Right." He wrote it. "Signature at the bottom is yours?"

"It is."

Roy Tarrant signed it. It took him a moment because the counter is not level and the form is on a board and the board is on the counter, and he had to do it twice, and the second one was not better.

"That's it, then. Two hundred assorted, nine Calder Way." He tore the top copy off and gave it over. "I'll take them down in about ten minutes, the van's round the back."

"Right," said Roy Tarrant.

Sean Petrie went out.

And in the silence after the door, the man of sixty took the pencil out from behind his ear and looked at the top copy that was on the counter, and said, to nobody, in a shop:

"**That is the first time in four years I have said where that box was going out loud to a person who was going to drive away with it.**"

"You did not have to."

"I know I did not have to. **And I have got about a hundred strips in there that belong to a doctor, and I have never opened it, and I have never been through it, and I am not going to, and if anybody ever asks me what is in it then they have four hours and a bin and a girl of sixteen on a Saturday and I am not going to help them.**" He put the pencil behind his ear again. "I said it because I have had a piece of paper with two boxes on it for seven days and a telephone call on the twelfth, and if the van says two hundred assorted and the van knows what is in it, then in about two years there is a piece of paper in a building somewhere that says a laundry in this city sent two hundred pieces of other people's paper to a unit in Calder Way, and that is a thing that can be said. **And I would rather it could be said that a woman of sixty in a laundry said out loud, in front of two men, that about a hundred of them were a doctor's, and did not open it, and would not.**"

Elias Venn had not asked a single question about the contents of the box in five months and had refused, in three separate rooms, to have a photograph of anything to do with it, and he stood at the counter and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that was not smaller than what the man had just said.

"A doctor," he said.

"A doctor's number. Not a doctor. **A doctor's registration number, on about a hundred strips, out of a coat, in this shop, off a customer, and the customer is dead or has moved or has stopped coming, and I have no way of finding out which and I am not going to find out.**" Roy Tarrant took the top copy and folded it in four and put it in the till. "**I have been in the trade since 1974 and I worked in a hospital laundry for nine years of it and I can tell you the thing nobody has told you: there is nobody. There is no one to send them back to. The person has gone and the number is a number and the strip is a piece of paper with a number on it and the two of them have got nothing to do with each other any more than my phone number has anything to do with my phone.**"

"You decided this in January."

"I decided this in January." He put his hand on the counter beside the box, not on it. "**And here is the thing I decided in January and have not said to anybody, so you are going to get it for nothing, and I would like you to understand that I have thought about saying it to you for four minutes and the reason I am saying it is that you have come in here twice and not asked me the photograph, and that is the only credential either of us has this morning.**

"I did not decide a company because a company was better. I decided a company because a bin is a bin and a bin is where you put a thing when you have stopped being able to stand having it. And I could have put a bin out on the twenty-sixth of January at about six in the morning and it would have been gone by ten and nobody in this city would ever have known, and I would have been a man who threw away other people's paper." He took his hand off the counter. "**And the reason I could not do it is that nobody told me that I could.** Not the trust, not the company, not the man on the telephone in January who was perfectly pleasant and did not say one word about a bin. There is no form in this city that says *you may dispose of this*, and there is no form that says *you must not*, and so I have spent four months holding a box of paper because a woman of sixty in a laundry could not find out whether she was allowed to put it out.

"**And that is the whole of what has happened to me and I am aware that it is not the end of the world, and I have said it to a stranger in my shop at ten to eleven in the morning on a Thursday and I would like to go back to the machines now.**"

He went back to the machines.

They came for it at about twenty to eleven, two men, and it took nine minutes, and the box did not open, and nobody touched it but them, and Elias Venn watched it go onto a tail-lift and did not put his hands anywhere near it and did not take a photograph with a telephone he did not have.

A woman of about seventy-six came in at two minutes to eleven with a parcel and a slip and waited by the machines while Roy Tarrant did it, and she said that his wife had told her on Tuesday that the shop was closing, and Roy Tarrant said that the shop was not closing, and she said that is what she was told and would not say it in a shop twice, and Roy Tarrant said **she is not wrong, we go to a unit in Ferrier Street in May, and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and I have known since January and I have not told her until Tuesday because I could not think of a way of saying it that did not put her in a car.** Then he gave her the parcel and she said *that'll be lovely* and went out, and nobody in the shop said anything about it.

At the door, the woman of fifty-eight came through with a market bag and put it down on the counter and looked at the space where the box had been.

She looked at it for a while.

"Was it the doctor one," she said.

"It was the doctor one," said Roy Tarrant.

"Then that's the end of that," said his wife, and went through to the back.

He waited until the door at the back had shut before he said the next thing, and he said it to the till.

"I am going to keep the top copy."

"The top copy is in the till."

"The top copy is in the till," said Roy Tarrant, "and it has got two hundred on it and an address on it and a date on it and a signature on it that is mine, and that is the only piece of paper in this city that says where that box went. **And in about a year somebody is going to come into this shop and ask what was in it, and I am going to want a piece of paper that says what I said about it, and I am not going to get it from them, because they will write down what I say and a thing they have written down belongs to them.**"

"That is a copy of nothing. It is your own paper."

"It is the only piece of paper in the world about that box and it is in a till in a shop, and a till is not a file and a shop is not a building anybody looks in." He pushed the drawer in. "**I have been putting things in that drawer since 2026 and there are about eleven years of job books in the shop and there is not one page in any of them with a customer's name on it, and that has cost me the ability to settle an argument with a customer about eleven times in four years, and I have never once regretted it, and I am not starting on a Thursday.**"

Elias Venn counted what was in his hand, which was four credits, and put them on the counter.

"I did not take a taxi," he said.

"I did not offer you a taxi."

"You said you would tell me anything I had not thought of."

"**I have said that I would say it and I have not said that I would buy you one.**" He pushed two of them back across the counter. "Take those and go and spend them on something that is not information. I have been in this shop since half seven and a man in a good coat has been in it since ten and neither of us has mentioned the box once, and that is two of us and one box and about nine hours, and you may write that down if you want and I have never heard of anybody who was harmed by it."

At two o'clock the shop was empty and the counter was clear and the space under it was a dark rectangle in the dust with a lighter rectangle inside it where the box had been, and Roy Tarrant stood in the middle of his own shop with nothing whatsoever to do until the following Wednesday, for the first time since 2026, and did not like it, and had nobody to say so to, and would not have said it if he had.

Elias Venn had been in a building in this city for four hours and twenty minutes and had taken a piece of paper out of it, and the piece of paper was a man's own form, and the man had told him where it was going, and neither of those two things was going to help anybody at all, and both of them had cost the man something, which was that a stranger now knew that Roy Tarrant's shop was going to a unit in Ferrier Street in May and that his wife had been told on a Tuesday and had not been told by him.
