# Chapter 0116 — Four Hundred and Six Pages

The Proof Registry at 14 Harrow Lane has a fan that comes on at about ten past nine and a counter on the ground floor and a queue system made of a rope and a set of expectations, and Elias Venn had been in that room a handful of times since the autumn and had been served once, and it had taken four hundred and six pages to serve him, and he had written in his own book that he would not come back.

He came back on Monday the eighteenth of March at five to ten in the morning, which was twenty minutes before the doors were properly open, and he was the second person in the building.

Bettina Haile was at the public counter and she had a cardigan on and a tray system behind her and a cup of something she had brought in from somewhere and had not drunk, and she looked at him for about four seconds and then put the cup down.

"No," she said.

"I have not asked."

"You have a face on you that has a request on it and you have had that face on since the sixth of December, and I have said no to about nine people with that face on it since then and I am not going to be the tenth," said Bettina Haile. "**You have had a production out of this building and a production is a hearing, and a hearing happens once, and if I give you a second one then the first one was not a hearing and the four hundred and six pages were a favour and somebody upstairs will read that on a Monday and be quite pleased with me.** So. No."

"That is correct and I want it on the page," said Elias Venn. "I have come to ask for a second hearing and I have been refused a second hearing and I am not going to argue with you about it, and I am going to tell you now that I have got nothing in my hand except a piece of paper with a number on it, and the number is not mine and I did not get it from an institution, and I am going to tell you where I got it, because you are going to find out what I am if I do not."

She put both hands on the counter.

"Go on."

"I got it off the back of a man's hand in a laundry," said Elias Venn, "on Thursday, at about half past three in the afternoon, with a man of sixty standing on the other side of the counter, and it came off a docket in his wife's book and it went out of his own telephone to a clinic on Saint Orra Reach and a woman read it back to him and would not say whose it was, and I have written it on the back of my own hand with a biro in four seconds in front of all three of us, and I have not written it in this book yet and I am not going to, and I have told you the whole of it before you have asked me one question, and that is because you have refused me once and I would like to be a man who is not refused twice for asking a thing the right way."

Bettina Haile looked at him for a long moment and then she said, "**I do not ask where a number came from.**"

"That is the sentence I have been waiting six months to hear and I have heard it in two other mouths this week."

"Then you have had a good week." She turned and pulled a tray out of a stack and set it down. "I have got about four hundred and forty in that stack and every one of them is a record of issue, and a record of issue is served on an account number, and I do not care who told you the number or how. What I do care about is whether it is the right number, and that takes me four seconds, and I am going to do it in front of you because you have told me where you got it and I would rather you saw me check it than watched me not check it."

She went through the tray. It took her about four seconds.

"That is right," she said. "It is one account. It is a housing charity, and it has one account, and it has had one account since about 2011, and there is a line on it that says *one account — all services*, and I have read that line about nine thousand times and I have never once been asked what *services* means on an account where everybody who uses that account sleeps in a different street." She pulled one sheet out and put it on the counter facing him. "There. A record of issue. It is yours to keep, it is a public document, and it is not a copy of anything you have to be entitled to."

Elias Venn looked at it for a long time.

It was one side of A4 and it had a box at the top with a number in it, and under the box there were eleven lines, and the eleventh line was the time.

> **Record of issue.** 11.00, district office, 14 Harrow Lane.
> Account. Premises of service. Date of service. Issuing office. Office reference. Sequence. Fee. Date of issue. Time of issue. Schedules relied on. **Issuing officer.**

"The certificate I am holding," he said, "carries a date and not a time."

"It carries a date and not a time, and I have told you why in a corridor in March of last year and you did not write it down then and you are not going to make me say it twice." She picked her cup up and found it cold and put it down again. "A sequence is an order, and an order has no clock in it. And a record of issue is a fact about an office, and an office has a clock. **Everything on this sheet is a fact about this building, and the certificate is a fact about a night, and the two facts are in two different rooms and only one of them has ever been wrong.**"

"Which one."

"Neither," said Bettina Haile. "That is the sentence I have been trying to say to a police officer in three volumes and I have never said it to a member of the public before, and I am not going to say it properly to a man on a Monday morning, so you may have the half of it and be annoyed. **The Registry does not hold sequences. It certifies them. The sequence is held by the court that is likely to be asked, and by nobody else, and if the court does not ask then it is held by nobody at all and it was never anywhere.**"

She had put the sheet down on the counter in front of him and had not let go of it, and he noticed that, and did not reach for it while her hand was on it.

"There is a second one," he said.

"There is always a second one."

"I am going to say the reference out loud and I am not going to write it down and it is not in a book of mine and it has not been in a book of mine since September, and I am going to say it because I would like to see whether the same counter will serve it in the same morning as the other one."

He said it.

Bettina Haile did not change colour and did not put her cup down and did not look at the queue.

"**That is a different premises and a different account and it is the same counter,**" she said, "and I am going to serve it, and I want you to understand that I am not doing you a favour and I am not being brave. A record of issue is the receipt for this office's own act. It is the only piece of paper in this city about a certificate that is not the certificate, and it carries no patient and it carries no procedure, and there is no category of person in this building that I am permitted to refuse it to. If you had come in here with a warrant I could not have refused you either, and I have never in nine years wanted to."

She went through the tray again. It took longer.

"Two October 2029," she said. "Nought nine forty, because a certificate is dated, and this building likes a quarter to." She put the second sheet down on the counter and left it there. "Claimant of record, a registered operator, and there is a premises, and the premises is two to eighteen Wexford Row. Date of service, the eleventh of September 2029. Patient schedule, and it is a ward and a bed and a date, and I am not going to read the bed out. Procedure schedule, a code, two hours. Fee settled, sixty credits. Schedules relied on: no first, no second, no third, and the fourth not compiled."

"And the certificate."

"I cannot produce the certificate," said Bettina Haile. "**And I am entirely correct about that, and so are you, and I would like us both to be careful, because the whole of what has happened in this building for two years is that one side has been told the other side is hiding something.** The certificate is a document about a sequence. This building does not hold sequences, it certifies them. The sequence is in a court. The certificate went out to the payer's drawer, and the payer's drawer is the only copy there is, and if you go to the payer's building and ask for it then you are asking a person who is not me, and I will not write down the name of that building for you, and I would have written it down in about November if you had been the officer you used to be."

Elias Venn did not touch either sheet. He had his hands flat on the counter on either side of them, the way he had put his hands flat on a laundry counter two days before.

"Who signed it," he said. "Not the office. The person."

"That is the one thing in this room that I am not going to give you, and I am going to tell you exactly what I am doing, because you have earned it and because you will go and ask somebody else if I do not."

She turned the second sheet round so the box was facing up and put her finger beside the last line without touching the paper.

"A registrar's name is not withheld from the public," said Bettina Haile. "**It is withheld from a member of the public who cannot show what the name is for.** And I will give you the office, and the office reference, and the sequence, and the date, and the time, and the fee, and the number of the record of issue, and I have given you all seven, and you have them in your own hand now, and there is one box on that sheet with a post in it and no name in it, and I am not going to fill it in, and the reason is not secrecy and I want that on the record of your face. **The reason is that to show what a name is for, a person has to show a proceeding, and there is no proceeding, and if I put a name in that box for a man with no proceeding then I have not protected a name. I have made a name into a thing that was handed out for a reason that did not exist, and the day somebody comes with a real reason I will have no way left to refuse him, because I will have already written it down once for no reason at all.**"

She said that in about nine seconds and then she stopped and looked at him, and he understood that she had said the whole of the most important sentence of her working life to a member of the public on a Monday morning and that she was not going to say anything else about it, and he understood that she was not going to be thanked for it, and he was right about both.

"May I have the second one."

"**No.**"

---

"You are going to ask me one more thing," she said, "and I have decided that I will answer it before you ask it, because if I answer it after you have asked it then it is a favour, and I have told you what I will not do with favours on a Monday morning."

"I am not going to ask you whether a certificate can be wrong."

"Then what."

"I was going to ask you what a form of words on this paper is for," said Elias Venn, "and I have decided that I am not going to ask that either, and I have asked you one thing this morning, and the thing I asked you you have not answered, and you have spent eleven minutes not answering it in about nine different ways, and I have been sitting here letting you, and I want to say that I noticed."

Bettina Haile laughed. It was the first time in six months he had heard her make a noise that was not arranged in advance.

"You are the only person who has ever come into this room and noticed the shape of what I was not saying," she said. "**I have been asked twice in nine years whether a certificate can be wrong, and both times the person asking me wanted me to say it could not.** And I have never been asked it by somebody who wanted either answer, and I have never been asked it in a way that was not already a decision somebody had made in a corridor an hour earlier, and I am not going to be asked it by a member of the public, because you cannot be the one who asked it, because the moment you ask it, it is a question about a night, and I do not answer questions about a night. I answer questions about a room. That is the whole of what I am."

She went away down the aisle and came back with a cardboard box and put it on the counter between them, and it was about four inches deep and the width of a ream.

"Four hundred and six pages," she said.

"You are giving me four hundred and six pages."

"I am producing four hundred and six pages," said Bettina Haile, "which is a different verb, and the difference is the whole of my working life, and I have never had a lawyer in this building to explain it to me and I have never needed one." She squared the box to the edge of the counter. "Those are records of issue for the same premises over a period of about nine weeks. Every one of them is a receipt for this office's own act. Every one of them has the same eleven lines. And every single one of them has a box at the bottom with a post in it and no name in it, and I am producing four hundred and six of them to a member of the public who has asked me for one, because you asked me for one and I cannot give you one, and the nearest thing to a yes that this building is capable of is four hundred and six noes in a box."

He read the heading on the top sheet. It was a date range and a premises and an account and it was not his premises.

"You have put four hundred and six pages on a counter in front of me," he said, "instead of one name."

"Yes."

"And you knew that I would look."

"I knew you would look at the heading and I knew you would work out that they were not your premises and I knew you would work out why," said Bettina Haile, "and I have decided that I would rather be audited four hundred and six times than be right once for a man in a good coat, and you are not in a good coat, and I have noticed that twice this morning."

---

He was outside on the bench on the wall at about three o'clock with the sun on the front of him and two sheets of paper in his own hand.

He had laid them out on his knee and put them one above the other, because she had let him do that, and because he wanted to see them next to each other and because he had realised halfway through the morning that this was the first time in six months that two pieces of paper about this case had been in the same place at the same time, and that neither of them had been in a file, and that both of them were in a member of the public's own handwriting because he had written them out himself in a coffee-break room at the back with a biro and had asked for them not to be photocopied and had been refused the copying and had been given the two originals.

The top one was a ward in another building and it said eleven weeks of nothing happening.

The bottom one was 2 Anselm Street, and the ninth of December 2029, and it had a date on it and a time on it, and the time was the time a page had been made, and the date was the date the page was about, and a man of fifty-four had been off the desk for thirty minutes that night and had written the word *Door* in a column of a book and had signed a certificate a month later.

**Two pieces of paper, a bench, and a wall, and the whole of six months between them.**

He had also, in the last eleven minutes of the morning, been given a fifth thing, and it was the only one of the five that was not a piece of paper, and it had been said by a woman who had not meant to say it out loud, and it was this.

"You have been counting the offices that hold a half," she had said, while she was putting the box away, with her back half turned and as though she were talking to the tray. "**There are five. Four of them are offices and the fifth is a course, and the course is the only one of the five that everything goes through.** A claim is made out of an account. An account is settled to a claimant. A claimant has a premises. And the premises has a person in it who did something to themselves, and that person has to be a person who can be asked, and the only anybody in this city who has ever asked a person to sign a thing and then told them what it was going to be used for is a woman of fifty-five in a building across the city who runs a course four times a year and has a card in front of a public register that says enquiries are not accepted."

Four offices. And a course. And a hardback register on a table in Concord Hall with a funder column that is the narrowest column in the book, and five bodies in it that he had written out from his own head on a servery ticket with somebody else's fork.

The Rowan Trust, which had a building in Kettle Rows. Harlowmere, which had a building in Wexford Row. A health board, which had somewhere he had not been. A civic committee with a minute number and no name, which had Corbie Street in it somewhere. And the fourth office, which was this one, and which ran the course as well as certifying it.

**Five bodies. Five buildings. One two-day course that all of them paid for, four times a year, twenty-two places at a time, a box on a form, filled in four hundred times, and not one person in this city who has ever been asked what is at the end of it.**

And there is no word in this city for that, either, and he had been inside it now for six months and had never once heard one, and the reason was that there was nothing there to name. A claim is a claim, and an account is an account, and a certificate is a certificate, and a course is a course, and if a man wanted to say that five buildings had all sent somebody to the same two days then he was a man with a theory and not a man with a document, and a man with a theory in this city is a man who gets told by somebody in a good coat that there is no form for what he is holding.

He did not write it down. He had told a woman of fifty-five on Wednesday that he would not ask her for a list, and he had told a woman of forty-four in a canteen that he had sworn on a step not to go back and check one of them, and he was a man who kept things, and this was the first thing in six months that he wanted to write down and did not, because writing it down would be the beginning of a list, and he had spent ten years learning that a list of buildings is how a case stops being about a person.

He put the two sheets in his inside pocket, one on top of the other, and got up off the bench, and the sun had gone round the building by then and the fan in the second-floor window was still going, and the door of the building opened and a woman of about thirty came out onto the fire escape with her coat on, and shut the door behind her, and went, and the two of them were about nine feet apart in the air and neither of them knew the other was there.
