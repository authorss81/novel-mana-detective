# Chapter 0121 — Four Streets Off a Parade

He had until Monday to find out which of the two he was, and he had until about ten past ten to find out where it was, and the second of those had taken four days longer than he expected and the first of them he found out on a pavement at twenty to ten in the morning with a folded sheet of paper in his hand.

The street was Pellow Street and the number was eighteen and it was a terrace of nine houses with the doors alternating and a railings gap every two doors for the yard, and the fourth one along had a step up to it with two bricks missing out of the top tread and a bell-pull on the left-hand side of the frame that had not been painted in the colour of the frame. The yards were about four feet deep and there was a washing line in the third one and a child's bicycle against a wall in the sixth one, upside down, with a flat tyre.

He read the sheet once more and put it back in the inside pocket of his coat, next to a plastic wallet and next to a piece of A4 with a name and a number on it, and he was aware, for the fifth time in five months, that the inside pocket of that coat had become a place where things went that he could not account for.

There was no bell that worked. He knocked at about a quarter past ten and there was nothing, and he counted that as a knock and not as two, and he stood on the pavement for about four minutes.

At twenty past ten he was about to go and look at the house from the end of the street, and the door opened about four inches, and there was a woman behind it.

She was about sixty-three. Grey hair, an apron over a cardigan with the sleeves pushed up to the elbow, and a radio going in a front room behind her, low, and she did not ask him who he was and she did not ask him what he wanted, and she looked at him for about three seconds and then at his coat, and then at his face again.

"I am not selling anything," said Elias Venn. "I have got a piece of paper in my pocket with your address on it, and I got it on Friday from a man who works behind a counter in a parade, and I have not come to your door to take anything, and I have come to it because I have been trying to find out what happens to a person when a case is closed against them since about November and I have got about nine answers and every one of them was a person telling me that it was not their job."

He had had four sentences ready and it had taken him nine days to write them and he got through two.

The woman did not move the door.

"What did they tell you not to write down," she said.

The radio went on behind her. A lorry went past at the end of the street, out of sight, and the sound of it arrived and then did not.

"Was I all right," said Elias Venn. "For a moment. Say that again."

"What did they tell you not to write down."

"I heard you."

"You did not answer it," said the woman. "**That is the only thing I ask anybody who comes to that door. I have never asked them anything else and I am not going to start, and it is not a test and it is not a form and I do not want to know whatever it is.** I have had four people sent here by the man at the counter in the parade and about a dozen I have never seen the front of, and every one of them has been able to tell me in about nine words what they were told to leave out, and about nine of them have told me in a kitchen where their family were and one of them has told me on a doorstep with a child on her hip, and I have never once known what any of it was and I have never once wanted to."

Elias Venn stood on a step in Pellow Street and could not answer a question, and the reason was in nine words in a different pocket of the same coat, made on a Thursday in February to a man who worked nights in a building four streets from here, and he was not going to say what the reason was on the twenty-fifth of March to a woman he had known for four minutes, and he was not going to say it because he had said it, and because saying a thing twice is how a thing stops being a promise and starts being a habit.

"I am not going to answer that," he said.

"That is all right," said the woman. "You would not have got a very good answer out of the four, and I have not had one from anybody in four years."

She opened the door the rest of the way and stood to one side of it and did not say come in, and he went in because not saying come in is not saying go out, and it is a thing about houses rather than about people.

The front room was a front room and the radio was on a sideboard and there was a hardback book on a table with a cloth over half of it. A hall with a stair going up that had a runner on it, and a kitchen at the back that was warm, and the whole of the house smelled of a house that has been lived in by one person for a long time.

"Sit down if you want to," she said. "I am not going to. I do not sit with people at that table and I have never and it is not about you."

He stood.

"Before you say anything else," she said, "you are going to want to put your hand on that book, and you are not going to, and I want to know why, because in four years nobody has told me and I have worked it out twice and got it wrong twice."

"I cannot read a person," said Elias Venn. "I am not able to and I am not going to be able to and I will not be able to, and there is no version of this where I put my hand on a hardback book in a house in Pellow Street and something comes back that is yours. So I am not going to. And I am telling you that in your own house, at your own table, because I would rather you had it from me in four minutes than work it out again."

She looked at him for longer that time.

"Nobody has ever said that to me at that table," she said. "Four years. Not one person. **The ones who come here put a hand on it straight away, every one of them, and they all get a strange look and go quiet, and I have always thought it was religion.**"

"Have people put their hands on it?"

"Two of them. I did not stop them. I have thought about stopping them since." She moved the cloth a little and then put it back. "**The book is mine. I do not read it to anybody, I do not read it to myself, and I do not take it out of this room.**"

"Then I will not ask about it."

"You will ask about it inside a week," said the woman. "Everybody does. And when you do I am going to say no, and you are going to be all right about it, because you have just told me you cannot read a person and I have believed you, and I have believed people before."

She said that last part lightly and not lightly.

---

From where he was standing he could see about two inches of the page at the near edge of the book past the cloth, and there was writing on it, and the writing went a long way down, and he looked at it for about a second and a half and then looked at the radio instead and did not look at it again. There was a smell of a house where somebody has washed a curtain and not put the washing out.

"Go on then," she said. "You have not come to the door to be looked at."

"I have not got anything," said Elias Venn. "That is the honest position and I have had it since about November. In six months I have been refused by four counters and the refusal was correct in four cases out of four, and two of the four are in this coat as sheets of paper in my own handwriting, and the other two are a number and a name I am not allowed to have."

"And you want me for the fifth."

"I came to see whether the door was real."

The woman put her hand flat on the table, not on the book, beside it, and stood there for a moment.

"It is real," she said. "**It is a rented house and I have been here eleven years and the rent comes out of a pension and there is no trust and no grant and no contract and no form anywhere in this city that covers a person who comes to a door like this one, and I have looked, and I did not have to look very hard, because you do not have to look very hard when the answer is that there is not one.** I am not an institution. Nobody pays me. Nobody sends anybody. That is the whole of what this is and there is not a word for it in this city and I have stopped wanting one."

"Then why do you do it."

"Because somebody has to and because I can," said the woman, "and that is a stupid answer and I have had it for four years and it has not worn out."

There was a bed under the stairs in the hall, made, with a blanket folded over the foot of it, and the runner went up the treads worn pale in a stripe down the middle, and there was nobody else in the house, and there had been nobody else in the house for long enough that the milk on the step had been an arrangement rather than an accident.

"**You are going to ask me why I do not write the other one down.**"

"I was going to."

"Everybody is. And I am going to tell you that I do not do that, and that is the whole of it, and if you want a reason then you will have to be me for about four years and I would not advise it, because I have got no more answers at the end of the four years than I had at the beginning and I have got a great many more people who know where this house is."

She sat down on the arm of the chair with the cushion and then got up again as though she had been caught.

"Now you have got to the part where you tell me what you found out at the back door of a parade, and I have never been to that building and I have never wanted to, and there is nothing in that office and nobody in that office has ever asked me a question about me, and if you are going to make me say that to somebody, I would rather you told me now and did not make me do it downstairs."

"Ray Tindall did not send me," said Elias Venn. "He sent four people and he has never been inside this house and he has told me where it is out loud in a room, at a counter, on a Friday afternoon, and he did it because he would rather somebody had said it in a room before somebody asked him about it in a corridor. **That is all I have come to tell you. I have not come to ask you for a name, I have not come to ask you for a book, and I have not come to tell you what I have found out about anybody, because I have not found out anything and if I had I would not put it on your table.**"

She looked at the tablecloth for a while.

"That is a strange thing to have come here for."

"It is," he said. "It is the only thing I have got and I have been putting it about four buildings for four months."

Then he said, because it was the sentence he had had ready since about eleven o'clock the previous night and because he had never noticed until the words were out that he had not made it, and had not known for a moment that he was saying it, and had certainly not known that he had first read it in a front office on a street in Kettle Rows in the mouth of a woman of thirty-three who does a police officer's job on a Wednesday:

"**A case that has been closed does not have an owner.**"

He heard it go out into the room and he heard where it had come from, and he did not take it back, and he did not explain it, and the woman did not move and did not look at him differently and did not appear to have heard anything at all that was not already in the room.

"There is a thing I have been not asking," she said, about a minute afterwards, and moved the saucer two inches to the left. "You are going to want to be the fifth person who did not ask me a question, and I am going to be straight with you, because you have been straight with me. **I have a person I cannot take.**"

"I am not going to ask."

"I know you are not. I am telling you because there is going to be a Friday in about eleven days and I am not going to be able to do it, and I have told nobody, and you are a stranger who cannot do anything about it, and I have got to tell somebody before the eleventh or I am going to be no use to anybody at all."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be sorry, it is a Friday," said the woman, "and it is a Thursday, and it is a Tuesday, and it is a Tuesday when I have to say a thing like that to a stranger in a front room and it is on the Tuesday that I know which of the two of us this is."

"Every one of them is a Friday," she said, without being asked. "Since the spring before last, all of them. I have a calendar on the kitchen wall with nothing written on it and I have looked at that calendar more times than is sensible. I asked the man in the parade once, at this table, in this room, about two years ago, and he said he had had four people through his back door and that it was always a Friday and that he had never worked out why. **I have not worked it out either and I have stopped expecting to, and it is the only thing about this house I would like to understand.**"

"Does it matter what day it is."

"It matters if somebody is waiting for me," said the woman. "And nobody ever is. I go on Fridays, and on the other six days I am in this house and I do not go anywhere, and I have not had a night off since Thursday, and I am not going to discuss that with you, because you have come here to ask me about a Friday and not about a bed under a stair."

Elias Venn stood in a front room in Pellow Street on Monday the twenty-fifth of March 2030 and said the sentence he had read in somebody else's mouth on the twenty-second of February without knowing he had it, and he did not know afterwards whether a man could hear a thing in his own voice that he had learned in a counter, and he decided that he did not care, because the woman's hand had been flat on the table beside a book for about ninety seconds and she had not been waiting for him to say anything, and because in four years four people had put their hands on that book and got a strange look and gone quiet, and she had thought it was religion, and that was a thing that was true and it was in a room and it was not going in a book of his.

---

He was out on the pavement at about ten to twelve and the door was shut behind him and the house had gone back to being a house with a bell-pull that did not work.

He wrote nothing in his own book for two days afterwards. On the Tuesday evening he wrote one line and it was this:

> *The woman at eighteen. Nothing. She gave me the address I already had and a question I could not answer and a person I cannot help and I am going to carry the last one for about a year.*
>
> *And I said a thing out loud in her front room that I read off a woman in a front office in Kettle Rows on the twenty-second of February, and I did not know I had it, and she did not look up, and I have not worked out what that means and I am not going to write down what I think it means.*

Behind the door, in the front room, a woman of sixty-three took her hand off the table, put the cloth back over the hardback book where it had been, and squared it at both ends with two fingers, and then went through to the kitchen and left the radio on.
