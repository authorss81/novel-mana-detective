# Chapter 0129 — Eleven

He got to Corbie Street at about twenty past one on Tuesday the ninth of April and stood on the pavement on the other side of the road from the front of a police station for about two hours, and it rained twice and stopped twice, and the ground-floor rooms had a door with a glass panel in it and behind the glass there was a table with about nine people round it, and he was not going to be let into the building and he had known that for six days and had spent the six days not finding a way round it, which was the first honest thing he had done since February.

The list was on a board inside the glass, on the wall by the door, and he read it from the pavement. There were nineteen names on it and he could read about two thirds of them at that distance, and eleven of the nineteen were not staff.

The hardback book was on the table. He could see it from the road, a dark shape about two inches thick with a marker in it, and there was a box on a notice beside the door with a slot in the lid, and there was a pen on a string hanging beside the box, and for two hours the pen did not move.

Somebody had been out and put a fresh coat of paint round the frame of the notice, badly, over the top of the old one, so that two rectangles of slightly different white were showing through and the date on the top slip had been half painted over and written out again in a different hand. Nobody had looked at it. That was the whole of the wall and he read all of it twice.

At about ten past two the door opened and a man of about thirty-eight came out with a paper cup, stood on the step for a minute talking to somebody inside, and went back in, and Elias Venn watched all of it and felt something go out of him, because it meant the door was not sealed, and it meant nothing else at all.

---

They started at two o'clock, because the notice said two o'clock.

A man stood up and spoke for about four minutes, and he had a folder, and Elias Venn did not know who he was. Then somebody said something and about four people nodded at once. Then a woman stood up on the far side of the table and said something short, and a man with a clipboard wrote something down on a form, and did not write it on the form for long, and the form went down the table to somebody else and was signed at the far end and came back.

The door opened again at about a quarter past two and a woman of about forty-nine came out and stood under the awning and telephoned somebody for ninety seconds and went back in.

Then, at about half past two, a woman who was not a member of staff stood up on the far side of the table, and she was not large and she had a bag on the floor at her feet and she had a registration number on the top of a piece of paper in front of her, and the man at the head of the table said something to the room and then said something shorter to her, and she said one thing back, and it was not long, and then the room was quiet in a way he could see through a window.

Elias Venn started counting her.

He got to six before he noticed what he was doing, and he took his hand off his knee and put it flat on the wall behind him and stayed absolutely still for about four seconds.

She was standing up. Her mouth was moving. He was four feet from a door with a glass panel in it and he was counting how many times a woman of forty-seven's mouth moved in a room, and if he had gone on to eleven then he would have had a number, and he did not have a right to the number, and there was no instrument in this city or any other that would give him one, and he had said so out loud in a room in a refuge in Kettle Rows in front of two witnesses in February and he was not going to unsay it on a wet pavement in April.

He did not count again. He stood on the pavement for the next fifty minutes and watched a woman of about sixty-three in a chair on the far side of that table take a pen out of a handbag and put it on a hardback book, and he watched her write, and the writing was slow, and it went for a long time, and at some point in the middle of it she stopped and put her hand flat on the page and left it there for a moment, and then she went on.

He could not hear one word of it. The glass was single and there was a fan going in that room and a radiator and nine people, and there was a lorry on the parade, and the whole of what he had for two hours was a woman writing on a table, and a doctor standing, and a man with a clipboard not writing.

At about twenty past three the writing stopped.

There was a silence at that table that he could see and could not account for, and then somebody said something and about five people said something at once, and the man with the clipboard put his pen down and shook his head, and the woman of sixty-three closed the hardback book and put her hand on the cover of it and left it there, and the woman of forty-seven sat down.

Nobody went to the notice. Nobody went near the box. At about twenty-five past three the door opened and eleven people came out of it over about four minutes, and they stood about on the pavement in the rain in twos and threes and did not go, which was the thing Elias Venn had not expected and could not afterwards explain to anybody: eleven people standing about outside a police station in the rain at half past three in the afternoon, not leaving.

---

He had a book of his own and he did not write anything in it that afternoon, and this is the reason, and he gave it out loud to a busker on the corner at about half past three and the busker did not want it and got a coin for it.

Nobody writes down what they were told by a person who is not in the room. That is not a rule anybody has written down. It is a habit, and habits are cheaper than rules, and by about ten past three on the ninth of April 2030 a man standing on a pavement in Kettle Rows with his book shut in his hand had worked out that if he wrote down eleven names he would have written down something about eleven people that a woman of sixty-three had said out loud in a room to a doctor, and that he had not been asked, and that a doctor had been asked, and that the person who had been asked was in the room and had come out in the rain and had gone up the street without speaking to anybody, and that if he put her eleven names in a book of his own then there would be two lists in this city with eleven names on them and no surnames on either, and that in about two years somebody would be able to say that the two of them had been compared, and that they had not, and that nobody would ever be able to prove that they had not.

He had heard that sentence in an office above a shop in April, and it had a person in it who had read a letter about a building and had decided, on a Tuesday, to be the fourth person anybody had ever asked.

---

The woman at the desk inside the door was about fifty-five and had a cardigan and a badge and a telephone, and she was the only person in that building who was going to say anything to him, and the door was shut most of the way.

"Are you on the list."

"No."

"Then I cannot let you in."

"That is the correct answer and I have been expecting it since Friday and I am not going to argue with you about it. **I am not here to be in the room. I have been told there is a review of a contract and I would like to stand in the building.**"

"There is no standing in the building."

"There must be somebody standing in the building."

"The corridor." She said it the way a clerk says a thing she has said four hundred times. "**The list is in the corridor, and about nine people are in that corridor at a quarter past two, and nine of them are reading that board, and two of them are my colleagues. I am not going to have a stranger in a corridor with my colleagues and a board.**"

"That is fair. I will be on the pavement."

"You are on the pavement."

"Then I would like to say one thing, and I would like to say it to you and not to the room, and it is about the corridor, and I am going to say it badly."

She did not open the door.

"There is not a corridor in this city for a member of the public," said Elias Venn. "I was told that in an office in April by the best-informed person I have met in six months and I did not believe her, and I have been on a pavement for two hours working out why she was right, and I have worked out that it is not because a building is cruel. **It is because a corridor is where the list is, and the list is the only thing in that building which belongs to the people on it, and a person who has not been asked is not on it, and there is nowhere in this city for a man to stand that is not somebody else's list.**"

The woman at the desk did not say anything for about four seconds.

"**That is the first thing anybody has said at that door about the corridor and it is true, and I am not going to be the one who agrees with a member of the public in front of my own glass,**" she said through it. "Sit on the wall if you want. Not on the step, because they come out of that door about twenty times an hour and the step is where they walk."

"Can I ask you one thing about the notice."

"You have not asked me anything about the notice and I have not said anything about the notice." She did not move away from the glass. "Ask it."

"The pen on the string. Has anybody ever put a name in that box."

"No."

"In four reviews?"

"**In the four I have been on this desk for, and I have been on this desk for nine years, and there have been seven.**" She said it the way she said the corridor. "And I can tell you the pen has been on that string for the whole nine years and I have watched it, and it has been taken off the string nine times, and every single one of the nine was a man from the trust or the company who wanted to write something on a form, and every single one of the nine put it back on the string afterwards, and not one of the nine ever put a name in the box, **and I have never once seen anybody take it off the string and then stand there.**"

"That is a pen nobody has ever wanted."

"That is a pen and a box and a card that says *or say it on the day*," said the woman at the desk, "and they went up on the fourteenth of January and I have been on this desk for nine years, and in nine years I have worked out that the three things on that wall are the only three things in this building that do not have anybody's job attached to them, and that is why they have not worked, and it is not because nobody wants to put a name in a box. **It is because everybody who wants to has been told to want to by somebody who works here.**"

He sat on the wall.

---

For two hours, on a wall on a wet street, a man in a good coat was close enough to a room to see eleven people in it and not close enough to hear one word, and the sentence that took him six months to arrive at was on a sheet of paper in a folder in a carrier bag on a shelf behind a door, and the hardback book was open on a table, and the pen on the string was where it had been for nine years, and a woman of sixty-three was about to go home.

---

Martin Rowntree was one of the eleven on the pavement, with a clipboard. Gareth Prothero was one of them, with a folder, and neither of them had a job any more and both of them were there.

A woman of about forty-nine from the housing charity was saying something to Rowntree that was not finishing, and a man of about fifty-eight from the trust was on his telephone in the doorway, and a woman of about thirty-six who had come on her own was standing by the wall saying nothing to anybody, and there was a woman of about sixty-three in the doorway with a handbag on her shoulder and a hardback book under her arm.

The woman of sixty-three stopped on the step.

Elias Venn was sitting on a wall about fifteen feet away, and he had about four seconds in which she could have looked at him, and she did look at him, and it was not a long look and it was not an unfriendly one, and there was nothing in it that asked him for anything at all.

Then she went down the step and turned left and went up the street at the pace of a person who has somewhere to be at six o'clock. She did not stop again and she did not speak to him.

He did not get up off the wall.

He had thought about it on the Friday, and on the Saturday, and on the Sunday, and he had a reason ready, and the reason was that a man in a good coat who has been outside a door for two hours is then introduced to a woman of sixty-three by a stranger who has not been asked to introduce anybody, and that a person who has come to a room on her own two feet for four years, and who has never once sent anybody to anybody, is not going to want a man in a good coat to cross a road to say thank you for it.

He sat on the wall until the rain started again and until the doorway was shut and the last of them had gone, and then he got up and walked to the end of the street and stood at the corner and took out his own book and wrote one line on a page and put it away again.

> *The ninth of April. Eleven names in the hardback book on the table. A doctor read them out, one at a time, and a woman of sixty-three wrote them, and a man on a pavement did not hear one of them and has not written down one of them. The box on the notice is for a twelfth and it is empty. The card over the first card still says or say it on the day.*

He looked at the six words of the second card for longer than he looked at the rest of it, and he had a thought about them, and the thought was that a card saying *or say it on the day* is a card that anybody may use on the day, and that it is the only piece of paper in this case with an open door in it, and that it was put up by a man with a folder in January because he was frightened, and that a woman in a doorway in a terrace had said on the twenty-fifth of March that she would be able to come on a Friday, and that a box on a notice with a pen on a string hanging beside it is not a record and is not a form and is the only thing in this city in nine months that nobody has been refused by.

He did not write that down either.

There were eleven names in a hardback book on a table inside a building on Corbie Street, and he had not been in the room, and there was a doctor at the table who had carried eleven names in her own head since about 2021 and had said them out loud to a room, and a woman of sixty-three had written them down in a hand that had been writing first names for four years for nothing, and a man in a good coat had been told by a Deputy Chief the day before that the last name in a book is not an appointment but a person, and a person has to ask.

He had asked. That was the whole of what he had done for six months, and the box on the notice was still empty, and the second card over the first card said *or say it on the day*.
