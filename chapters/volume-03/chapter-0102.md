# Chapter 0102 — A Statement Superseded

He got to the lane behind Corrie Street at about ten to eight in the morning and stood at the end of it for a while, because there was nothing else to do at ten to eight in the morning, and a man who has been told that a letter exists and has not been told where it is has a very narrow morning.

She came down the lane at about five past nine and stopped, which she did not have to do, and she was carrying a notebook and not a case.

"Nadia Kest," she said. "I am not here in uniform and I am not on duty and if you want to complain about that you can do it at the front office and I will not stop you."

"You have come round to Kettle Rows at a quarter past nine in the morning."

"I have." She did not apologise for it. "There is a letter in a building of mine that came in a tray on Wednesday, about a case that was closed on the ninth of January, and there are two things I can do with a letter about a closed case and they are both bad. I can write it off, which is what a tray is for, and then in about four years a man is going to turn up at a counter somewhere and ask whether anybody ever wrote to him and the answer will be that nobody did, and he will be right and it will be nobody's fault, because a tray is a tray. Or I can put it in front of somebody who can reopen it, and **reopening it is not mine to do**."

"You could go to a Deputy Chief."

"I could go to a Deputy Chief," said Nadia Kest, "and I am not going to tell you her name, and the reason I am not going to tell you her name is not secrecy, it is that if I say a name to you then you will go and stand in front of that name with a question, and I have spent twenty-two years trying to make sure that officers do not go and stand in front of names. That is the whole of what I have learned and I am not going to unlearn it in a lane at a quarter past nine in the morning."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is a thing that happened to me, and you have accepted it, and I would like you to notice that you did it in about a second and a half and that four months ago I would have had to explain it twice."

She looked down the lane and then back at him.

"I am going to read you a letter," she said. "I am not going to give you the file and I am not going to let you near a building with a sign on it, and I am going to tell you why in a moment. First I am going to read you a letter, because a letter is a document that came into a building out of a tray and a document that came into a building out of a tray is not a file yet, and while it is not a file it is a piece of paper in a drawer, and I can read you a piece of paper."

"Read it."

She did not have it with her. She opened the notebook, turned it round, and read out of it, and he understood afterwards that she had copied it out at the desk on Wednesday afternoon and taken it home and copied it again, and that the notebook in her hand was not the file notebook and had nothing in it that the file notebook did not have.

"Twenty February, twenty thirty. *To the officer holding the case. I signed a statement on the eleventh of December about the night of the ninth. I did not do what it says I did. I have never done a working in my life and I do not know what one is. Please tell me what to do about it.* Signed: **Ruben Sallis**, and an address in Dromond Street, and then a number in a second line under the signature, six digits and a letter, and she read the number out and did not explain it. "That is his date, the eleventh of February. It came into a tray on the morning of the twentieth. **Nine days.** Somebody sat on it for nine days and it was not on purpose, because nothing about a tray is on purpose, and the reason it was not on purpose is that there is a person in that building whose job it is to clear trays, and there were two hundred and ten other pieces of paper in front of it, and one of them was about a stolen bicycle."

"Who cleared it."

"I did," said Nadia Kest. "I cleared it, and I read it, and I have not been able to put it down since Wednesday morning, and I have not slept, and I am telling you that because you are going to find out whether I have slept from the way I am standing."

---

They sat in a car park on Ferry Street with a bay number on the wall in front of them, because it was the only place with two chairs and no people in it.

"Here is what I can tell you and I am going to say it in order and I am not going to go round it," said Nadia Kest. "A dangerous-working certificate for the night of the ninth of December was issued out of the Proof Registry on the ninth of January at some time in the afternoon. **A month after your man put his name to two pieces of paper in one room, and two days after he gave a statement to a police officer.** The case was closed the day the certificate went out, because that is what the certificate is for. It is not a case about who did it. It is a document that says a working of a particular kind happened on a particular night in a particular place, and once the document exists the case has answered its own purpose and it is shut."

"Certificate for what kind of working."

"**A self-directed working performed by a person on their own person in a licensed or occupied premises.** That is the class. It means, in words, that a man did a thing to himself, on himself, deliberately, and that it is finished, and that nobody in that building did anything wrong, and that the building does not carry any liability at all." She put her hands on her knees. "That certificate has not been touched since the ninth of January. It is not going to be touched now. And the withdrawal that came through my building on Wednesday morning does not touch it, and **could not touch it if it had been written in blood and brought in by a man with a warrant and his grandmother.**"

"Then the letter does nothing."

"The letter does one thing, and I am going to tell you what it is, and then I am going to tell you the thing underneath it, and the thing underneath it is the whole of why I am in a car park." She turned the notebook over and shut it. "**The letter does this: it stops being true that he said it.** That is all. From Wednesday morning, in the eyes of this force, Ruben Sallis has not given a statement about the ninth of December, because he has withdrawn the statement, and a withdrawn statement is one that the person who gave it no longer stands behind, and a file that contains a statement the witness has withdrawn is a file that says *this account is not available*. **It does not say it is wrong. It says it is not available, and there is a difference and I have spent two days on the difference and it is a real one and it is the only thing I have got.**"

"That is worse."

"That is *nothing*, and it is the difference that is the problem, and I am going to give it to you in a sentence and I am not going to give it to you twice, so write it down." She waited until he had the pen up. "**A witness can withdraw and a certificate cannot be withdrawn, and they are two different documents in two different buildings, and the law has never once had to say which of them matters more.**"

He wrote it down, in the car park, on the paper side of the notebook, because the page side was her notebook and he had not asked.

"Now the thing underneath it," said Nadia Kest. "The case is closed. A closed case is reopened by a supervisory authority, and the supervisory authority above me is a Deputy Chief, and if I put a letter in front of her about a closed case on the ninth of January then in about a week a man who is not me will be asking why a member of the public was in a car park with the officer holding it, and I will not be able to answer that question in a way that does him any good, because **the answer is that I rang him, and a man who rings a witness and asks him what he has got is a man who has shown somebody with a good coat where the witness is.**"

He looked up.

"That is the second time somebody has said that to me in five months about a different person," he said, "and the other person said it about himself, and I did it anyway, and I would not have done it if he had not asked me not to."

"He has not asked you not to."

"No," said Elias Venn. "He has not, because I do not know where he is, because a woman on a night shift in that building would not give me a surname on Friday and I have not been back since."

"That," said Nadia Kest, "is the first sensible thing anybody in this city has said to me about a witness in four months, and I would like it recorded that you said it to me and not the other way round, and I would like it recorded that you have just told a police inspector that you are not going to be a route, which means that if I do come and find you, I will be doing it because I have run out of people, and not because you made it easy."

She stood up and put the notebook in her coat.

"I am going to do three things in the order I am going to do them, and none of them is reopening a case. One: I am going to find out whether the letter had been sitting in a tray for nine days or in a filing system for nine days, and there is a difference and only one of them is a person. Two: I am going to go and stand in a corridor and describe your man to about thirty people without saying his name, which is a thing I am allowed to do, and two of those thirty will know something. Three: I am not going to give you a certificate number, and if you ask me for it a second time I will not speak to you for a fortnight, and I am telling you now so that you can decide what to do with the next fortnight."

"I will not ask you."

"You might need to. There is a building at Harrow Lane and it has a counter and it sells copies to anybody and it is four minutes' walk from where I am standing, and a member of the public is allowed in it, and the fee is not extravagant, and I have never in twenty-two years had a member of the public walk in there on his own account." She was already walking. "I am not telling you to go. I am telling you it is possible, which is a different thing and I have been in this job long enough to know the difference matters."

---

He used the middle hour of the afternoon on the rehabilitation enquiries counter at 6 Verge Row, because it was the one counter in this city that a member of the public was allowed to stand at, and because he had four numbers in a notebook that he had carried since the twenty-third of January and had not been able to do anything with.

Maureen Skelland was forty-four and had been on that counter eleven years and she said general questions were free before he had got to the counter, which is a thing that had begun as a joke and had stopped being one.

"I am not going to give you a number," she said. "I am not going to look at a number for you. Eleven years and I have never looked at a number for a member of the public and I am not going to start in February because you have got a good coat on and a face like a Wednesday."

"I am not going to give you a number either. I want to know whether a claim number carries a premises."

"It does not."

"Then how does anybody get from a claim to a building."

"You have already got the building," said Maureen Skelland. "That is the whole of it and it took me four years to be able to say that sentence without sounding pleased with myself, so forgive me if I do. **A claim does not carry a premises. A claim carries an account, and an account has a registration, and a registration has a premises, and the premises is on a different page in a different building and it is not on the claim, and the only thing that joins them is a person who already knows both, and that person is a clerk, and a clerk is on a floor.**"

"Then the four offices."

"Which four."

"The claim, the account, the premises and the certificate."

Maureen Skelland put her pen down, which was the first sign she had given of interest in eleven years and she gave it and then took it back.

"That is a good list and I am not going to tell you whether it is the right one, and I am going to tell you that there are more than four and that when somebody says four it is because they have been counting the ones they can get into." She looked at the leaflet rack instead of at him. "You cannot get into any of them. That is not my opinion, that is a fact about a counter, and the reason it is a fact is that the only one of them you could walk into today is the one that sells you a piece of paper, and that one will sell it to anybody and will not tell you a single thing about the other three, and that is not a scandal. **A shop that sells the same thing to everybody and explains nothing is the last honest building in this city and I have worked on four floors that are not.**"

"Have you ever had a person come in here with a claim number and a story."

"About four hundred times a year." She picked the pen up again. "And about four hundred times a year the person has come in with a number and a story and has wanted me to find the building, and about four times a year somebody has come in with a number and no story and has wanted to know who signed a thing, and those four are the ones I think about at home, and I have never once been able to help one of them and I have never once been asked to try."

She looked at the clock over the leaflet rack.

"You have been in my queue for eleven minutes and I have told you three things and I have not asked you one question, and that is my whole job and I have never been able to explain it to anybody in a nicer way than that." She put the pad away. "Come back when it is about a person and not a number. I am better at people. Everybody is better at people. **It is numbers that this city has organised.**"

---

At about two o'clock in the afternoon he was standing on the far side of a road in Kettle Rows looking at a building, and it was not the building he expected.

Two Anselm Street was a building of flats over a ground floor, four storeys, brick, with a wooden stair inside it and a handrail on one side only. There was a shop that had been a shop and was now an office with a card in the window that said **ANSELM STREET REFUSE — OPEN 24 HOURS — BEDS BY REFERRAL OR WALK-IN**, and there was a lamp over the door that worked, and the lamp was the only thing on the street that looked newer than 1990.

The lamp was lit at two in the afternoon.

Inside, on the left, there was a hall with a counter in it and four chairs bolted to the floor and a man of about thirty-six asleep in the third chair with a newspaper folded under his arm. On the right there was a door and a shelf of leaflets, and there was a wall next to the door with a notice pinned to it that had been there long enough to have lost its curl.

He read it standing up.

> **ROWAN TRUST — CONSULTATION ON THE FUTURE OF THE ANSELM STREET SERVICE**
> The contract for this service ends on **31 March 2030**.
> A review will be held on **Tuesday 9 April 2030 at 2.00 p.m.**, Corrie Street Rooms, Corrie Street.
> **Anyone who uses this service, works in it, has used it, or has lost it may attend and speak.**
> Please put your name in the book on this table. **A box is provided below for anyone who would rather not give their name in the book.**
> Rowan Trust, Contract Office.

The book on the table was hardback and about two inches thick and it had a date on the front in somebody's handwriting, and he opened it and there were three pages of ruled paper in it and **there was not one name on any of them.**

The box below was a printed square of card with a line drawn round it and a hole in the middle of the line, and the line said **NAME**, and it was for a person to write a name on a card and put it in a box, and it was empty, and above it somebody had stuck a second card over the first one at some point in January, and the second card said **or say it on the day**.

Nobody had said it on the day. The day had not been.
