# Chapter 0118 — What Was Done to Doris Sowerby

The day room at 2 Wexford Row is on the ground floor and it is warm because the warm water is on in that building between seven in the morning and about eleven at night, and it has two radiators, a table in the middle of it with a jigsaw on it, a television on a bracket with the sound off, four armchairs against the wall with the covers on them, a side table with a tin on it, and eight chairs and two wheelchairs, and a door that shuts.

Sana Okafor counted the chairs twice, both times in the first four seconds, and it was eight, and she was aware of the number afterwards and did not think about it again.

The charge nurse was called Delphine Otieno and she had been in that building for twelve years and she stood in the doorway at about ten past ten and said, "I can stay."

"Not today," said Sana Okafor.

"I would rather stay."

"I know you would, and that is why. I am going to sit down with three people and shut the door and I am not going to have somebody stood by a shut door for two hours, because in about six weeks somebody is going to write a note about today and I am not going to have my own name and a nurse's name on the same page with a line about a door in between them."

"All right," said Delphine Otieno. "I am on the desk until two. If you want me I am on the desk until two."

"I will want you at two," said Sana Okafor, and the nurse went, and the door shut.

There were two of them in the room.

---

Vi Harker was seventy-one and she was going into a home in April and she had been told on the Thursday that it was April, and she had a cardigan on the back of her chair and a pair of spectacles on a string round her neck and a cardigan folded over the back of the other chair, folded the way a person folds a thing they have decided about.

"Mrs Harker, I am a doctor and I have seen you here on nine occasions since the seventh of February, and I have not come here this morning to look at your hip. I have come to tell you something about the evening of the twenty-second of January, and I am going to tell you all of it, and then I am going to stop talking and you are going to tell me something, and it will not be a question."

"You'll have to be quick," said Vi Harker, "because the old get confused and I have been told so."

"I am not going to be quick and I am going to tell you again in a moment if you need it."

She told her. She told it plainly and in order and without any of the words for it. On the evening of the twenty-second of January, in this room, at about ten to ten at night, a dangerous working was performed. It was not an accident. It was not a treatment anybody here gave anybody. It was done by somebody who came in, and nobody present knew what it was, and it took a short time, and the room went strange for about half a minute, and then it was over.

"Did I have my tea first," said Vi Harker.

"I do not know. Nobody has written it down."

"You could have asked the girls."

"I could have, and the girls were not told either, and that is the whole of the reason I am here," said Sana Okafor. "Nobody in this room was told. Not you, and not the woman next to you, and not anybody on this floor, and that has been the position since the twenty-second of January and I have come here this morning as a doctor and not as anybody else, and if you would like me to leave I will leave, and if you would like me to stay I will stay and I will not write anything down."

"You will write something down," said Vi Harker. "You have got a face like a woman writing things down."

"I have got a face like a woman who has not got a pen," said Sana Okafor, and turned her own hands over, and there was nothing in them, and Vi Harker looked at the two empty hands and said, "Well," and settled back into the chair.

"Ten past ten at night," she said, after a while. "I was asleep. I am asleep by nine and I am up at six, and that is the whole of what I remember, and I remember the day because the wireless was on and there was a thing about a bridge, and I do not remember the day after that at all."

"You were asleep."

"I was asleep and awake, which is what I have always done and what my mother did," said Vi Harker, "and I have never once been able to explain it to anybody and I have stopped trying, and if you have come to tell me I was asleep then you have told me the only thing anybody could have told me, and it is the answer I would have given you, and I am not going to argue with a doctor about it in a warm room."

Sana Okafor sat in the chair opposite for about a quarter of an hour and let the room be quiet, and Vi Harker looked at the television with the sound off for a while and then said, "Are they going to make me sign something."

"No."

"Good," said Vi Harker. "Because I have signed four things since I came in here and every one of them was about what happens next, and I am going into a home in April, and I do not want a piece of paper going with me. **I have had eight weeks of being told what is in a file and I have not asked for one word of it, and if you write something down about this morning then it goes in a file, and it will go to the home, and the home will have it in a drawer, and in about two years somebody will want to know why I was in a place like this.** So no. Nothing written. And I would like the last of that tea, because you have taken nearly an hour of my morning and I have got a day."

She got up out of the chair. Sana Okafor helped her with the cardigan and did not write anything down, and there was a noise from the corridor of a trolley and a woman's voice saying a number, and neither of them said anything else.

---

Doris Sowerby was eighty-one and had been in this building since the twenty-eighth of November with a hip, and she was awake, and she had been awake since about six, and she was at the table with the jigsaw in front of her and about nine pieces in the open tin and a piece in her hand that did not go anywhere.

"Mrs Sowerby—"

"**Nine,**" said Doris Sowerby.

Sana Okafor had not got that far.

"Nine," said Doris Sowerby, and put the piece down on the table, out of the tin, in the wrong place, which is where it went when it did not go. "Nine are out. I have counted them every day since Christmas and I have not told a soul, because I could not think what it would be for, and you are the first person who has ever asked me a question about anything in this room and it was about the wrong thing."

"I did not ask you a question."

"You asked me what I remember," said Doris Sowerby, "which is a question, and everybody else has asked me about my hip."

She was eighty-one and she had a cardigan of her own folded on the back of the chair, and Sana Okafor had seen it there on nine occasions and had never asked about it, and she asked about it now.

"Do you fold that every day."

"I fold it every day at about four o'clock when I have finished," said Doris Sowerby, "and I have folded it every day since the twenty-eighth of November, and I fold it because it is mine and it is not a house cardigan, and a person should know which of their things are theirs."

"Mrs Sowerby, I am going to tell you what was done to you on the evening of the twenty-second of January."

"I know what was done to me," said Doris Sowerby.

Sana Okafor stopped.

"**I have known since the Wednesday morning,**" said Doris Sowerby. "You can sit down if you like, doctor, because you have gone the colour of the wall and you have a thing to say and there is no reason for you to stand up and say it."

She sat down.

"A man said sorry, love," said Doris Sowerby. "**Twice.** To somebody, and it was not to me, because I am not the one he was talking to, or if I was then he was talking to a room. And before that there was a noise like somebody moving a chair and being careful about it, and you do not move a chair carefully in this room, because the chairs are against the wall and we have all been told about the chairs since we came in, and somebody moved a chair carefully. And then there was something else and I cannot tell you what it was, and it is not that I have forgotten it, and I want that said, because that is the thing people say. **I have not forgotten it. It is not there.** It is like a place in a road that you have walked past four thousand times and then one day it is not a place, it is nothing at all, and you still know the way your feet go."

"How long."

"I do not know. There was a man on the wireless and there was the bridge and there was the chair, and then it was the day after the twenty-second, which was a Wednesday, and I have had eight weeks of the Wednesday and I can see every hour of it, and the Tuesday is not there."

Sana Okafor did not write any of it down. She had her hands in her lap and she was aware of the weight of the not writing it, and she was fifty-eight minutes into a day that she had planned for two hours, and she understood in about the second minute that two hours was not going to be enough and that nobody was going to be able to shorten it, and that she had not been told anything about that either, on the form, at Vine Street, at half past eleven the day before.

"You told nobody," she said.

"No."

"Why."

Doris Sowerby put her hand flat on the table, on the tablecloth, in the way that people do.

"**Because I thought it was a dream, and then I thought it was not a dream, and I have been on a ward since the twenty-eighth of November and I have worked out what a ward does with a thing you are not sure of.**" She said it without any drama at all. "They are kind about it. That is what I want said. They are extremely kind about it, and they would not lie to me, and they would move me, and they would put me in a room on my own, and I would never see the woman with the spectacles again, and I have been here since November and it is the first place since 2019 where I have not been frightened all the time, and I am eighty-one, and I am not going to be moved out of it over a noise and a man's voice."

"That is a decision and I am not going to argue with it."

"No," said Doris Sowerby. "You are not, and I would not have argued with you either, and I want to tell you the other half so that you know what I did about it. **I have told nobody and I have not been silent. I have been doing something for eight weeks and I would like to know if it was mad.**"

"What have you been doing."

"I have been counting," said Doris Sowerby. "Not the beds and not the people, I would never. I have been counting the holes in that puzzle, and there are nine, and I have counted them about four hundred times, and I have been through that room since the Wednesday after and I have tried to work out whether the nine is a number about anything. It is not a number of anything I have been able to find. It is not the number of anybody in here, because there are only the two of us and the day girl, and it is not the number of days, because I have been here since November. **And I have never once been able to work out what it is for, and that is what has kept me awake, and not the other thing.**"

Sana Okafor looked at the board on the table and did not look at it for long.

"I am not going to tell you what the nine is," she said.

"That is what I thought you would say, and I am glad, because a doctor who told me the nine was something would be a doctor telling me the nine was something, and a sister would tell me the nine was something, and then it would be a thing about me and it would be in a file and it would go to a home in April."

"I am going to tell you what the nine is not. It is not about you and it is not about anybody in this room, and there is nothing in this building that has ever been counted, and I know that because I have asked, and I am not going to tell you what I asked, and I have not found anything." She stopped, and then said the thing she had come to say, and she said it as a woman of forty-seven who had been in a laboratory in January and had watched eleven people sit in a room and had not asked one of them. "**Nobody has ever asked you whether the nine was a number to you. That is what is wrong with it. Not the nine.**"

Doris Sowerby put her head on one side.

"Well," she said. "Now somebody has."

---

She asked her the question at about twenty past twelve, with the door shut, and there was no water in the room and the clock on the wall was a second slow and there was a trolley in the corridor every twenty minutes.

"Mrs Sowerby, I have not written anything down this morning and I am not going to, and I am about to ask you one thing, and it is not a question, and it is the last thing I am going to ask you today. **What would you like written.**"

"That is a strange thing to ask a person."

"It is the only thing I have got," said Sana Okafor. "If I ask you what you remember I will put it in a set of words that I have chosen, and there will be a set of words in that form in a building somewhere on Friday that has my name at the bottom of it and it will not be your words, and you will read it one day and you will not be able to get at the part of it that was yours. So I am asking you what you would like written, and if the answer is nothing then I write nothing, and that is not a brush-off. That is the whole of the offer."

Doris Sowerby thought about it for a long time, and the trolley came and went.

"**One sentence,**" she said. "And I will give you the sentence, and you will write it in my words and not in yours, and I have thought about it for eight weeks and I have got it ready, which I did not expect, because I have been composing it on the bus and in the bath since the Wednesday and I did not know that is what I was doing."

"Go on."

"You will write it and you will not change the words, and you will not put *patient* on it, and you will not put *reports* on it, and you will not put a time on it that I have not given you." Doris Sowerby took her hand off the table. "**It was not a dream and I would like somebody to write that down who is not a nurse.**"

Sana Okafor did not move for about four seconds.

"That is a sentence about a dream," she said, "and it is a true sentence, and it is the only true thing anybody can say about that night, and I want to tell you what I would have written if I had not asked you, and I am telling you because you are entitled to be the only person in this building who knows what is in the file. I would have written that a patient of eighty-one recalled an auditory experience on the evening of the twenty-second of January with no recollection of the preceding period, and I would have written it in about eleven words and it would have been correct in every word and it would not have had a single word of you in it."

"And mine has got me in it."

"Yours has got you in it and a job in it," said Doris Sowerby, "which I have decided is not the same as writing a person's name down, and you may disagree and you may not, and I am eighty-one and I have got a bath to have."

Sana Okafor went out into the corridor at about ten past one and stood against the wall for a minute, and then went back in and said, "I have to do a thing in the corridor and then I am coming back for a minute and I am not going to be long."

She did the thing in the corridor.

The attendance sheet was on the wall by the door in a wooden holder, and it was ruled in days, and it was a working document and not a record, and it was the operator's. The front of it was the operator's and could not be written on, and there were three lines in February in a second, heavier hand, out and back and back again, with a time against one of them and no reason against any of them.

She turned the sheet over. The back of it was blank, and it had been blank since it was printed, and there was nothing on it at all.

Sana Okafor wrote in the space at the bottom of the back of a sheet of paper in a building she had been asked by a magistrate three days ago to come into and had been given no instrument to come into with, and there was no pen-case and she used a biro out of her own bag, and she wrote:

> *It was not a dream and I would like somebody to write that down who is not a nurse.*

and under it, in her own hand and not in the woman's:

> *W. Sowerby, spoken and dictated, 20 March 2030, in the day room at 2 Wexford Row, in the words above, at her own request, and asked for by her and not by me. Nothing else was written down this morning. S. Okafor.*

She did not tear it off. She put the sheet back in the wooden holder with the back of it showing, because the front was the operator's and a doctor writing on the front of an operator's sheet in a licensed day room is a document, and a document goes in a file, and a file goes to a home in April, and there was a woman of eighty-one who had made a condition and the condition was *not a nurse*.

The three lines in February and the one sentence in March were on the same piece of paper, about a foot apart, and neither of them had anything to do with the other, and that was not a system and it was not an arrangement and it was the only page in the whole of the eight weeks on which anybody had written anything at all about what was done to the three people in that room.

---

*Elias Venn, in the corridor, at about half past one:* *He was not in the building. He was in a car park at the back of it and then he was on a bench at a bus stop, and at about ten past one she came out of a side door and stood in the yard for about four minutes with a folder under her arm, and she did not look at the fence, and he did not get out of the car, and they had agreed that in a corridor, and the agreement was the reason he could sit there at all.*

---

The third one was not in the day room and had not been in it since February.

Mavis Dellow was sixty-nine and she was in a room on the same floor with a bed in it and a chair and a window that looked at a wall about four feet away, and there was a chair in the corridor outside that room as well, and the chair in the corridor was where she was at ten past two, sitting on it with a blanket round her and a plastic cup on the sill.

"You've come at last," she said.

Sana Okafor stopped in the corridor.

"I have not met you before."

"No, but I have been in that room nine times and I have seen you through a door," said Mavis Dellow, "and on the ninth I saw you write something down, and I have been sitting on this chair since the seventh of February waiting to find out whether you were the sort that writes things down."

"I am the sort that writes things down," said Sana Okafor. "I have written one thing down this morning, on the back of a sheet in a wooden holder on a wall, and I am going to tell you what it says, and then I am going to tell you what happened to you, and then I am going to shut that door and sit down, and I am sorry that it has taken until Wednesday."

"Go on, then," said Mavis Dellow, and pulled the blanket up, and did not ask what was written on it.

Sana Okafor told her. The same order. The twenty-second of January, this building, about ten to ten at night, a dangerous working, done by somebody who came in, nobody present knowing what it was, half a minute, over.

"And I was moved out," said Mavis Dellow, "and there is a sheet on that wall with two lines in February in somebody else's hand and my name on one of them, and no reason, and I have seen it and I have asked."

"You have asked."

"The day girl asked for me," said Mavis Dellow. "She is a good girl and she did it twice and she was told no twice, and the second time she was told that the sheet is not for asking about."

"Yes," said Sana Okafor. "She was not wrong, and I am not going to tell you why, and I am going to ask you a question I have no answer to, and I am going to be straight with you about that now rather than afterwards."

"Go on."

"You have never been told what was done to you. I am telling you now, and there is nobody in this building who was told then and there was nobody in this building who was told afterwards, and that is not a mistake that somebody made, that is how it was arranged, and I have not been able to find out who arranged it and I am not going to spend the next ten minutes pretending to you that I have."

"I do not think you have," said Mavis Dellow.

Then she said: "**Was I asleep or was I awake.**"

Sana Okafor did not answer straight away, and the silence went on long enough that Mavis Dellow looked at her.

"I have told you what I know," said Mavis Dellow. "I have been in this chair since the seventh of February and I have been asleep in that bed and awake in it, and I have got a hip that has not worked since before Christmas, and I have had eight weeks of it, and I am not asking you the first part. **I am asking you whether it matters which, and if it does, who decides that it does.**"

"It matters to somebody."

"It matters to somebody," agreed Mavis Dellow. "**That is the whole of the question and you are the first person who has heard it. Eight weeks I have had a day girl and a television with the sound off and not one of them has known that there is a question. They think it is whether I remember. It is not whether I remember. I remember a chair.**"

Sana Okafor stood in a corridor on the ground floor of a building on Wexford Row and found that she did not have an answer, and that the reason was not a technical one and not a legal one and not one that a woman of forty-seven with a registration number could go and look up on a shelf, and that it was the first time in a long time she had been asked a question in her own professional capacity and could not answer it and could not write it down.

"I do not know," she said. "Whether it matters, and who decides."

"Then say it again in a bit, in case it comes to you."

"I cannot tell you whether it matters," said Sana Okafor, "and I can tell you the second half, and the second half is worse than the first, and I am going to say it because you are owed a straight one. **It matters to whoever has to write it down. And nobody is going to put you in a room with whoever that is.** It is going to be a person in an office, or it is going to be a person in a building with a form, and you will be told that a thing is what a thing is because somebody in a building has decided which of the two you were, and the decision will have been made in about four minutes on a Tuesday, and there will be no box anywhere in this city on any form for a person to say *I was both*."

"Then it is not fair."

"It is not fair," said Sana Okafor, "and I have been in this city for nineteen years and I have got a registration number and a folder in my bag and I could not put a single word in it to help you, and I have not got it, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have, and I have never once pretended to anybody and I am not starting."

Mavis Dellow drank about a third of the water out of the plastic cup and put the cup back on the sill.

"Write that down," she said.

"What."

"Write down that you do not know," said Mavis Dellow. "**You have got a sheet on that wall with my name on the front of it in February and no reason, and a doctor wrote a sentence on the back of it this morning for the woman with the jigsaw, and that is the only thing that has happened in this building since Christmas that was not somebody filling in a box.** So write down that you do not know, and put the date on it, and let the next person have to look at a page in this building where a doctor wrote *I do not know* and did not write anything else, and make them decide what to do about that."

Sana Okafor did not move.

"That is not a form," she said.

"No," said Mavis Dellow. "It is a corridor."
