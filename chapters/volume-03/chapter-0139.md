# Chapter 0139 — Nine Minutes

He had been up the stairs twice by eleven o'clock at night on Thursday the twenty-fifth of April and Denise let him through the office on the second time without saying anything, and he found Gaynor Riddell on the landing with her back against the wall and a cup beside her, on the landing with the four doors and the fire door at the end of it, and the board of names on the wall behind his head was about four feet away and he did not turn round to look at it and she knew that he had not.

"Sit down," she said. "There is a chair. It is a fire chair and it is on the landing because somebody decided about eleven years ago that a landing in this building needed somewhere for somebody to sit, and nobody has ever taken it away, and I have used it about four times in nine years and all four times were somebody's husband."

He sat down.

"**You came on Friday and you asked me about a form and I told you about a telephone, and then I told you there was a thing I was not going to tell you, and you said *all right*.**" She drank the tea. "So here it is and it is not a big thing and that is the whole of what I have to say about it."

"Go on."

"**On Friday morning I was shown two pages and asked whether I agreed with them, and I said no, and then I said yes, and the yes is minuted, and it took about nine minutes, and there were two people in the room and one of them had a cup of tea for me and it went cold while I was reading.**"

The landing had a window at one end and the window looked at another building about nine feet away, and the light in it was a strip light, and the strip light did not change, and the woman beside him went on.

"What was in the two pages."

"**The record of a working I did on the eleventh of October last.** It is a small thing. It is on me, on my own person, twice a week, when somebody in this building will not settle at about two in the morning, and I have been trained for it and I have a card for it and it is lawful and it is on the card as lawful. **I do it on myself. That is the whole of the craft and there is nobody else in it.**"

"And the two pages are the record of that."

"The front and the last and the act in the middle. It is a real record and everything on it is true and I have never once seen a word of it that was wrong." She put the cup on the floor. "**And it says that on the eleventh of October a dangerous working was performed in this building by me, and one practitioner, and one person, and one address. And that is true. And in October a colleague of mine was standing in the corridor about four feet away with a torch on for about nine minutes while I did it, because that is what she was doing that night, and she did not take part in it, and she has never taken part in one, and she is not a practitioner and she has no card and she does the nights with me on a Friday.**"

"Then your account and the record do not correspond."

"My account says she was there. The record does not say she was there. **Those are the same fact and they do not correspond, and that is the only disagreement that has ever existed in nine years between me and a piece of paper.**" She looked at the strip light through the window. "**And they showed me the record as two pages and they asked me whether I agreed with it, and I said no, and the woman who asked me said *say that again for the minute*, and I said it again.**"

"And then you said yes."

"**And then I said yes.**"

Elias Venn sat on a fire chair on a landing in a care home and did not say the thing that came into his head, which was a word, and the word was why.

"I want to say why," she said, "and I have not said the why to anybody, and I am going to say it to you, and then you are going to tell me that you have read about this before and I am going to tell you that I do not care."

"All right."

"**There were two people in that room, and I want that written down in the order I am saying it: one of them was the office and one of them was the officer, and the officer was a woman in her forties who had a form on the table in front of her, and she was polite, and she was quick, and she did not once look at the clock, and at no point in those nine minutes did anybody in that room do anything that anybody in this city would call anything.**"

"Then what happened in the nine minutes."

"**Three minutes of the nine were the reading and the rest of it was me.**" She said it and then stopped, and when she went on her voice had not changed at all, which was the thing about it. "The extract came down to me at about half past nine. I read it. Then the officer read the minute out to me, all of it, including the part about what happens to my account afterwards, which I had not known there was a part about. Then she asked me whether I wished to add anything to the record, and I said no, and she said *are you sure*, and I said yes, and she wrote *witness declined to add* in a box, and then she asked me whether I agreed with the record.

"And I said **no**. And she said, *say that again for the minute*, and I said it again. **And then nothing happened for about four minutes.**

"**Nothing happened for about four minutes and I sat in that chair and I read those two pages again from the top, twice, looking for a mistake.** I am a nurse of nine years and I have read about nine hundred clinical records and I am extremely good at finding the wrong figure in a document. **I looked for a wrong figure for four minutes and there was not one, and the date was right, and the building was right, and the thing I did was right, and the only thing about those two pages that I could not get past was that they were complete, and they were complete, and completeness is not an error.**

"And then I said yes. And the woman wrote it down and read it back and I signed it, and she thanked me, and the cup of tea had gone cold, and it took nine minutes, and I have been a person for forty-four years and that is the only time in my life I have been asked a question I could not answer and got it right afterwards."

"Then what was in the minute. You have not told me that yet."

"A name and a time and mine and hers," she said. "It is called a continuity amendment. That is the phrase. It is not called anything else, it is not called a correction, and I looked it up afterwards and the phrase is exactly that, and the form of it is one side with a box for the record and a box for the account and a box for the date and a line for a witness to sign, and it goes into a file and not into me."

"And Helen."

"Helen Naisbitt is on the ward, thirty-nine years old, and she does not know, and she is not going to know, and I have not told her and I am not going to, and you are not going to tell me to tell her and I want to say that now so that you cannot be decent at me about it later." Her voice did not change. "**Because I have worked it out. If my account had gone in with her name in it then there would be two people on a record of a dangerous working in a building, and one of them has no card and no training and was holding a torch, and the next thing that happens to a person like that is not a hearing. It is a form with a box on it and nobody comes and knocks on your door to explain it first.** So I said yes, and the machinery has done the only decent thing anybody in four buildings was ever going to do for Helen Naisbitt, and it did it without asking any of us, and Helen Naisbitt is thirty-nine and she thinks she waters plants on a Tuesday.

"So that is your machinery," said Gaynor Riddell. "**It took a person out of an account and it did not put anybody in danger and nobody in four buildings broke a rule, and I have been the only person in this city who knows both of those things, and I have had six days.**"

"What did you want," said Elias Venn. "On the Friday. When it was finished."

"I wanted to be asked again," said Gaynor Riddell. "**Not in a room. In a fortnight. In writing. I wanted something on a piece of paper that said *the witness may be asked again before the twelfth of May*, and I wanted a box with my name on it, and I wanted to be able to say the word *no* one more time to somebody who had to write it down.** And I did not ask for it, because I sat on this landing on the Friday night and worked out that I was not going to be able to have it, and that asking is the whole of what a person can do and that the request is a thing you put in a box, and there is no box. There is a form for a withdrawal and a form for a copy and a form for a request for a record. **There is not one form in this city that says a person may say a thing twice, and I have been looking for about six days, and I am the person in this building that everybody comes to when they need to know where a form is.**"

"And they will have said no."

"They will have said no in a kind way," said Gaynor Riddell, "and it will have been the fourth time in nine years that somebody has been kind to me about it, and it will have been Pamela on the day desk, and she will have been right."

"It was Friday morning," she said. "The nineteenth. Half past nine, because I start at nine and it is the one hour of the day I am a person and not a nurse."

Elias Venn did not move.

"Yes," he said.

"That is all the information you need." She looked at him. "You have gone the colour of that wall and I am going to let you get on with it, and then I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to go and do eleven people, and I have not done them yet, and they have been waiting since eleven and that is my fault and not yours."

"I am not going to ask you about it."

"I know you are not, and I want to tell you what that costs, because you will not do it and nobody else will either, and so nobody ever does. **You are going to carry the date and I am going to carry the date and neither of us is going to put the two halves together out loud, in this building, ever, and that is the arrangement and the arrangement is going to hold for about four years and then one of us will be ill and the other will be dead and then somebody will find it in a file and neither of us will have to be there.**"

She reached into the pocket of her cardigan and took out a folded sheet of paper and held it out.

"**This is my copy. I am entitled to a copy of the minute of my own undertaking. It is one sheet. It has a name and a time and a record number on it and about four lines.**"

He did not take it.

He could not take it, and he knew exactly why he could not take it, and the reason was printed, and it was a good reason, and he had heard a woman of fifty-four give him a version of it at a counter in Harrow Lane on Tuesday afternoon.

"**A witness is entitled to a copy of a minute of his own undertaking,**" he said, "**and a member of the public is not entitled to a copy of anything at all, and there is no form for me and there is no proceeding and there is no ground, and if you give me that sheet in this corridor at eleven o'clock at night then you are a night sister in a care home who has handed a document to a stranger, and that is a thing you cannot put back in a pocket, and in about four years you will be asked about it by somebody who has the right to ask.**"

"**Yes,**" said Gaynor Riddell. "That is the reason. That is the whole reason and you have got it right in about four seconds, and I have known it since Friday morning, and I put it in my pocket this morning anyway and I have carried it round with me all day and I have twice taken it out and put it back, **because I wanted to see what you would do, and because I am forty-four and I have been in this building nine years and I have not done anything yet in my life that I have wanted somebody to see me do.**"

"I am not going to take it."

"I know you are not going to take it," she said. "**You have not taken a single thing off a person in five months, and the last person who offered you something in this building was me on a Friday, and you did not take that either, and I want to say one final thing and it is the only thing in this conversation I have been waiting two days to say.**"

He waited.

"There is a colleague of mine on the ward on Tuesdays and Thursdays and she does not know, and you are the man who came here in February to find out who was in a room with a man on the ninth of December, and I have worked out in about four days that the person you are looking for and the person they have just done to me are the same shape, and **if you ever go anywhere with what I have told you tonight then a woman in a corridor on a Thursday will have been a woman who agreed, and that is what I will be, and there is nothing in this city that can put me anywhere else.**"

"I will not."

"You will not say that to anybody, because you cannot make a promise like that to a person you have met twice, and I would think less of you if you said it." She put the sheet back in the pocket of her cardigan and squared it. "**So write nothing down. Not my name, not the date, not the two pages, not Helen. I am not asking you to protect me and I am not asking you to protect her. I am asking you to leave a piece of paper alone, and that is the only thing I have wanted out of any man in nine years.**"

He wrote nothing down.

He had a book of his own in the inside pocket of his coat with six things in that pocket and a name written on Monday in a garage office in a hand he had never shaken, and a date he had written down on Monday night in a back room, and a commitment he had made in a corridor in a linen office at a quarter past one in the morning, and the whole of the case was in his own handwriting in about four pages and about nine lines and none of it would be worth one hour of Helen Naisbitt's life.

"You're not writing it down," she said, watching him not do it.

"No."

"Then you are a man who has read a page," said Gaynor Riddell, "and I am a woman who has signed a form, and between the two of you there is not one piece of paper, **and that is the entire state of this city on the twenty-fifth of April and it will still be the entire state of it in ten years, and the only difference in ten years is that I will be fifty-four and you will be forty-nine.**"

She picked her cup up off the floor and looked into it and put it down again, because it was cold and because she was not going to take it to the kitchen while a man in a good coat was on her landing.

"Go down the stairs and out of the front," she said. "**You come up those stairs twice and I have said to you once that the board behind your head is a list of who is in this building and that it is a public document in exactly the way a form is, and I have said it to you because I have had you twice and I do not think you are a man who would read it, and I have been wrong about a great many people in nine years and I would rather be wrong in that direction than the other.**"

He went down the stairs and out of the front of the building past the shuttered counter and the leaflets for a service discontinued in February, and out into a street in Calder Street at about ten past eleven on a Thursday night, and he stood on the pavement for a while with his hands in his pockets, and did not take anything out.
