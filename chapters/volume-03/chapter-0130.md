# Chapter 0130 — A Number Ringed on a Wall

The shop at 3 Wendle Street had nine machines in a row and a counter and a window and a smell, and on the Thursday morning of the eleventh of April the box was out from under the counter and on the counter, and it was a cardboard box with about two hundred sheets in it and about a hundred of them six inches by two, and UNCLAIMED was still on the front of it in the same marker as it had been since 2026.

There was a form on the counter next to the box, and it was one side of A4, and it had a heading and a box for a number of items and a box for a destination and a line at the bottom for a signature, and it did not have a box for a name, and it did not have a box for a reason, and the man behind the counter had already filled in about half of it in biro.

"You have come to ask me about the box."

"I have come to ask whether you have decided about the box."

"**I decided about the box in January and I have not told anybody and you are the first and you did not hear it from me.**" He put the pen down. "A company comes on the eighteenth. It is a company and not a bin, and I have chosen the company over the bin and I have been thinking about it since January, and I want you to understand that choosing the bin would have been the easy and the decent thing and I have done neither." He tapped the form. "The company takes about two hundred pieces of other people's paper and they have a form, and on the form there is a number, and the number is going to be about two hundred."

"And the hundred strips."

"The hundred strips are the half I have thought about most and they are going on the same form under the same number, and there is nothing on that form that says what they are, and the company are not going to ask me, and I have had four years to work out a way to say what they are on one side of A4 and I have not found one."

"You could write it on the form."

"I could write it on the form in the box for a destination and the company would take it as instructions about a delivery," said the man, "**and then a piece of paper in this city would be saying something true that a company has been told by a laundry, and about six months after that somebody would be able to read it. I have not got six months. I have got the eighteenth.**"

Elias Venn looked at the hundred strips for a while.

"You refused me a photograph in March."

"I did."

"Give me the true reason again."

"**A doctor's registration number is not going on a box in a shop, and you asked me that in March and I am telling you the same thing in April, and it is the same answer and it is going to be the same answer in June.**" He did not raise his voice. "And I have been in this trade since 1974 and I have had this shop since 2026 and I have never once been asked for a form by a customer, and a customer's property is not a bin, and I have not opened that box and I am not going to open it, and there is no version of the next four days in which I open it."

"I was not going to ask you to open it."

"You were going to ask me to put a name on the form."

"I was."

"Yes," said the man. "**And I have thought about that for about eleven weeks and I have decided that the reason I have not is not that I will not and it is that I have not got anywhere to put it.**"

---

"I am going to ring a number," he said, at about ten past eleven.

"You said you would."

"I said in March I would ring a number when the box went, and I am not going to tell you what it is about."

"I have never asked you what it is about."

"You have not," said Elias Venn. "**And I have noticed that you have not, twice, and I am not going to ask, and the reason I am not going to ask is not politeness. It is that a number a man rings once and will not say out loud is not a number. It is a favour. And a favour is not a document and it is not in a book and I have spent six months collecting numbers and every one of them has been a door with nothing on the other side, and I am not going to be the man who writes this one down.**"

The man went to the wall and picked up the receiver of the telephone that was on the wall by the door, and it was a telephone that had been in that shop since the year he took it, and it had a pencil and a pad of paper nailed to the wall beside it with about nine numbers on the pad and four of them had gone.

He dialled it from the pad and he did not read it off the pad and he did not say the digits out loud, and it rang four times and a woman answered.

"It is me," he said. "It is the fourth. I have got the box out on the counter and I have had it out since half past eight."

Then he listened for about eleven seconds.

"No," he said. "No, I have understood that. Thank you."

He listened for another twenty seconds and said, "I am not going to argue with you about it, because you are not wrong and it is not that simple, and I have known that since January. I will do it the way you said. Yes. All of them. Yes, that is what I have got."

He put the receiver down and it went back into its cradle on the wall, and the shop went back to being a shop with nine machines in it.

"I am not going to tell you who that was," he said. "And I am not going to tell you what it is about, and I did not say anything on that telephone that you would want written down, and if you had been standing where you are standing you would not have understood one word of it, and I have not said a single untrue thing and I have not said one single true thing either, and that is the whole of what I am able to do about this box and I have known that since January."

---

Then he did the thing that was not about the box.

He took the hardback job book off the shelf behind the counter, which had a rubber band round it and six years of entries in three hands, one of them his wife's, and he turned to the first clean page, and he took the pen off the string that held it to the counter, and he wrote something at the top of the page.

He wrote it without looking at it and he wrote it the way a man writes a thing he has been thinking about since January, and when he had finished he put the pen back on its string and shut the book and put the rubber band round it, and he did not look at the page.

"My name is Roy Tarrant," he said. "**I have had this shop since 2026 and I have not given my name to a customer in four years, and I have given it to a man in a good coat on a Thursday morning because I am going to ring a number this morning and if anything goes wrong then somebody is going to want to know who was standing in this shop, and I would rather it was a man whose name is written down at the top of a page than a man whose name is nowhere.**"

"You wrote it in the book."

"A name said out loud in a shop goes in the air and it does not come back and it is not anywhere on Monday," said Roy Tarrant. "**A name written down goes somewhere. I have known that since about 2027 and I have used it the other way round for four years, because a name in a book in a shop is a name a person can find you by, and I have never wanted to be found. And I have just written it at the top of a page on purpose, and I am not going to rub it out, and that is the first thing I have done in this shop that I could not have done last year.**"

Elias Venn did not write it down either.

The till in the back room at 3 Tanners Row had stood at fifty credits since the twelfth of January, minus eleven that went into an envelope in a post box on the twelfth of March, plus nine that came back in an envelope with a lease in it on the second of April, minus two for a stamp on the same afternoon, and it stood at forty-six, and on Thursday the eleventh of April it was still at forty-six, and nobody in that shop and nobody in that building was going to be told that.

---

There is about one coat in thirty that comes back with something in the pocket, and a customer's property is not a bin, and the woman who brought the third one that morning was about sixty and had been coming to that shop since 2027 and stood at the counter and went through her coat and found a folded strip of paper about six inches by two, and she put it on the counter and said, "That is not mine."

He did not touch it.

"That is not mine either," said Roy Tarrant.

"Then it belongs to whoever left it in the pocket."

"It has been in that coat four years and I have never known whose coat that was, and I do not know how it got in there and neither do you, and that is the whole of what I know about it, and I am not going to tell you what it is, because you have not asked, and if you asked I would not know either."

The woman looked at the strip on the counter for a while and then she put it in her own bag.

"**Well, it is going home with me,**" she said. "**It is not much of a thing to throw away and there is no bin for it and there is no form for it and I am sixty-one and I have three bags.**"

"Yes."

"You are not going to look at it."

"I am not going to look at it," said Roy Tarrant, "**and I have got a box of about two hundred of them under that counter and I have never opened it, and I have told a man that in March and I have told it to about four people in four years and every one of them wanted it opened.**"

His wife came in at about ten to twelve with a bag from the market and she was fifty-eight and had been in the job book in a different hand for six years, and she put the bag down and saw the box on the counter and said, "Right," and then saw the page in the job book with the rubber band off and the pen on the counter where it was not, and she did not say anything about that either.

She came and stood beside him and looked at the top of the first clean page for about two seconds.

"That is your name," she said.

"That is my name."

"You have never—"

"No."

She looked at it for another two seconds and then she put the rubber band back on the book herself and pushed the book under the counter, and she said, "**Then I am not going to say anything about it either, and I am not going to be one of the two of us who is talking about it in a shop on a Thursday,**" and went through to the back with the market bag.

"**That is the only person in this city who has understood what I have been doing since January,**" said Roy Tarrant, "and she has not asked me a single question about it, and she is fifty-eight, and she has been putting a job number and a date and a street in that book for six years and not one name, and if you want to know why there are no names in a laundry's book then there is a woman in the back of this shop and she will not tell you."

Saint Orra Reach has a corridor behind a linen office with a fire door at one end and a window at the other that looks at a wall, and there is a laboratory in the middle of it with a bench down one side and four instruments on the bench and a plate in a slot four inches from a hard case that is not opened, and a brown envelope in the bench drawer with a consent strip in it that has not been read since the sixth of January, and a foil stencil in a box, and a log book with a rubber band, and a set of five informal lines written on a torn-off piece of something in a hand that is not a lawyer's.

Mara Quill was about thirty-five and had been on that bench since half past one and had a plate of toast on a mug that she had not eaten, and she did not say hello, and she turned round when the door shut and said, "You have been in four counties in nine days and you have not telephoned, and I have decided that is a decision and not a failing, and I am telling you now and I am not going to tell you again."

"You have been in a laboratory since January on a rule that came off the back of a delivery note."

"Two rules." She put the pencil down. "One: I do not work on an object in contact with a person who is still in a building. Two: you do not read the same object twice inside a month, and not at all if two readings disagree, and not on a night when you have been awake. **And both of them are on the back of a sheet of paper that a contract officer signed in a corridor because a stranger asked him for a fire door, and neither of them is a protocol, and we have got five lines and no document, and I have written to the only person in this city who could write one and she has not written back, and I have not chased her because chasing her would have made it her job.**"

"I have not come about the protocol."

"I know why you have come, and you are not going to say, and that is the second time in seven weeks." She turned the stool round. "**There is a piece of paper in your coat. It has been in your coat since February and I have watched you not tell me about it for seven weeks and I have watched you bring it into this building four times and put it in the other room, and I am not going to ask you what it is, and I want you to know that not asking is a decision and not a forgetting, and I have made it four times.**"

"I know."

"Do you."

"I am not going to tell her either," said Elias Venn.

Mara Quill looked at him for about four seconds and then she nodded, once, and went back to the bench, and neither of them said anything else about it, and it was the largest thing that had happened in that laboratory in six weeks and it had taken eleven seconds.

"**And the reason I am not asking is not a rule and it is not the protocol. It is that a man who has been refused four counters in six months has had about four things asked of him and about three of them were fair, and if I ask him the fifth then I am the fifth building, and he will tell me the smallest true thing, and he has been doing that all spring.**"

She pulled the stool out from the bench and sat down on it.

"I am going to work," she said. "And I am going to work on something that came out of a box in a building in Morrow Hill, and it is a month old, and it is not in contact with anybody, and I have checked twice. And you can sit on that end of the bench and you cannot touch anything, and if you start counting things in this room I will know, because you have told me twice that you do it and you have never once told me what it is for."

"There are four stools."

"There are four stools and they are four," said Mara Quill, "and that is a fact about a laboratory, and you have not written it down, and I noticed that you did not, and I am not going to ask you about the other thing, and you are not going to tell me, and we are going to work."

She turned the plate round so that the seam faced the window, and put two fingers on the edge of the glass, and did not touch it, and left it there for the length of a count that was not a number, and Elias Venn sat on the end of a bench in a laboratory behind a linen office at about twenty past two on a Thursday afternoon in April and did not tell her, and the bench drawer with a brown envelope in it was shut, and the hard case was shut, and the foil stencil was in its box, and the five informal lines on the torn-off piece of something were the only document in the building that either of them had ever written and they were not a document and they were not finished and the fifth one was about a person who was still somewhere in the city and could not be said about, and she would not write the fifth one down until there was somebody who could hold the other end of it, and there was not one, and they both knew it, and that was where the arc of the seventh month stopped, in a corridor, with two rules on the back of a delivery note and a man who could not tell her which building.
