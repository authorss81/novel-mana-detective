# Chapter 0131 — Nine Hundred People

The office was up a stair behind a garage on a back street off Calder Street, and the stair smelled of oil and hot dust, and there was a bell on the door that had been unscrewed and put in the same drawer as every other bell that has been unscrewed in this city.

She was thirty-eight and she had a screen and a telephone and a hardback book on a shelf behind her with a rubber band round it, and she said her name before he had got to the counter.

"Denise Crozier. We do not do enquiries by telephone, we do them at the counter, and if you have come about an old job you need the day and the street and you do not need anything else, because there is nothing else."

"Then it is the twenty-eighth of January," said Elias Venn, "and it is out of 2 Anselm Street in Kettle Rows."

She turned the screen about ten degrees, which hid nothing and showed nothing, and typed four characters and stopped.

"Who are you."

"Somebody who is not from this street."

"Then I can help with the street." She put her hands flat on the desk. "**The office takes about nine hundred jobs a year and I have been doing it since my father got tired, and I have worked out in about four years that there are exactly two things a person ever wants out of a removals firm, and one of them is their own goods and the other one is a receipt, and neither of them is where anybody went.**"

"I am not asking after anybody's goods."

"Then you are going to have to tell me what you are asking after, and I am going to have to tell you that I have never in four years been asked it, and those two sentences are the whole of what happens in this office when it is a Monday morning."

Elias Venn put his hands on the counter, which was a counter, and a counter is a thing in this city.

"Two men and a van on the twenty-eighth of January, out of a building in Kettle Rows, about three hours, paid in cash. I would like the name of the person who paid for it."

Denise Crozier unrubbered the book.

It was a bound book and not a pad, and she turned to the end of January with two fingers, and it was a Monday, and it was the twenty-eighth, and the whole of the line took her about two seconds to read and then about four to decide about.

"Two men," she said. "The small van. My father, and a lad called Reece who is not related to anybody in this building. Three hours, and they were in and out inside that, because the book says three hours and the book is written up at the end of the day by my father, who writes the hours up at the end of the day because he does not trust a clock in a house with furniture in it. **Cash. And he wrote *paid*, which means it was in his hand before he left, and he only ever writes *paid* when it is.**"

"And the payer."

"**The payer is on the line above the job and the payer is on every line, because I am not going to have a van go out and come back and not know who to ring.**" She turned the book round. "It says a name and it says a telephone number, and it does not say anything else, and I am going to read you the name because you have come to the counter and you have given me a day and a street, and because a booking page is a piece of business paper and a piece of business paper does not know what a person is."

It said **GAYNOR RIDDELL**.

He wrote it in his own book, in his own hand, on a line of its own, and it was the first time in four months that he had copied anything anybody had given him, and he was aware of the weight of it going into the pen.

"Thank you."

"You are welcome." She had not moved her hands. "**Now let me tell you the thing you have not asked me, because in four years nobody has asked me and I would like one person to have asked me, even late, even in a stair.** We do not write down where we take people. It is not a secret and it is not a policy. It is that a man moving his mother out of a house does not want the next tenant to have his mother's address, and so the destination goes on a card and the card goes in my father's wallet, and the wallet goes in his coat, and my father has never once in fifty years left a coat in a bus. **So there is a day and a street and two men and a van and the name of the person who paid, and nowhere in that line does it say where he went.**"

"How many people have you moved."

"About nine hundred." She said it the way a person says the price of fish. "**And I could not tell you where one of them went. Not one. Not the four hundred and something that my father did before me and not the nine hundred I have had on this book, and I have been asked in four years by two people, and both of them wanted an address for a reason that was their own and not mine.**"

"That is the answer to a question I have not asked yet."

"Then ask it properly and I will tell you whether I have got an answer."

There were three things in his head and he had been carrying all of them since the twenty-fifth of March. One was a description of a man that would be enough. One was nine words he had given in a chip shop on a Thursday in February, in a cold shop, to a man who was not sympathetic. The third was a Tuesday in April in which a woman behind a desk had told him he could not be in a building at all and had been entirely right.

"I am not going to ask you what the card says," he said.

Denise Crozier looked at him for the first time.

"No," she said. "**You are not, and I want to say something about that, because you are the first person in four years who has decided that on the stair.** A customer who gives us no name is a customer who has a reason, and the reason is theirs, and we are not the police, and we have never once been the police, and there is no version of this business where the office rings a man up and asks him where his belongings are. **If I did that I should lose the trade in about a year and I should deserve to.**"

"That is not what I meant."

"Then what did you mean."

"I meant that I know why there is no name on it, and I am not going to make you say it out loud in a room you pay rent on."

That got a nod. It was a small one.

"Where do the cards go," he said. "Not today. In a year. In five years."

"They go in the wallet with the rest of the day, and they get used maybe four times, and then they are pieces of card with a name on them." She said it without any weight, which was how she said everything. "**And then the wallet gets emptied on a Sunday night, because my father is a Sunday night man and he does the pockets, and the cards go in the bin at the bottom of the yard. And I have never once looked at one and I have never once been able to and I am telling you that as a fact about a business and not as a story about a lost man, because I have had two people in this office tell me a story about a lost man and one of them was a husband and one of them was a police officer, and both of them assumed the paper was somewhere.**"

"There is no paper."

"There is a **card**," said Denise Crozier, "and the card is in a wallet on a Sunday night, and the wallet is in a bin at the bottom of a yard, and if you want to go and look for it then it is a bin and it is a yard and it is about nine hundred cards' worth of nothing, and I would not know which bin and neither would he."

---

He put four credits on the counter, which was most of what he had that month, and she looked at them and did not ask and picked them up.

"Take the half hour," she said. "**And if you go out and stand in that yard you will get oil on a good coat, and there is nowhere in this building for a man to sit down, and I am not going to apologise for that either.**"

---

Gareth Prothero had two rooms on the first floor of a building off Anselm Street, over a tobacconist's, and he opened the door in a shirt with no sleeves and said that he was going to put a kettle on, and then said that he was not going to put a kettle on, and then put the kettle on.

He was forty-four and he had been a contract officer for eleven years and had done three contracts and the third of them had ended on Sunday the thirty-first of March, and he had not been paid since the Friday, and he had a folder on the table with about nine things in it, all of which were the same shape.

"You are the one who stood in the rain," he said. "Outside the Corrie Street rooms. On the ninth."

"You had a folder and no job."

"I had a folder and no job and I have had a folder and no job every day since the first of April and there is a difference between the two, and the difference is that on the ninth I had a fortnight of nothing in me and now I have got a fortnight of nothing in front of me." He put two mugs down and did not pour into either. "**You are not from the trust, because if you were from the trust you would not be here, and you are not a journalist, because a journalist would have a notebook out by now, and you are not from the council, because the council has never once in eleven years asked me a question I could answer in a room.**"

"I want to ask you one."

"Ask it."

"You put a schedule up in a hall in January with a post on it," said Elias Venn, "and the post was a night porter, and it said VACANT, and there was a date in February in the third column, and in March you were in that hall telling me about a contract that pays for furniture. **I did not ask you then what the date in the third column was. I am asking you now.**"

Prothero looked at the kettle for a while.

"Fourteenth of February," he said.

"That is nine days before the building was announced as closing, and a fortnight before anything happened."

"**It is nine days before the building was announced and it is the day the post became vacant in a resources office in a building in Kettle Rows, and those are two different buildings and two different facts and nobody has ever put them together in a piece of paper, including me, and I have had the sheet in a folder on this table since the first of April and I have looked at it every day.**" He opened the folder and turned it round. "**That is a notification. One side. Four boxes. A post, a date, a reference, and a heading that says SOURCE. And in the box that says SOURCE it does not say a name and it does not say a telephone number and it does not say a post. It says one word, and the word is EXTERNAL.**"

"Where did it come from."

"It came by telephone at about eleven in the morning, and a woman in a resources office I have met twice wrote it down while she was on the phone, which she told me afterwards and which I have thought about a great deal." Prothero put his finger on the word. "**She said it was not a letter and it was not a form, it was a person who rang the main number and asked for the resources office by name, and she did not ask who it was because people who ring a main number and ask for an office by name are, in her words, somebody's colleague, and you do not ask a colleague their name.**"

"Did she know that they were not?"

"She had no way of knowing either. **That is what EXTERNAL is. It is a box for a person she could not name and would not ask and did not need to name, because the word told her everything she needed to know, which was that it was not us and it was not the council and it was not a shop, and that the post was to be treated as gone from the second of February and the building as a building that had lost something rather than as a building that was going to close.**"

"And it was the only one in four months."

"It was the only one." He shut the folder. "**And I am going to tell you the whole of what I think about it, because you have not put a notebook out and because you asked me in the right order, which is that you started with the column and not with the man.**"

"I do not know the man."

"Then it is better that you do not, because in about seven weeks a solicitor from the trust is going to ring me and ask whether I gave a document to a member of the public, and I am going to have to say yes, and I have had eleven years of saying no to things and about eleven days of finding out what it is like to say yes to one."

He put the notification on the table between them. It was a single side of A4 and the printing on it was four years old and there was a staple in the corner that had been there since it was filed.

"I am giving you that because it is the trust's own form and it was completed by a person in a resources office off a telephone call and there is no privilege in it and there is nobody in it, and I have kept it because it was in my folder and my folder was mine. **It is not the closure schedule and I am not giving you the closure schedule, and there are forty-one lines in that and forty-one is the number of the building and not the number of anything, and I have watched two people this month come into this room and start counting.**"

Elias Venn did not count anything.

"You said the source was external," he said, "and the office was in Kettle Rows, and the word tells you that the person was somebody's colleague. What I cannot work out is what the person thought they were doing."

"Neither can I." Prothero poured the tea at last, into both mugs, and gave him one. "**I have been sitting in two rooms over a tobacconist's for eleven days with a folder and a kettle and I have worked out that whoever rang that office believed they were being helpful, and that is the part I have got stuck on, because a person who is being helpful does not ask a resources office to remove a post in the middle of a contract without saying a reason.**"

"A reason in words."

"**There is nowhere in this business to put a reason in words.** That is what I have got stuck on. I have been a contract officer for eleven years and I have never once been asked why a thing was decided. I asked once, in about 2019, and a woman in an office in Kettle Rows explained to me for about four minutes why the reason did not matter to me, and she was right, and I have thought about those four minutes every March since."

The folder was on the table and the notification was on the folder, and Elias Venn did not touch either, and then he did, because it was not anybody's document in any sense that this city recognises, and because a piece of paper with a post on it and the word EXTERNAL in a box is a document, and the only document in four months that has come out of this case by being handed over.

He put it in the folder inside his coat, which made six things in that pocket, and he did not count them.

---

He went back up the stair off Calder Street at about ten past four to say thank you, and the office was shut and the light off and the booking book on its shelf with the rubber band round it.

Through the glass there was a second-hand calendar on the wall, and it was a wall of small squares with a day on each, and on each day there were two or three numbers in the boxes, and once or twice a week there were six.

In four days there was a strip about two inches wide where the squares were blank.

He put his face closer to the glass to be certain. It was. Four days in the middle of a month, cut with a blade along a ruled line and lifted out and gone, and the numbers that had been on them taken with them, and the squares either side of the cut had been dated in a different pen, because a man dating a calendar that has been cut into does not go back and re-date the ones he has left.

He stood there for about a minute working out what that was.

A man who keeps a calendar cannot throw a page away, because a page is the only thing in the business that says a job was on it, and a booking book with a day cut out of the calendar beside it is a day that is only in one place. So the man who owns the business cuts it out, and takes it, and puts it in a coat pocket, and re-dates the squares either side so that the gap is a gap and not a mistake.

And a man who cuts four days out of his own calendar and re-dates the days either side of them is a man who has decided, more than once, that there is a set of days in this city that belongs to somebody else and not to him.

It was a Monday in the office, and the job on it had been a Monday, and the man in the coat was a night porter, and Elias Venn stood in a yard off Calder Street and found that he had nothing whatever to say about any of it, which was the first time in four months.
