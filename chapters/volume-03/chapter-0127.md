# Chapter 0127 — A Tray Four Feet From a Door

The registered premises was on the first floor of a building with a shop at the ground floor and a letting agent's board in the window of it, and the stairs went up past a door that was open, and the door that was open was a door anybody could walk through, and there was a bell on it that had been unscrewed and put in a drawer.

The letter had come that morning, and it was three weeks to the day since it had gone into a box on a forecourt. He had it in his inside pocket in the inside pocket of a coat that had five things in its inside pockets, and there was an envelope inside the letter and the envelope had nine credits in it in coins and a slip of paper.

He read the slip first, standing on the landing, because the letter was four pages and the slip was the part that had been done on purpose by somebody who wanted him to find it without having to ask.

> *Sir,*
>
> *We hold one document which names the building you have given us and a date in the second half of 2029. It is a lease of the premises, dated 14 August 2029, and it is four pages including the schedule. A copy is enclosed. Our search and copy fee was two credits and you enclosed eleven, so the balance of nine is returned with this letter. No further sum is due and you do not need to do anything else.*
>
> *If you require anything further about this building you may write to this address and it will reach us.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> *for the Company Secretary*

There was a name under that, and it was a woman's, and it was not the Company Secretary's.

---

"Can I help you."

Elias Venn had a folder in his hand and a person behind a desk on the other side of a room with two desks in it and a window that gave on to a stairwell.

"You did a letter for me. It went to a company and this is the answer and I would like to ask you one thing about it and I am not going to pretend the thing is fair."

The woman was about thirty-four. She had a screen and a tray with about forty other envelopes in it and a second tray on the floor with more in it, and the tray on the desk was about four feet from the door.

"Then ask it."

"You are not the company."

"No."

"You work here."

"**For a firm of accountants, and there are nine of us, and we are the registered office of about forty companies, and about thirty of those forty have never been through this door, and one of them wrote you a four-page letter with nine coins in an envelope about a building.**" She turned the screen about thirty degrees, which was not hiding anything and was not showing anything either, and it was the movement of a person who has been in an office for eleven years. "I opened it on the Friday the twenty-second. I took it to the client, because that is what the tray is for. The client read it and the client's own solicitor read it and it came back on the Monday with an instruction, and I typed it out on the Tuesday, which was the twenty-sixth, and the boss signed it, and it went in the post on the Friday the twenty-ninth, and it came this morning, and that is the whole of my involvement in it and I have typed about nine hundred letters of this shape."

"Then I will not waste your time. Did you decide that the building was anybody's problem."

"The company decided that, not me." She said it without any weight at all. "**And I have to tell you that I have been at this desk for eleven years and you are the first person who has come up those stairs and asked me that, and everybody else who comes up those stairs asks me what the fee is.**"

"The fee was two credits and nine came back in an envelope with a slip that told me I did not need to do anything else. Somebody wrote that slip on purpose."

"A solicitor wrote that slip," said the woman. "Not the company and not me. **The slip is the only part of that letter that a person wrote. The other four pages are a company doing what it was asked, and there is a difference and you can see it if you look at it, because a company that has decided a building is somebody's problem writes like a company and a person who has decided a building is somebody's problem writes a sentence with a full stop at the end of it that is not about the company.**"

Elias Venn put the four pages on the desk and opened them on the second one.

The lease was a lease. It was a ground floor and a first floor and a hall in a building in Kettle Rows, it was four pages long, and it named an address and a date and two parties and a term of fifteen years and a schedule of condition in small print, and there was nothing in it about a night, and there was nothing in it about a man, and it was the most useful piece of paper he had read in six months.

The second page had eleven lines in it and nine of them were about drains and roofs and the keeping of a garden and the two that were not were at the top, in the middle of a list of what the landlord provides, and one of the two said that the fittings and the floor coverings and the curtains were the tenant's and the tenant's at the end of the term, and the other one said:

> *The hall, the stair and the office at the premises are available to any person entering the building at any hour. No restriction is imposed by this lease on the hours of entry or exit, and no key is required at any hour.*

"That is a landlord's clause," said the woman at the desk. "**I have read about nine hundred leases and that is in every one of them and it is the single most useful line in the whole of this document and I have never once known anybody read it.**"

"It says there is no key at any hour."

"It says there is no *requirement* for a key at any hour, and the difference is the whole of what landlords are, and the effect is that anybody can walk into that building at four in the morning and there is nothing anybody could do about it and nobody would want to." She turned her screen a little further away. "So a man who works nights in that building has a building he can walk out of at any hour of the night and go round the front, and there is a van that goes on Fridays, and there is a hardback log with a fifth column in it, and none of that is on this piece of paper, and all of it is on this piece of paper, and that is the trouble with leases."

**"Why are you telling me this. You are an accountant's clerk and I have bought nothing from you."**

"Because in eleven years you are the fourth person who has come up that stair and read something out loud to me, and two of the other three read a mistake in a set of accounts and one of them read a name, and you have read a clause about a door, and I am going to be a woman of sixty in this office and there is not going to be a fifth." She put her pen down. "**And I am aware that is not a professional reason and I have already decided to do it anyway, and you may put it down to a Tuesday.**"

Page four was the schedule.

It was a single column headed **SERVICE PREMISES** and it ran down the page in a typewriter hand, and the second line of it read:

> *2 Anselm Street, Kettle Rows — residential, contracted*

and the ninth line of it read:

> *2 Wexford Row — day services, associated provider*

and there was a number in a box at the end of that line, and it was a provider's registration number, and it was the shape of the number that a provider has and not the shape of the number that anything else in this case has had.

At the foot of the column, in the same hand, there were two lines:

> *Premises at which a service is provided by an associated provider are listed above. Such premises are not the registered premises of the company. The company does not hold a schedule of the operations of an associated provider and will not provide one.*
>
> *We have not been asked about the two of them together before. We have not said they are the same. The schedule above is a list of addresses.*

"**That last part is a person,**" said Elias Venn, "and not the company."

"That last part is a person who has been in this building for nineteen years and who has a list of forty companies and who reads all of them," said the woman, "and who has never in nineteen years been asked for a schedule, and who put the last three lines in on her own because you asked about a building and she thought you ought to have all of it, including the parts that do not help you."

"And the number in the box."

"That is a provider's registration number and it is on a public register and it has been on a public register for about four years and I have looked it up myself, at that screen, about three weeks ago, for no reason at all, because I have a habit." She said it flatly. "**It is not a name and it does not get you a document and it will not get you anything this morning. It is a number, and a number is what your whole case has been made of, and I have read your letter twice and I have worked that out from one sentence in it and I would rather you did not tell me anything else.**"

"I have not told you anything."

"You have not told me anything," said the woman, "and I have read a letter about a building, and I have told you what a company holds and what it does not, and if you come back in June with a different question I will take it to the client and it will go round again, and it will take a fortnight, and whatever it is will have happened by then, and that is not me being obstructive. **That is where post goes. There is a tray four feet from a door that anybody can walk through and there has never been a camera on that tray in this city and there is not going to be one, because a tray is not a room.**"

---

He sat down on the stairs, which were the only place to sit, and took out his own book, and did not write the registration number down, because a number a man has been given by a stranger in an office is a number and not a document, and because a provider's registration number is the fourth kind of number he had collected in six months and none of them had ever once been the thing he needed, and because in March a woman of fifty-four behind a counter in Morrow Hill had told him that the only paper in this business that said anything about a night was the acts, and that the acts were four miles away in a payer's drawer, and that the field he was holding was a fact about an office.

He wrote two lines and closed the book, and the stairwell was going on being a stairwell with the letting agent's board opposite the bottom window.

> *Tuesday 2 April. The answer is about a building. It is four pages of a lease dated the fourteenth of August 2029 and on page four there is one column with two addresses in it and a number in a box at the end of the second one, and a person has written at the foot of the page that they have not been asked about the two of them together before and have not said they are the same.*
>
> *And on page two, in a list of what a landlord provides, a sentence saying that no key is required at any hour.*

He sat on the stairs for another few minutes with the folder on his knees and worked out that he had bought a sentence about a door, and that the sentence had been in a list of eleven lines between one about a garden and one about floor coverings, and that it had been printed there since the fourteenth of August 2029 by somebody who was not thinking about him, and that if he had put a man's name on the paper instead of a building's on the twelfth of March then this would have gone out through a solicitor's name at the top of it and would have taken about nine weeks instead of three, and that in nine weeks the trust's contract would have been over and the last bed would have been out of the building on Anselm Street and he would have had the same four pages in his hand six weeks later with a hardback book already full.

"You are still there," said the woman from the desk.

"I have been sitting here for about four minutes working out how much time I spent on a letter," said Elias Venn. "**That is not your fault. It is the fault of a rule that says a paper about a man goes through a solicitor and a paper about a building does not, and that rule is the only reason I have anything at all.**"

"It is not a rule," said the woman. "It is a habit. And habits are cheaper than rules and that is why they last and that is why they are not in any book."

"Can I ask you one more thing and get a straight no."

"You have been getting straight noes off me for twenty minutes. Ask it."

"**If I wrote to them again in my own name, on my own paper, with my own address at the bottom, about the other address — the ninth line on page four — how long would that take.**"

She did not pretend to think about it.

"**Nine weeks,**" said the woman. "And you would pay for it, and you would get an answer, and I have watched it happen twice. The first time a stranger writes to a company about a building, the company has to find out whether the building is anybody's problem, and it is a question and it gets a question. **The second time a stranger writes to the same company about the second building on the same schedule, it is no longer a question about a building, it is a question about whether the two are connected, and that is a question about a relationship, and a company can answer a question about a building in three weeks and cannot answer a question about a relationship at all, because the answer is that it is not telling you, and it will say so politely in nine weeks.**"

"And you would still put it in the tray."

"I would still put it in the tray," said the woman, "and I would watch it come back in nine weeks with nine words on it, and in those nine weeks I would have to keep working in a room with the person who sent it, and that is the part nobody thinks about when they decide to write a second letter. **The tray is not the cost. The tray is four feet from a door and the cost is that I have to sit here.**"

Elias Venn folded the four pages back into the folder and squared them, and did not ask her a third thing, because she had told him the price of the third thing before he had asked it and had not made him pay for finding out.

He went back down the stairs past the door that was open and out into a parade, and the shop at the ground floor was letting a unit with a card in the window, and he stood on the pavement for a minute with a folder in his hand, and the folder had a lease in it, and the nine credits had gone into the till in the back room at 3 Tanners Row before half past ten that morning, and that till had stood at fifty since the twelfth of January, less eleven into a post box on the twelfth of March, and it said forty-eight, and there was not one thing in the folder about the ninth of December 2029, and there was not one thing in the folder about a person, and the whole of what he had done for nine days had been to buy four pages that said a building was a building and that no key was required at any hour of the night.

Then he went back to Tanners Row and bought two credits' worth of a stamp, because a lease in a folder in an inside pocket four miles from a car park in Kettle Rows was a thing that got wet, and because a man who has decided to be the man who keeps things dry is not the same as a man who has decided anything else, and because it was Tuesday and there were six days until a review.
