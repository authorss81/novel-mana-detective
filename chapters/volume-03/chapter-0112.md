# Chapter 0112 — Somebody With a Standing to Ask

The car park behind the building on Verge Row had four bays and a wall with a bay number stencilled on it and a man of about sixty reversing a van with a light on and no hazard lights, and Elias Venn had asked for that bay number on the telephone on Friday and had been given it by a woman who said it as though she were reading it out of a book, and he had written it down, and the writing down had not helped at all.

Nadia Kest came out at five past nine in a plain coat with her hair down, which he had seen twice in five months and both times had been because somebody had died or somebody had been discharged.

"Nadia Kest. Tuesday the twelfth of March. I have found out that there is one road into a claim number in this city and it is a person with standing to ask."

"I cannot be that person." She said it before he had finished and she said it the way a woman says the price of something in a shop. "That is four sentences and I have used two of them and I am going to use the other two now because you have come to a car park and not to a corridor, and I have decided to count that."

"Go on."

"**The claim is not evidence. I have no standing in it, and nobody in this building has standing in it, and if a police force writes to a payer about a claim number then that payer knows on the day it arrives that this force is interested in that claim, and from that day the claim is a different claim and everything in it is a different claim, and there is no version of me that is a smaller version of that.**" She shut the car door and did not lock it. "So the police can do it for you exactly once, in a room, with an instrument in her hand, and never on paper, and if she puts it on paper it is a disclosure and I will not be the one holding the pen. That is the whole of what I have got and I am not going to dress it up."

"You told me to ask somebody with a warrant and a good coat."

"I told you to ask somebody. I did not tell you it was going to be easy and I did not tell you it was going to be me, and if it turns out to be me then I will do it and it will be the wrong thing for both of us, and I have spent twenty-two years finding out which of those two things I was going to do about half an hour before I did it."

"There is a third one."

"There are three and I know two of them and you have found the third, and go on, because I have to be at a building at half past ten."

"A letter. In my own name, to a company, at its registered premises, asking it for a document it holds about a premises and a date."

Nadia Kest stood still in a car park with a van indicator going behind her.

"**Yes,**" she said, "and that is the one, and I want to tell you the three things about it and then I am going to leave, because I have got a room at half past ten and because you have got a face on you that means you already know one of the three and you have not admitted it yet."

"Go on."

"It costs money. It takes about three weeks, because a company has three weeks to answer correspondence and that is not a target anybody in that building has ever been given. And **it cannot be expedited by anybody, including me, because expediting a request tells a company that somebody wants to know, and the second that a company knows that, the request stops being a letter and starts being a thing that is being chased.**" She looked at the stencilled bay number on the wall as though she had never seen it. "And that is the only one of the three that does not use a single person's standing. Not mine. Not a sergeant's. Not a doctor's. It uses a man's own name at the foot of his own handwriting, and the price of it is that when it is answered, or when it is refused, it is answered to *him*, and there is nobody in that exchange who can be leaned on and nothing in it that another person can use without asking him first. **That is why it is the one you should do and it is the only one I cannot help you with, and I have thought about that for two days and I have decided that it is not a failure of mine.**"

"That is a long way round for saying you cannot help me."

"It is four minutes further than saying I cannot help you," said Nadia Kest, "and the four minutes are yours, and I would rather you had them in a car park than at my desk, and now I am going to ask you one thing and I want you to hear that I am asking it and not checking."

"Go on."

"On the Wednesday you rang me and said four words, and I did not ask you one further question about them, and I told you that I would not as long as I live, and I have not." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "**If a person is somewhere I could reach, then tell me that he is, and do not tell me where, and I will go on not knowing where, and that is not a promise I make easily and it is not a favour to you, it is because a man who will not tell me where a person is has told me something about himself and I would rather have that than have a name.**"

The van behind them finished reversing and put its light on and took it off again.

Elias Venn looked at the tarmac between his boots.

"There is nobody to reach," he said.

"All right," said Nadia Kest.

And she got into the car, and she did not say *I did not believe you* and she did not say *that is the fifth time*, and she put the mirror on and said "get the number back if it does not come by Friday" and drove off, and the whole of it took about four seconds, and it was the first time in five months that he had been in a conversation with that woman and had not lied to her, and the reason he had got away with it was that he had said nothing at all, and he stood in the car park for about a minute afterwards working out that those two things are not the same thing and that one of them is a decision and the other one is a habit.

---

He had got there by a bus and he had forty minutes and he spent them in a café that charged eleven credits for something he did not order, and then he walked up Verge Row to number six, which is a building with no name over the door, and a counter on the ground floor, and a woman of forty-four who says general questions are free before a member of the public has got his mouth open.

"You have come to ask me one and you have brought it written on a bit of paper, which means you are frightened of getting it wrong, and I am going to tell you to say it out loud anyway."

"Maureen Skelland. Tuesday the twelfth. I want to know who is allowed to ask a payer for a claim number, and I have been told that the police cannot and that a company can be written to, and I would like to know whether there is a form."

"**A person is entitled to ask about a claim that concerns them,**" said Maureen Skelland. "**And a person is not entitled to ask about a claim that concerns an account.** And you are the second and not the first, and there is no form that makes you the first, and I have looked for one, and I did not look quickly."

"That is what I have been told twice this week by two people who do not know each other."

"Then it is true, and I will tell you how I know it is true, because it is not because of a rule. It is because I have a tray of letters on that wall that people have written to this counter asking after accounts that are not theirs, and every single one of those letters has been answered, and every single one of those answers has said *we are unable to discuss an account that is not yours*, and it is a good answer and it is the right answer and it has been given about four thousand times." She turned round and looked at the wall, which was what she had been looking at for eleven years. "The trouble is not that we cannot answer you. The trouble is that about one letter in a hundred comes in asking about a *place*."

"A place."

"A street, a building, a ward, a room, a night, a date." She turned back. "And a company can answer a question about a place without telling anybody anything about an account, and it has to answer it, because a company that operates a building is the only party in this that has ever held a document about that building that is not a document about a person. And I will tell you the thing nobody tells you, which is that **nobody in this city ever thinks of it that way, because everybody thinks of a building as a where a person was, and it is not, it is a where a person was and also the only party in the whole arrangement that cannot be a patient and therefore cannot be frightened and therefore cannot be refused.**"

"So I write to the company that runs the building."

"You write to a company at its registered premises and you ask it for a document it holds about a place, and you put the place on the paper and you put your own name at the bottom, and you do not put a man's name on it, and you do not put a date that means a Tuesday, and you do not put anything that anybody has to be frightened of, and I want to say one more thing about that and then I will let you go because there is a queue."

"Go on."

"The absence is the document." Maureen Skelland said it flatly, the way she said the other things. "**If you name a man on that paper then the paper is about a man, and a company will answer a question about a man through a solicitor and in about nine weeks. If you name a building then the paper is about a building, and a company will answer a question about a building in about three weeks, and it will send the answer to you and not to a lawyer, because there is no lawyer in it.** And the reason for that is not kindness. It is that a company which answers a stranger about a building has not admitted that the building is anybody's problem, and the first thing it has to do is find out whether it is."

"And when the letter arrives?"

"It arrives at a registered premises, and a registered premises is not a building, it is an address that a company has given to a government so that letters have somewhere to arrive." She turned and pulled a card out of the rack on the wall behind her and read two lines of it and put it back without giving it to him. "**A registered premises can be an accountant's office on a floor above a shop. It can be a solicitor who has never heard of the company. And about one in three of the care companies in this city has one that is an agent, and the agent's job is to receive post and put it in a tray, and the tray is four feet from a door that anybody can walk through, and nobody has ever put a camera on a tray in this city because a tray is not a room.** That is not a scandal and it is not a loophole I am offering you. It is just where the paper goes, and if you write to a building you may be writing to a person, and if you write to a person you are writing to a building, and you do not get to choose and neither do they."

Elias Venn stood at that counter for a moment with the pen in his hand.

"That is the third time this week somebody has given me a way round a door by telling me the door is not a door."

"They are not doors, they are *routes*," said Maureen Skelland, "and a route is made out of people, and there are only about nine of them in this city and you have met three, and the other six are worse than the first three."

---

The forecourt of 6 Verge Row had a bench under a plane tree that had been planted before either of the buildings, and at two o'clock in the afternoon on the twelfth of March the sun came onto it from the side that made the back of his hand warm, and he sat on it and took the notebook out and turned to a clean page, and he wrote a heading at the top.

> *Civilian letter of request. My own name. My own hand.*

Then he stopped, and put the pen down on his knee, and worked out what he had to leave off.

He had known since the car park and he had been carrying it round for two hours as a sort of warmth.

The letter had to ask a company for a document. Documents are about things that happened. Things that happened are done to people. If he put the name on it, the paper became a request about a man, and a company that answered it would be answering about a man, and somewhere in that building a woman would read the name and go and look in a file with the name on it, and he would have taken a man who works nights and has asked one thing of him in a chip shop and put him into a building he has spent eleven weeks not being in. And if he left the name off, the paper became a question about a wall, and the answer to it, if there was an answer, was an answer about a wall.

He said it out loud on the bench, to the plane tree, because it needed saying and there was nobody else there.

"**I am not going to put his name on it.**"

Then he said it a second time, differently, the way he checked a door.

"There is a man who works nights in a building in Kettle Rows, and I have promised him in nine words that nobody is to be told where he is, and it is now the fifteenth day of that promise, and I have told nobody, and I am writing this letter to a company about a building and not about a man, and the omission of his name is the whole of the point of the letter, and if anybody ever asks me why I did not put a man's name on a piece of paper when I had it, this is why, and it is a better reason than the law and it is the only one I have got."

He picked the pen up.

The letter took him about forty minutes and it is in a file somewhere and nobody has ever read it except the woman who opened it, and this is the whole of it.

> *To the Company Secretary*
>
> *I am a member of the public. I am writing to ask whether you hold a document that names a building, 2 Anselm Street, Kettle Rows, and a date in the second half of 2029, and if you do, I would like a copy of it, and I am willing to pay the fee for the search and the fee for the copy in advance.*
>
> *I am not asking about any individual. I am asking about a building, and if the answer is that you hold nothing about that building, then that is an answer and I will be glad to have it.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*
>
> ***Elias Venn***
>
> *3 Tanners Row*

He read it through once and did not change anything. Eleven credits went into the envelope with it, in coins, counted twice, out of fifty credits in a till on a Monday night, and the stamp came out of the same envelope and the envelope went into the box on the wall of the forecourt of 6 Verge Row at about half past two in the afternoon, and the box was red and the flap was rusted and it took the envelope at an angle.

The clock on the building across the road said twenty to three. Three weeks from the twelfth of March is the second of April, and the letter would be answered or not answered somewhere in the second week of April, and the second week of April is inside Arc C, and he wrote that in his own book on the bench, in his own hand, with the building on it and the man's name not on it, and then he sat for another minute because the sun was on the back of his hand and there was nothing at all else to do.
