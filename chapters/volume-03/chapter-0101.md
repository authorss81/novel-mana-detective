# Chapter 0101 — The Twenty-Eighth Day

The path up to the clinic had a rill across it about two thirds of the way and Elias Venn went round it the way he had gone round it since September, and there was a length of tape on the door of the Kettle Rows Community Clinic at about the height of a person's hand with two drawing pins in the top corners of it, and the tape was holding a piece of paper that said the order was in force from the twenty-third of January 2030 until the twentieth of February 2030, and the word *temporary* was in a heavier face than the rest of it, and a registration number was at the bottom in a hand he had read four times.

He had counted the days without meaning to, which is a thing that happens to people who are not expecting anything.

Inside, the waiting room had nine chairs in it. He counted them on the step, before he went in, and he counted them again from the inside, and it was nine both times, and it was not eleven, and he was aware of the number the whole time he was in the room.

The board behind the polythene at the top of the fan carried three kinds of paper where it had carried four. The sheet public since the twenty-seventh of November was still at the top of it on four drawing pins of his own and could not be un-published. The four sheets in a doctor's ink were where they had always been. The registrar's transfer order was in its clear sleeve with its pins in and a bent corner. The clean rectangle about nine inches by eleven and two pin holes was still there and nothing was in it.

The fourth kind of paper was on the door, and it was the door, and he stood in the middle of a waiting room at twenty to nine in the morning on the twenty-eighth day and understood that he had come to a building to look at a piece of paper and that the piece of paper had already been looked at, because she was standing in the doorway of the back room with a pen behind her ear and a mug in her hand and she had been up since six.

"You have come on the right day," said Sana Okafor. "Do not tell me you have counted."

"I counted on the step."

"Everybody counts on the step." She put the mug down on the counter. "Come and look at it in the office, because there are two people in this room who do not need to hear what a doctor says about a piece of paper, and one of them is you."

---

The back office had a window that gave on to a brick wall about four feet away, and a desk, and a drawer, and a board on the wall that was not the board, and on the desk there was a single sheet of paper with a stamp in the bottom right-hand corner of it and the stamp was a rubber stamp and the word in it was **EXPIRED**.

"Read that out," said Sana Okafor.

"It says expired."

"It says expired, and it is dated the nineteenth, and it is not the word I was afraid of." She turned it round so it was the right way up for him. "In January the first one of these died and the word was *lapsed*, and a clerk of about fifty-five on a nineteen-year rota writes *lapsed* in a box on the day it ends when nobody has done anything about it, and I sat in this building for five weeks waiting for a box to be filled in with that word. **This one says expired, and expired means somebody looked at it.** There is a line under it and the line has a date on it and the date is the nineteenth of February, and there is a second line under that, and the second line says the applicant may apply again."

"You applied again."

"I telephoned on the nineteenth and spoke to a woman who was very pleasant and who read me three paragraphs of a document that I could have read myself, and she told me that a fresh application is a different document from an order, and that a fresh application needs a fresh set of premises, and that a fresh set of premises needs a fresh set of reasons, and that a fresh set of reasons cannot be the reason I gave in January because the reason I gave in January was a room on a parade four streets away and I have not got that room any more and I am not going to be able to describe it to a magistrate in a way that is useful to anybody." She said the last part without any weight on it at all, which is how a person says the part they have practised. "So: twenty-eight days, and somebody looked at it on the nineteenth, and I have four premises on a list and no instrument for any of them, and that is the whole of the twenty-eighth day."

"Which four."

She turned the sheet over and there was a second sheet under it, and she had written on it herself in a hard hand on a Sunday, because she is that sort of woman, and it read:

> 1. 14 Kettle Rows (ground, shop and first floor rear) — the building, not a person.
> 2. 9 Calder Place, first floor — a parade. A man with a set of keys. Under a notice since the twenty-second of January.
> 3. 4 Corrie Street, first floor — a landing of eight rooms. Two doors opened on the eighteenth.
> 4. 3 Wendle Street, ground — not mine, not anybody's on that wall, and it is a laundry and it has been a laundry since 2026.

"Number one is this building," she said. "Number two is a room over a shop that is not the room we went into, and I want that said properly, because the man in that shop was charged on the twenty-third and the room that was worked on is the first floor of that building and I have never been on it and did not know it existed until January. Number three is a landing. Number four is a laundry and I cannot tell you why it is on a list of premises that somebody walked round in December, and the clerk at Vine Street could not tell me either, and she was extremely fair about it."

"What happened on the nineteenth."

"Nothing happened on the nineteenth," said Sana Okafor. "A woman read three paragraphs to me. That is what happened on the nineteenth. What I want to tell you, and I am telling you because you are the person who remembers dates, is this. **Two officers came into eleven rooms on the twenty-second of January because of a piece of paper. On the twenty-eighth day of the life of that paper there were no officers, and there was no paper, and there is a man on a parade who does keys and a landlord who has a set of keys on a belt and about two hundred Tuesdays coming up in about seven weeks.** I have looked at it. I have done the looking. And what I have found is that a date with an end in it buys you the looking, and nothing else at all, and I would not go back to a board without an end on it, and I am not going to have a fourth month of a wall that nothing comes back to."

She picked the sheet up, folded it once, and put it in the drawer, and shut the drawer.

"There," she said. "It is in a drawer, and the drawer is a drawer, and I have kept it, and if you tell anybody in this city that I have kept it then within a month there will be a person here who wants it."

---

The shop at 9 Calder Place sold cleaning things and did keys, and it had a back room with a radiator in it and a window that looked at a wall four feet away, and the man behind the counter was twenty-five years old and had been in that building four years and had never been given a key to the rooms above it and had never asked for one and had told two people in the last two months that he had never been given one.

"You have come about upstairs," said Kieran Slack.

"No," said Elias Venn. "I have come about the twenty-eighth of February."

"That is upstairs."

"Yes," he said. "That is upstairs."

The shop had a bell on the door and a till tray that did not need squaring and a rack of keys with about ninety on it, and there was a man of about thirty-one at the end of the counter in a coat putting a fob into a bag and being very slow about it.

"I have not been up," said Kieran Slack.

"I am not asking whether you have been up."

"Everybody asks whether I have been up." The young man put the fob down. "The first gentleman in January asked whether I had been up and then asked what was up there and I told him I have never been up there and I have never had a key, and he wrote that down, which I did not enjoy." He put his hands flat on the counter. "There is a notice on the first floor door. It has been on it since the twenty-third of January. It is a piece of A4 in a plastic wallet and it says DO NOT ENTER and it does not have anybody's name on it or anybody's business on it, and I have looked at it about four hundred times, and I have never opened it."

"How many times has he come past the shop."

"Who."

"The man with the keys."

Kieran Slack did not answer straight away, and he looked at the rack of keys, which is a thing a man does when he is deciding whether the number he is about to say is his to say.

"About one hundred and forty," he said. "I have kept the count and I have never told anybody and I have never told anybody because it is my shop and it is my count and nobody has ever asked a man behind this counter a question about a Tuesday in his life." He looked up. "You are the first."

"What time does he come."

"About ten to five. He has a set on a ring on his belt. He does not look at the shop, and he has not looked at the shop once in four years that I have noticed, and I have watched him every one of them."

"Does he go in?"

"He goes up. He comes down. That is all of it."

"Then what is there to tell anybody," said Elias Venn, "and I am asking you properly and I will take a no."

Kieran Slack considered that for long enough that the man at the end of the counter got his bag and left and the bell went and neither of them looked at it.

"There is a man in this city," he said, "who is a landlord and a practitioner and a man who has a set of keys, and he has decided not to know what his room is for, and in four years he has not once gone out of that building on a Tuesday, and everybody in this street knows it, and nobody in this street has ever had a reason to say it out loud." He squared the till tray that did not need squaring. "If somebody came in here tomorrow with a form, I would like there to be a man behind this counter who could say that."

"That is not evidence of anything."

"I know it is not evidence of anything," said Kieran Slack. "That is why I have been keeping it for a month and why I have not slept properly since Friday, and it is you I am telling and you have got a good coat on and I do not think you are the police."

---

The front office of a police station on Corbie Street had a counter, a bell, three chairs bolted down and a rack of leaflets about lost property, and there was a woman of thirty-three behind it who had been doing it for nine years and who told him, before he had got to the counter, that general questions were free and that a member of the public got one.

"That is a rule?"

"It is *my* rule," said Petra Lund, "and it is not a police rule, and it exists because in nine years a man came in here on a Friday and asked me a specific question about a specific person and I said no, and he went away and did it himself, badly, and about nine months later there was a woman on the front page of the evening paper who was not a criminal, and every time I have thought about it for nine years what I have thought about it is that he asked me the wrong question and I gave him nothing, and the two of those together is what made her."

"Fair." He took the pen off the string it hung from. "How does a person withdraw an account they have given to the police."

She typed nothing, because there was no terminal in front of her; she wrote the time on the top of a pad and then turned the pad round so he could see it, which is a thing she had clearly decided to do before he asked.

"Right," she said. "That is a general question and it is the right one, and I am going to give you the whole of it and then I am going to stop." She ticked them off on her finger. "It is done on a form. **WDS one, withdrawal of statement, four pages, and the one everybody asks for is page two.** It has to be signed by the person giving it, in the building, in front of a supervisor, and the supervisor signs it too, and it is not a telephone call and it is not a letter, and the reason it is not a letter is that a letter cannot be countersigned."

"Can it be done at any station."

"It can be done at any station. It does not have to be the station the statement was taken at, and it does not have to be the same force, and it does not have to be a particular day." She looked at him. "It can be done about eleven minutes' walk from where you are standing, in a room with a table in it, and it takes about four minutes, and people do it, and I have issued nine of them in nine years and four of those people were crying and one of them was not."

"And what happens to the account."

"**It is superseded.** It stays in the file. It stays in the file for the life of the file, and anybody who reads the file afterwards will see both, and there is a box on the form, and the box is ticked before you get to it."

Petra Lund stopped, and turned the pad round again, and drew a line under what she had written, and looked at him to make sure he had it.

"You have not asked me what happens to the certificate," she said.

"You have not told me about a certificate."

"That is right, and it is because a certificate is not a document this force holds." She said it evenly, the way a person says a thing that is a rule and is also an opinion. "A dangerous-working certificate is issued by the Proof Registry at Harrow Lane, on a fee, and it is a public document, and this force cannot withdraw one, cannot suspend one and has never in nine years been asked to. The statement is ours. The certificate is theirs. Two buildings, one street apart, one a form with a tick in it and the other a fee and a counter and a signature, and the law has never once had to say which of them matters more, and I have been at this counter for nine years and I have watched about four hundred people come through that door and **not one of them has ever asked me both questions in the same breath, and you are the first, and it is the only reason I have gone on talking.**"

He wrote it down in his own book, standing at the counter, with the time and the date at the top and the word *Corbie* and the name of the counter and nothing else, and he turned the pad so that she could see the page, which he did not have to do, and which she looked at.

"Twenty February 2030," she said. "Member of the public. Four questions, and the fifth one got answered and I have decided that is a day I am going to remember." She put the pen back on its string. "Go on, before I remember I am supposed to be doing a job."

---

He walked back up the row at about twenty to seven in the evening because the bus had already gone, and the clinic door had nothing on it at all.

The tape was still there. The two drawing pins were still in the top corners of it and the paper was gone, and there were four small pale rectangles on the paint where it had been for twenty-eight days, and a strip of clean colour along the top edge where the length of tape had protected it from the weather, and the pins had gone with the paper and the holes they had left were the size of a nail and about the colour of a small bruise.

He stood and looked at them for a while. There was no version of that door in which anything had happened on the twenty-eighth day, and the woman whose name was at the bottom of a piece of paper in a drawer in the back office had looked at it, and the looking was the whole of what the date had bought.
