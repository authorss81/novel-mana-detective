# Chapter 0117 — Three in a Day Room

The corridor outside the magistrates' chambers at Vine Street at eight o'clock on a Tuesday morning in March has a stone floor, a radiator that knocks, eleven doors, a bench with the middle slat missing, and a clerk's window with a hatch, and at eight o'clock there is a queue of two men who want to pay a fine and a woman with a plastic folder who has clearly been there since half past seven.

The woman with the plastic folder was a doctor of forty-seven and her name was Sana Okafor and she had a registration number on the top of a form in the folder and a first attempt at an application in her own handwriting, and she had asked Elias Venn to walk her there at seven forty-five because she could not think what else to do with the two hours and would rather have a witness who was not a colleague.

They got the first refusal at about eight twenty.

"The premises field." The clerk was a woman of about thirty-four with a cardigan and a stamp pad and about four years of it, and she said it kindly. "It says two Wexford Row."

"That is the address of the premises."

"That is the address of a company," said Colette Marr. "**Harlowmere Rest and Rehabilitation Ltd, two Wexford Row. That is a legal person and I cannot make an application against a legal person, and if I could then the instrument would name a company and companies do not have doors.** What I can do is take a room, and a ward, and a part of a building that a person may lawfully be in. You want the day room."

"Yes."

"Then put the day room," said the clerk, "and the address underneath it, and be careful with the address because the address is what I will read out loud when somebody objects."

Sana Okafor wrote it out again on the second page. It took nine minutes and she did it twice.

The second refusal was at about nine twenty-five and it was in a room, not the corridor, and it took about ninety seconds.

Magistrate Ruth Dyment was fifty-seven and had a cardigan off and a bundle of papers with a rubber band round them and she read the second attempt standing up against the edge of her own bench, which she said she did because she was not going to sit down for it.

"**You are not the operator.**"

"No. I am a doctor. I have treated two of the three people in that room and I have seen both of them since the seventh of February."

"Then this is not an instrument I can give you, because an instrument for access to a private licensed ward runs to the operator or to somebody with a statutory right, and a doctor is neither, and a doctor with a registration number is a doctor with a registration number and not a door." She put the paper down. "You may attend your own patients. You may attend them at your own surgery, in a hospital, in a corridor, on a doorstep. You may not apply for a building."

"I understand."

"You do not, and I am going to tell you why, because you have written two pages and you have got the shape right twice." She turned the first page round and put her finger on it. "This asks for access. Nobody can give you access to a room, because a room is not a thing anybody has. A room is a set of walls and a door, and a door in a private building is the operator's, and the operator is a company, and a company has already said no, or has not said anything, and neither of those is a thing I can overrule at twenty to ten in the morning on a Tuesday without a document from somebody."

---

The third attempt was made in a corridor at eleven and it took eleven minutes to write and it was not the same document.

Elias Venn watched it happen from a bench with the middle slat missing, and he had said nothing for two hours, and he had been asked for no opinion once, which he had been braced for.

Sana Okafor had gone back to her first page and taken out everything that asked for a room. What was left was four lines.

> *I am the treating clinician of two of the three people in the day room at 2 Wexford Row.*
>
> *On the night of 22 January 2030, at about ten to ten, a dangerous working was performed in that day room, and two of the three people in it are my patients, and I can say that it happened in one sentence because I have been in that room on nine occasions since 7 February, and on every one of those nine occasions two of the three of them described the same evening to me in the same words, and neither of them knows that they have described it in the same words as the other.*
>
> *I am asking to speak to them. I am asking to take nothing and remove nothing and to have a member of the operator's staff in the room throughout. I am not asking about anything else that happened in that building that night, and there was a great deal more that happened in that building that night, and it is not mine and I am not asking.*

Ruth Dyment read that standing up as well, and it took her about forty seconds, and then she said: "Say that last part again."

"I am not asking about anything else that happened in that building that night."

"**Then I will tell you what that is worth and I will not pretend it is more than that.**" She put the four lines on her bench and squared them. "You have not asked me for an instrument, and I have not given you one, and there is no instrument in this city that would let you into that room in your own name, and if somebody later produces one and says it was made on the strength of what you have just said, I want to be able to read my own note and see that the words *access* and *instrument* are not on it."

"So what is on it."

"On it is a request by a doctor to attend two of her own patients in a day room, with a member of staff present, taking nothing." She wrote for about half a minute. "I grant that. And I am granting it because the sentence in the middle of that page is the first sentence anybody has said out loud in a room in this building about that night, and it is the only sentence in nine weeks that anybody has said to anybody about that night that was not written down first and shown to the person being asked. **I am not granting it because I believe it. I have got no evidence of it and I have not asked for any and you have not offered any, and a magistrate does not grant a thing on a belief.** I am granting it because a doctor has told me in a room that two of her patients have said the same thing twice and cannot tell, and that is not evidence, and it is the only thing I have, and I am not going to be the second person in nine weeks to hear nothing at all about those people."

"You are the first," said Sana Okafor.

"I am the first anybody in this building has been," said Ruth Dyment, "and I do not want any credit for it and I am not going to be given any, and I have not had a telephone call about it in nine weeks and I have not asked for one."

---

The rules were written on the back of the form at twenty past eleven in a corridor and Sana Okafor wrote them herself and read them to him before she went in, standing up, with her bag on her shoulder.

"Before I say the rules I am going to tell you three things about that room, because I am not going to be in a corridor for four hours with nothing, and because you have been standing on this floor since a quarter to eight and you are allowed to know what you are waiting for."

"Go on."

"The room is on the ground floor and it is warm and it is not a hospital room, it is a room where people sit." She said it in the flat voice she used for things she had looked at properly. "There is a table with a jigsaw on it that somebody has started and stopped, and there are nine pieces missing from it, and the loose ones are in an open tin on the same table, and I have counted the holes twice because I count things now, and there is nothing whatever in the number nine. It is a jigsaw on a table in a warm room and I have decided that is all it is and that nobody in that building is going to hear me say the number out loud in there."

"And the second thing."

"There is a woman of eighty-one called Doris Sowerby and she folds her own cardigan. **She has folded it and put it on the back of a chair, and it is a cardigan she has had for a long time and it is folded the way a person folds a thing they have decided about.** She does it every afternoon when she has finished, and when I came in last Tuesday she asked me whether I would mind the radiator, which was not about the radiator, and I have not worked out what it was about and I am not going to in a corridor."

"And the third."

"**There is an attendance sheet on the wall by the door, and three lines in it are in a second hand, in February, and there is no reason given on any of the three.**" She looked at the floor. "The second hand is not the ward's hand. It is later and it is heavier and it is somebody who does the paperwork at the end of a shift. And what the three lines say is that a person was transferred out of that room on the eleventh of February and transferred back into it on the eleventh of February, and both entries are in the same hand and the same pen, and the transfer back has no time on it, and the transfer out has a time, and it is not ten o'clock at night, it is a quarter to six in the afternoon, which is when a person is moved back into a room they have been out of all day."

"The person is not in the room."

"The person is not in the room," said Sana Okafor. "**There are two of them in that room and there should be three. The third is on the same floor, in a room of her own, and she was moved out of that day room and has not been in it since, and the sheet does not say when she was moved out of it, and the only thing the sheet says happened is that it happened in February.**"

"That is not the same thing."

"It is not the same thing and it is the only door anybody has looked through, and I have been in that room on nine occasions since the seventh of February and I have not asked about it on any of them, because I have had an hour each time and because I am not going to spend the hour I have been given on a book when there are two women in the room I am going to spend it on." She looked up. "And I am telling you it and not writing it, so it is in your head and not in a book, and that is not me being careful with you. That is me being careful with me, because I am about to go and sit down with two people and I do not want to walk in there with a document in my head."

"One. I go in. You do not."

"There is nothing I would—"

"**You do not get to have that conversation with me at half past eleven in a corridor, and the reason is not the case and it is not the building.**" She did not raise her voice. She had a way of arriving at a thing and standing in front of it. "A man who reads orders is a man a frightened person can feel. That is all it is. It is not a faculty and it is not a reputation and it is not anything you have done. It is that I have been in a room with a frightened person who could tell, and I was not told one thing about him that I could not see for myself, and I have thought about it every day for eight weeks, and I am not going to walk you into a room with two women in it of eighty-one and seventy-one and a jigsaw on the table and a woman in a corridor who is frightened, because I have been the person who is not told, and so have you, and I am not doing it to you in a building where it is going to be done to somebody else all afternoon."

"Sana—"

"Two. I will be in there from about half past ten until one. I have told them I am coming and I have not told them what about, because I have not worked out what about, and I have got three hours and a folder and I am going to work it out in a car park if I have to. Three. If anybody in that building asks you a question you say that you are the member of the public and that you are not the police and that you are not the doctor, and then you say nothing else, and if they ask you a second time you say *I am here to wait* and then you say nothing else. Four."

"There isn't a four."

"There is a four and it is the only one that matters," said Sana Okafor. "**You do not come in. Not once, not for a minute, not to the door. There is no instrument for this and there is no instrument against it and I could not stop you and you are not going to, and if you came in for one minute then the two of us would have spent the next five months knowing that the day I told two old women what had been done to them in a room, the man who reads orders was stood outside the door.** I was twenty-five and I was in a building where somebody stood outside a door, and I have not forgotten it. Do not do it."

"All right."

"Say it back to me."

"You go in and I do not, and I am not going to come to the door, and if anybody asks me I am a member of the public who is not the police and not the doctor and I am here to wait."

"Good," said Sana Okafor. "That is the first time in five months that somebody has told me what to do and I have watched you not argue, and I am going to think about that later, in a car park, for about ten minutes, and you are not going to be there."

---

There is a car park at the back of 2 Wexford Row with nine bays and a lamp on a post that is on in the daytime and off at night, and Elias Venn got his car into it at about twenty-five past ten and did not have a flask and did not have a book, and was in the car park for two hours and forty-five minutes.

He counted the bays when he got out of the car, because he always did, and there were nine, and he counted them again from the driver's side with the door shut and it was nine both times, and it was not eleven, and he was aware of the number for the whole of the afternoon and it did not once do anything.

He found that he could not read. He found that he had brought nothing to read, and that this was on purpose, and that he had not known it was on purpose until about eleven. He took his own notebook out at about ten to eleven and wrote the date and the time and then wrote nothing else for an hour and twenty minutes, and the page had two things on it and a great deal of white paper, and he looked at the white paper for a long time and it did not get easier.

He thought about the four lines on the form, and about the sentence in the middle of them, and about the fact that a magistrate of nineteen years had heard a description of what had happened in a day room for the first time in nine weeks, and had granted four lines and no instrument, and had written down a request and not a finding, and had said in a room that she wanted to be able to read her own note and see that the words *access* and *instrument* were not on it. He thought about the second attempt, the one that was refused in ninety seconds, and about the fact that the reason it was refused was that a doctor is not a door, and he thought that if the second attempt had been made in October, in a corridor, by a police inspector, with a different two words in it, then the third attempt would not have been necessary and there would not have been a woman of forty-seven standing in a corridor at Vine Street at half past eleven in the morning writing four lines on the back of a form, and he thought about that for about ten minutes and then thought about something else.

At about half past twelve he got out and stood at the fence. The yard behind it had a wall along one side with a gate in it and eleven stacked chairs under a canopy, and he looked at them for about a second and a half and then deliberately looked at something else, and he was about four feet from a chain-link fence with a lamp on a post and the sun coming off the front of a building he was not going to go into for another two and a half hours, and there was nothing in the world for him to do.

He did the thing he had not done since September, which was to look at a thing with no reason at all. He looked at the gate for a while. Then at a drainpipe. Then at a poster on the wall of the building opposite with a telephone number on it for a service he had never heard of. Then at the sky, which was the colour of a coin on a cold day, and which he looked at for about four minutes, and which he had not looked at since he was a boy in a street that is not Kettle Rows.

At about ten past one Sana Okafor came out of the building on her own and got into her car on the far side of the yard without looking in his direction, and sat there for about two minutes with her hands on the wheel, and then reversed out and drove off and did not wave and did not stop.

He sat in the car park for two hours and forty-five minutes and nobody asked him to be and nobody asked him to leave and he did not object to either, and for about a minute after she had gone he sat with the engine going, looking at a door on the ground floor of a building on Wexford Row through a chain-link fence, and then he reversed out into a street and went home, and he did not tell anybody where he had been and nobody knew, and the door of 2 Wexford Row was still shut.
