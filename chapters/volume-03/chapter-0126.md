# Chapter 0126 — A Clipboard and a Bed Frame

The building at 2 Anselm Street had a print on the door by half past nine on Monday the first of April, and the print said the building closes, and it had been on the door since the middle of February, and there were three men and a van at the kerb and the van had a mattress in it already.

Gareth Prothero was on the step with a folder under his arm and a key in his hand, and the key was not being used on anything, and he was forty-four and had been the contract officer for eleven years and three contracts, and on Sunday the thirty-first of March the third of those contracts had ended.

"You are not on the list," he said.

"I have not come in."

"You are standing in the doorway of a building that is closing, which is inside it." He moved and let him in. "**Do not touch the board.**"

The notice by the office door had three things on it and had had three things on it since February, and the top one was a review notice with a date on it and a hardback book on a table under it and a box on the wall beside the table with a slot in the lid, and over the first card was a second card that said *or say it on the day*, and under the review notice was a printed slip the size of A4 that said the beds close from the thirty-first of March and this building closes.

The book had four pages in it that were blank and it had been on that table since the fourteenth of January. The box had been empty since the fourteenth of January. Nobody had put anything in either.

Perpetua Ayodele was at the office door with a list on a clipboard and she was forty-four and had been the day worker for six years, and she looked at Elias Venn and then at the man in the doorway and did not say anything about either.

"Right," she said. "**Seven.**

"Seven what."

"Seven of the frames are already down in the hall and there are four more on the first floor and the hoist is working, and he has been going up and down it since half past six." She looked at the clipboard. "And I am not going to say how many people went out of that building yesterday and I am not going to say how many went out of it last week, and I did not say it in March when you asked me and I am not going to say it now, and the reason has not changed and it is the same reason and it is the only thing I know about that building that I am certain of."

"What is the reason."

"There is no list." She put the clipboard against her chest. "There has never been a list. **Forty-one lines on a schedule and a bed, a band, a rate, an operator code, a care need and a review date, and not one of the forty-one is a person, and I have been here six years and I have signed off the end of that schedule at about seven every morning and there has never once been a mark on it that said how many of them were slept in.** I have got a line for a person's name for every single morning and I keep it in my own head, and I have never written it down, and I have been asked for it twice in six years and both of those were in the last six weeks."

---

The sign-off was at seven and it took about ninety seconds and Elias Venn watched all of it from a chair against the far wall, and there were nine chairs against that wall and he counted them without deciding to and it was nine and he was aware of the number for the rest of the morning.

Perpetua Ayodele went down the lines with a pen and made a mark at the end of each one, and the marks were the same mark, and there was no column for a reason, and she got to the end of the schedule and turned the sheet over and signed across the bottom with a date, and the date was the first of April, and then she put the pen in her pocket and said the thing she had said every morning for six years to whoever happened to be standing in the room.

"**That is the end of the schedule and there is nobody on it.**"

"Sign it off," said Martin Rowntree, from the doorway, not unkindly.

"I have signed it off."

"You have signed the end of the schedule. I want the void signed off, and the void is on my sheet and it wants a number against the second column and there is no number in there that is not a piece of furniture." He came and looked at the page over her shoulder and did not touch it. "I am not asking you for a person. I am telling you that a piece of paper I have to sign at four o'clock this afternoon has a column headed BEDS REMOVED, and I have got seven, and I am going to write seven, and in about a year somebody in a different office is going to read that sheet and see a seven and put it next to your forty-one, and that is not me and it is not you and it is going to happen anyway."

"Then write seven," said Perpetua Ayodele.

"I am going to write seven."

"**Then do it and stop talking to me about it, because you are the fourth one this month and the first three had badges and one of them had a warrant that she did not need and told everybody about it in the car park.**" She did not raise her voice. "I have signed off the end of that schedule every morning for six years and nobody has ever asked me to put a number anywhere and every one of you has wanted me to, and I have said no to every one of you, and I have got a rule of my own about that which is not a policy and I have never said it out loud to anybody, and you are going to hear it from me once and I am not going to say it again."

"Say it."

"I do not give people to buildings," said Perpetua Ayodele. "Not to this one, not to the sheets, not to the register anybody has ever shown me. **You want to know how many people are in a building and I have to look at the building, and the second the building has a number in it, the building has got a number in it, and I have watched it happen to about eleven people in this district in six years and not one of them was asked whether they wanted to be a number.**"

She went back to the door and the clipboard and said, "Seven," again, to nobody, and that was the end of it.

---

The man with the clipboard was called Martin Rowntree and he was forty-seven and he worked for the trust on contracts and buildings, and he was not a nurse and had never claimed to be one, and he was doing a void.

"Right," said Martin Rowntree. "**Furniture, linen and equipment. Beds, frames, mats, hoists, curtains, the lot. That is the void and that is what I get paid for and I am not going to apologise for it.**"

"No one has said anything."

"You have got a face on you." He turned the clipboard round. "Bed frames, seven. Side tables, eleven. Curtain rails, six. I have got it all and I am counting it and I am going to be honest with you about why, because you have got a coat on that says you are asking. **I am counting furniture because furniture can be counted, and I have got to sign this sheet at the end of it, and the sheet says what is removed from the premises and where it goes, and I cannot sign a sheet that says a person.**"

"You do not have to."

"No, I do not." He looked round. "And I want to be careful here, because I have been in this trade nineteen years and I have done four voids and there is a thing that happens and I have stopped arguing about it. **The contract pays for the removal of the furniture. It does not pay for the removal of the people. There is no clause for it. There was never going to be a clause for it, because a clause for it would be an admission that the building has people in it, and the building has beds in it.**"

"So the people go on their own."

"The people go on their own two feet, over nine days, with their own money, to wherever they are going, and about two-thirds of them have somewhere and about a third of them have not and the third are the reason there is a review on the ninth of April and a hardback book on a table and a box on the wall." He put the pen in his pocket. "**And the ninth of April is a Tuesday, and the last one of them goes out on Monday, and I know that because it is on my sheet, and nobody has ever asked me why a closure runs for nine days, and the answer is that a clause in about 2011 said nine, and before 2011 it was three.**"

Nobody said anything about that either.

---

Elias Venn stood in the hall of a building he had read in twice in the dark and had never been in in the daytime, and the office had nine chairs against the far wall and there was a wooden stair with a handrail on one side only and a television on the wall with the sound off, and the hall smelled of a building that has been emptied and not yet aired.

Gareth Prothero came past with a woman from the trust and stopped.

"You will want to know the dates," he said.

"I have got the dates."

"Have you got mine." He did not wait for an answer. "**On the thirty-first of March the contract ended. I have been the contract officer on this contract for eleven years and I have been paid for forty-one beds for eleven years and I have never once been paid for a person, and I have thought about that about once a year and I have a filing system and a budget cycle and I have never once put it in writing.**"

"You have said that to me before."

"I have said it to two people before and one of them was in a stairwell at half past eleven at night in a coat." Prothero looked at the clipboard. "**And I have not been paid since Friday, and the building is empty, and I have been paid for neither this month, and I would like somebody to put that in a book.**"

"There is no box for it."

"That is what I have been trying to say to a budget meeting in June for eleven years," said Gareth Prothero, "and you have said it to me in a hallway in four seconds and in better words and I am going to be annoyed about it for the rest of the day."

On the second sheet of Martin Rowntree's clipboard, under a heading that said POSTS VACATED ON CLOSURE, there was a list, and one of the lines on it read:

> *Night porter — VACANT*

There was no name against it, and there was no name in the column beside it, and there was a date in the third column, and the date was in February.

Elias Venn read it and did not ask who had told them the post was vacant, and did not ask whether anybody had said it to the man, and did not ask whether the man knew that his post had been written off on a piece of paper in an office in a building four streets away in February. The chapter did not say who the man was, and the reason it did not say was nine words long and had been said on a Thursday in February to a man in a chip shop, and a man who has promised a thing and then asked a question about it in a corridor in April is a man who has found out what the promise was worth in a different currency than the one he was told about.

He had promised that nobody would be told where the man was, and he was keeping it, and the post said VACANT, and the two of those fitted together in a way he was not going to examine in a hallway with a man holding a clipboard.

---

They took a bed frame down at about eleven and it went out through the front door on the shoulder of two men and into the van, and the frame was green and had a number painted on the end of one leg in the same hand as the schedule, and nobody asked for the number to be read out and Rowntree wrote it in the second column.

The woman came out at about a quarter past eleven.

She was about seventy-six and she had a bag with two things in it and a coat on over a cardigan and she came down a wooden stair with a handrail on one side only, holding the rail with her left hand, and she stopped in the hall and put her hand on the wall and stood there for a moment, and then she went out through the front door of a building that had been closing for a fortnight and did not stop on the step.

"That is the last of the ones who were here on their own," said Perpetua Ayodele. "**I am not going to tell you how many of them there were. I have told you twice and I am not going to tell you a third time and I have now told you twice for nothing, which is what comes of it.**"

"How long was she in it."

"Three weeks. She came in the March before last and went out on the first of April and she has a sister in Calder Street and I have telephoned the sister twice." The day worker put the clipboard down on the window ledge, which was the first time Elias Venn had seen her put it down. "**And I want to say the thing about her, and then I am going to go back to the door, and it is not a story and there is nothing at the end of it.**"

"Go on."

"I carried her bag down the stairs and I took her to the shop on the corner and bought her a sandwich she did not want, and I did all of that, and none of it is in the contract, and the contract does not pay for a sandwich, and I paid for the sandwich, and I would do it again on Wednesday." She picked the clipboard up. "**And the reason I am telling you is that for about two seconds on that step she was a person walking out of a building and about a second after that she was a person who had been in a building, and the second thing is what a piece of paper does to you, and you have been here twenty minutes and you have already started to be the second thing, and I have watched about nine men in good coats do it in this doorway in six years and not one of them noticed that they had.**"

"I have not written anything down."

"Then you are the first," said Perpetua Ayodele, and went back to the door, and said, "Seven," again, to nobody, and that was the end of that as well.

At about half past two a mattress went out on a different shoulder and Rowntree wrote another number in the second column of his sheet, and at about ten to four he came out onto the step with the clipboard and read the first column of it out to nobody in particular.

"**Frames, seven. Side tables, nine. Curtain rails, six. Beds removed, seven.** That is furniture and I am going to sign it at four o'clock and take it upstairs, and I would like it noted that I have been counting furniture all day and it is the only counting anybody has done in this building since the twenty-second of January, and I have been in this trade nineteen years and I know exactly how that sounds and I have decided to say it anyway.**"

"**I have started to think the box was a mistake,**" said Gareth Prothero, standing in front of it with his folder under his arm and his hand not touching it. "I put it there in January. I want somebody to put a name in a box next to a hardback book and I have wanted that for about two years, and in January I put a box and a book and a card, and it has been empty since the fourteenth of January and I have not once seen a person stop in front of it."

"Nine people came to the last one."

"**Nine people came to the October one and every single one of them worked for me, and I have known that since about half past two on the day, and I put a box on a wall in January because I thought a different kind of person might come, and a different kind of person has not come in eleven weeks, and the reason a different kind of person has not come is that a different kind of person does not know this building exists.**"

He went back in. Elias Venn stood on a step on Anselm Street at about ten past four in the afternoon with a building behind him that had a hardback book on a table and a box on a wall and both of them empty, and there was a print on the door that said the building closes, and he did not know how many people had slept in it, and there was no list, and there was not going to be one, and he had been told so three times in six weeks by three people, and the third one had been a day worker holding a clipboard, and the fourth person to have told him anything about that building in six months was a contract officer with a folder under his arm who had not been paid since Friday.
