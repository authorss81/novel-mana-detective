# Chapter 0125 — The Back Door on a Friday

He got to the parade at about twenty past two on the Friday afternoon and he walked past the front of the building without looking at the sign, and then he went down the side to the yard and through the gate, because that was the door and the whole of the point of the door was that it was the door.

There were four bins and a stack of flattened cardboard and a wall with a poster about a recycling collection that had been taken down and had left a clean rectangle and a staple. There was a door with a bar across it and no sign on it, and the bar was down.

He stood in the yard for about two minutes. On the far side of the glass in the waiting area there were eleven chairs and a table with a leaflet holder on it, and he walked past the glass and did not count them, and he noticed that he did not count them, and he stood there for a second afterwards working out what that was.

The door opened.

"You have come round the front," said Ray Tindall, and he was in the fleece with the Royal National at the top of the list and nothing under it, and he was carrying a bin bag in each hand and it was about half past two on a Friday afternoon and he was going out to the bins.

"I have."

"Nobody comes round the front and then comes round the back." He put the bags by the door. "**You have come round the back first and you have stood in my yard for two minutes, and if you had come round the front and asked me for the address I would have had to think about it, and I would have written it, and it would have taken me about four seconds, and you would not be standing in a yard.**"

"Would you have written it."

"I wrote it on Friday and I have been thinking about it all week and I have decided that I would do it again," said Ray Tindall, "and I have also decided that if you had asked me in the parade I would have said no, and I would have said no because you were a man in a good coat in a parade, and I have been right about nine things in four years and that is not one of them."

He came and stood against the wall opposite, six feet away, at a distance that was not accidental.

"You are going to ask me why it is a Friday."

"I am going to tell you why it is a Friday," said Elias Venn.

The man in the fleece did not move for about four seconds.

"Right," he said.

"There is a civic store on the north side of this city, past the tram shed, and on the wall by the counter there is a board with a week on it and seven columns and about a hundred and forty buildings down the side of it. The north of this city has been a Friday since before the war. The round goes out on the Friday and comes back on the Saturday and the paper is unloaded on the Monday, and the office that listed it wants to be able to say the paper went out on a Friday, and that is the whole of the reason and it is a scheduling arrangement and the man behind that counter has been there eleven years and nobody has ever asked him."

Ray Tindall put both hands in the pockets of the fleece.

"A van," he said.

"A van."

"Four years." He was looking at the wall with the clean rectangle on it. "Four years I have had about a dozen people through that door and it is always a Friday, and I have got a whole thing about it. I have a **theory**. I have had a theory about it since the second year and it is a good theory and it has got about four things in it and one of them is that Friday is the day the office is quietest and one of them is that a person who has just had a case closed does not want a weekend, and I have never been able to join them up, and I have told nobody about it, because there is nobody to tell."

"Does that hurt."

"What?"

"That it is a van."

"It is not a van," said Ray Tindall. "**It is a van. I want it to be a van. I have spent four years with a theory about a Friday and a man has come round the back of my building and told me it is a lorry timetable in ninety seconds and now I have got nothing.** I am not being dramatic. I have got a drawer of four hundred forms in the back office and not one of them is for what happens to a person, and a theory was the only thing in four years that was mine, and now there is a board on a wall."

---

"You get one question," said Ray Tindall, "and then I am going to move two bins and go back inside, and I have said that at the start so that neither of us is doing anything about it later."

"I know. I am not going to use it on the door."

"Then ask it."

"Who was the first person you ever sent to that door."

Ray Tindall took his hands out of his pockets.

"Twenty-six," he said. "It was a man of about forty and he had a bag and he had been in my office four times and on the fourth time I wrote it on the back of a form and pushed it across, and I did not read it out then either, and I have not read out one since. **He went. She took him in. And eleven weeks later he came back into this building through the front door, which I have not told anybody that either, and he stood where you are standing, and he asked me whether there was a form.**"

"What did you say."

"I said I did not know." Ray Tindall did not move. "I have got four hundred forms in that drawer and I said I did not know, and I have thought about that for four years and I have not been able to make the sentence better. **And I have not sent anybody since. Not one, in four years. There were three more and I have their names on no piece of paper at all, because I put them in my head, and I know that is exactly the mistake and I made it anyway.**"

"Why did you stop."

"Because he came back to me." The man in the fleece shifted his weight. "**Not because of her. Because of me. He came back to this counter and asked me for a form and I said I did not know, and I have been in this job four years and I have had about nine hundred people through that front door and I have never once been asked for a form that I do not have, and it turns out I did not know that, and I have not got over it.**"

Elias Venn did not say that it had been the right thing to do and he did not say that it had not, because both of those would have been about him and there was a man standing in a yard who had said something true about a counter.

"There is a thing I have never been able to explain to anybody," said Ray Tindall, "and I have been asked about it twice by women who wanted to help and both times I have given them a worse answer than this. **Nobody closes a case in this building.** That is what I want said about my own job first, because people assume. I have got a counter and a screen and a drawer of four hundred forms and about eleven keys on a board, and I do not have a key to anything, and I could not close a person's case if somebody gave me one. The letters come from four buildings and one of them turns up on a Friday and I have read about nine hundred of them and I could not tell you which building does what to a piece of paper except that I know which four, because that is a thing you can find out and a lot of things you cannot."

"Which four."

"That is the second question and I am not going to do two." Ray Tindall put his hands back in the fleece. "**But I will tell you the thing about the letters, and it is the only thing about them I have ever noticed, and I have had four years to notice it and I have noticed exactly one.** They do not arrive. They arrive on a Friday. Every single one I have ever looked at, and I have not looked at nine hundred, I have looked at about forty of them because the rest are about rent, and every one of the forty that are not about rent came on a Friday, and I did not think that was interesting for about three years and then I thought it was interesting and I have not been able to stop thinking about it."

"That is the van."

"That is whatever you say it is," said Ray Tindall, "and you have been in a store for a day and a half and I have been in a parade for four years, and I am not going to be the man who tells a man in a good coat how to be right about Fridays in my own yard."

"Can I ask you something you did not offer."

"You can ask it and I can say no."

"**Is she safe.**"

Ray Tindall did not answer straight away, and in the four seconds it took him not to answer, a bus went past the end of the yard and Elias Venn understood that the man had understood the question as being about the woman in Pellow Street, and had understood it correctly, and had not expected to be asked it in his life.

"I do not know," said Ray Tindall.

"That is the honest answer."

"That is the only answer I have got and it is the first one anybody has given me." He took his hands out of the fleece again. "**Four years. Four years and about a dozen people through that door and everybody I have spoken to about it has said *yes*, and not one of them has been able to tell me why they know, and I have stopped asking, and you have come round the back of my building and asked me the question and I have had to say that I do not know, and I want you to understand that this is the first useful thing anybody has said to me in four years and it is not useful at all.**"

"That is how useful works."

"That is how useful works," said Ray Tindall, "and I have not got a word for the rest of it and I am not going to get one at half past two on a Friday with a man in my yard."

---

He moved the two bags out to the bins and came back and put his hand on the bar across the door.

Then, before he lifted it, the gate at the end of the yard moved.

Elias Venn had about four seconds to decide where to stand and he went and stood behind the stack of flattened cardboard, which was the only thing in that yard a man could stand behind and not be standing in the way of a door, and he stayed there and he did not look at the gate directly and he did not go out of the yard, because a man who goes out of a yard in the middle of something is a man who has decided that something is his.

Ray Tindall heard it before he saw it. He turned round, put the bags down again, and said, "One moment, I am just—" to nobody, and then he walked to the gate, which was a thing he had never once done in four years and which he did now without a thought, and which told Elias Venn more about the arrangement than any of the words either of them had used.

There was a bar on that gate as well, and he lifted it, and he stood about two feet inside it, which was the most he had to do, and which was enough, because a person came four inches into a yard and then stopped.

Nobody said anything at all. There was a bag, and a coat that was not a coat, and the whole of the business took about fifty seconds, and at no point in those fifty seconds did anybody in that yard raise his voice or touch anybody, and at no point did the man in the fleece ask the person a question that Elias Venn could hear, and at the end of it the gate went back and the bar went across and Ray Tindall picked the two bags up again.

"You have not asked me a single question about that man," he said.

"No."

"**He comes at about this time on a Friday and he has never once been early and he has never once been late, and I opened that gate about two hundred times and I did not open it today until I had put my bags down, and I put my bags down because I did not want him to have to wait while I was carrying them.**" He shifted the bags. "And I do not know his name. I have never known his name. He is not a customer and he is not on anything and I have never written him down, and if he were a customer I would have had to open the front door, and if I opened the front door then he would have had to walk in past the sign."

"You have never asked why it is a Friday."

"I have never asked him anything at all," said Ray Tindall, "**and that is not a courtesy. It is that I am a man behind a counter in a parade and if I stand in a yard at half past three on a Friday and ask a person who has come through my back gate why they came through my back gate, then I am a man who has done that, and I am not going to be a man who has done that, and I would rather be a man who did not know.**"

He went on up the ramp with the bags, and stopped at the door.

"There is a thing I have never been able to work out and I am going to say it once and then I am going to open this door." He did not turn round. "**She has never sent anybody to me. Not one, in four years. She has never telephoned and she has never written and she has never come to this front door, and I have sent four people to her and she has sent me nothing at all, and if I had been put in a room with a woman of sixty-three and a book I do not think I would have managed that.** I have been four streets away for four years and I have known her address for four years and there is a part of this that I think is her being better at it than me, and there is another part of it that I think is the whole of why a person comes through a back gate at half past three on a Friday and lets a man with two bin bags do the rest of it."

He lifted the bar.

---

"Go on," he said, with his hand still on it.

"You are not going to ask me whether I have ever been inside."

"No."

"**Everybody asks.** They ask it in the first ten minutes. They ask it in the yard and they ask it at the counter and one of them asked me if she had a business, and that was a man who had come from a building with a name over the door and he said it as though he were asking about a shop."

"Then I will not ask."

"No," said Ray Tindall, and lifted the bar, and went on holding it.

"I want to say the reason, and then I am going to go, because you have got a Friday and I have got a bin."

"Go on."

"You have sent four people to a door in four years and you have never been inside it." Elias Venn did not move his feet. "**I have been asking people for things for six months and I have been in about nine rooms in this city that I had no business in, and every one of them had a person in the room who had decided I was allowed. You have decided four people are allowed and you have never once decided about yourself, and I am not going to be the man who follows you through a yard door to find out whether the inside is better.**"

Ray Tindall stood in a doorway with a bar in his hand.

"That is either the best thing anybody has said to me since I started here," he said, "or it is a very good line, and I have been doing this job for four years and I have not worked out which, and I am not going to find out today."

He put the bar back across the door and shut it, and the glass in the door was the same glass, and inside the building, through the glass at the front, the man of thirty-six went across a waiting area with eleven chairs in it and did not sit down on any of them.
