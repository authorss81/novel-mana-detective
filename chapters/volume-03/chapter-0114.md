# Chapter 0114 — The Count at the Front of the Building

The Kettle Rows Community Clinic had a dispensary at the back with a hatch in it and a hatch that opened on a corridor, and a hatch that opened on the corridor had a queue at ten past eight on a Thursday morning, and the queue was for a thing that took four minutes and was not a prescription.

Sana Okafor came out of the back office at about ten to nine with her coat already on and a bundle of paper under her arm that she put down on the hatch counter and squared to the edge of the counter, which is a thing he had noticed her do.

"I am going to be in the corridor at half past for a thing I cannot leave," she said, "and you are going to say what you have come with, and then you are going to do something useful, and the something is on this counter, and if you do it well I will tell you one thing at the end of it that I have not told anybody, and if you do it badly I will tell you that the shelves need dusting, which they do not."

She put a hardback ledger about four inches thick on the counter on top of the bundle.

"I have nothing," said Elias Venn.

"I know that. Sit down."

"I have come three times in six weeks and twice of them I have had nothing, and I am not going to pretend I have got something this morning, and I would rather you knew that at the start than at the end of a morning."

"**Then here it is, once, and then it is not a thing between us any more,**" said Sana Okafor. "You have come to my door twice in three weeks with nothing, and a man who comes to a door with nothing is a man who wants something, and I would rather you wanted something out loud. If you had walked in this morning and said *I would like a fortnight in this building and I have nothing to give you for it*, I would have said no and I would have said it in four seconds and you would have gone and it would have been a better morning for both of us. What I cannot do is have a man stand in my corridor three times being *honest* at me, because I have got about four other people in this city being honest at me and every one of them wants something and I have not got the years for the difference."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is expensive," said Sana Okafor. "That book is what people at this clinic are owed. Not owed by anybody. Owed *to* them. A repeat not collected because the person was in hospital. A fee not charged because a person could not pay and would not say so out loud and I have not made them say it. A letter not sent because somebody forgot. A blood test not done in January and not asked for since. It is eleven years old and it is about four hundred pages and it is the only book in this building that nobody has ever audited, and it is not on my board and it is not in a drawer and it is not on anybody's list, because there is no list of people in this area, and I have told you before and I will tell you again that a list would be a bad thing to have, and I mean it, and I still want the book."

"What do you want done to it."

"Read it. That is all. **Read it and tell me which lines have never been closed.**" She went through the hatch and down the corridor. "Every line has a date on the left and an amount or a word in the middle and then a column on the right for the day somebody dealt with it, and the right-hand column has a tick in it when the thing was done, and the tick is put in by whoever did it, and the tick is not signed, and I have thought about the tick not being signed every month for about six years and I have never done anything about it, because doing something about it would be a form, and I have told you where I stand on forms."

She was gone.

He read it for two hours. It was not difficult work and it was the first work anybody had given him in five months that was not about a sequence, and he understood about a third of what he was looking at, and at about half past ten he had got to 2027 and he had worked out the shape of it, which was this: the book does not go forward. A line is opened when a person is owed something, and it stays open until somebody closes it, and nothing in this building goes back and looks at the open lines, and so a line opened in the February of 2028 by a woman of about eighty who could not get to the counter is still open, and there are a great many of them, and about half of them are for people who have died.

He did not count them all. He read every page from the back of 2026 forward and he marked nothing and he wrote nothing, and at twenty to eleven he had a list of nine lines in his head that had been open for more than a year, and he went and stood at the hatch and looked at the corridor and she was not in it.

He wrote the nine on the back of a dispensary label and gave it to the girl on the hatch, who was about nineteen, and said, "Give that to Dr Okafor and do not read it."

---

The wall at 2 Anselm Street had a notice on it about a review and a hardback book on a table under it and a box for a person's name, and there was a second card over the first one saying *or say it on the day*, and there was now a third piece of paper, a printed slip with a date on it, taped to the bottom of the notice.

**Beds close from 31 March 2030. This building closes.**

He stood in Anselm Street at about twenty past eleven on a Thursday morning and read it four times. It was A4, it was printed in house, and there was no signature on it and no letterhead, and it had been put up by somebody who had put a drawing pin through the notice above it and had not put a second pin in because the notice had only one hole in it any more.

Perpetua Ayodele had the office door open and the list on the desk and did not come out.

He did not ask her anything. He stood on the pavement for about four minutes and looked at a hardback book that had been on that table since the fourteenth of January and had nothing in it, and at a box on a notice for a person's name that had been empty since the notice went up, and at a piece of paper about a building closing, and he thought that in seventeen days forty-one beds would begin to empty and there was nobody in this city who would be able to say afterwards how many of them had been slept in, because the only record of who was in this building on any night in twelve years is four words and a time in a column of a book on a shelf in that office, and that is better and worse than a number.

He wrote nothing in the book. He had written nothing in the book on the twenty-eighth of February either, and he had told a woman of forty-four in that office that he would not, and he was a man who kept things, and this was the third week of keeping it.

---

3 Wendle Street, ground, was a shop with a counter and a window and about nine machines behind the window in a row, and a man of about sixty behind the counter in a cardigan with a cigarette he was not going to be allowed to finish, and the whole place smelled of hot fabric and steam and about four different kinds of softener.

"That is the fourth one," said Elias Venn.

"Which one."

"The twenty-third of January," said Elias Venn. "This year. A temporary order on a door in Kettle Rows that named four places, and this is one of the four, and I have been in the other three and I have got nowhere, and you are the last one, and I have been outside for about four minutes working out how to ask you."

The man put the cigarette in a saucer.

"I have had this shop since 2026," he said. "I have been in the trade since 1974 and I have never had a shop of my own before this one. Ask."

"You had paper brought in to you. Not washing. Paper."

"Everything comes out of coats," said the man. "That is the trade. You send a coat, a jacket, a bag of overalls, and it goes through and it comes back and whatever was in the pockets is in the pockets, and about one in thirty comes back with something in it that is not a receipt. **We have a box under the counter. It says UNCLAIMED on it in marker, and it has said it since 2026, and it is not a bin, because a bin is a bin, and a customer's property is not a bin.**"

"How much is in it."

"About two hundred sheets. Mixed. There is a lot of old post and there is a great deal of betting slips from a man who used to come in on a Saturday and there is about a hundred of these little torn-off things." He nodded at the box. "**We never had a name for them and nobody ever asked.** You are the first person in four years to come in and look at that box and everybody who has seen it has asked me what the machines cost to run."

"Consent strips," said Elias Venn.

"That is what they are called, is it."

"They are the torn-off line at the foot of a form. Six inches by two. It is the only part of that form anybody is required to keep, and the rest goes in a file and the strip goes in a wallet, and a strip has got a person's name on it and nothing else on it that is a person."

"And they are not mine."

"They are not mine and they are not yours and they are not anybody's in that box, and that box has been in a shop for four years." He put his hands flat on the counter. "**I am not going to ask you for them, and I am not going to take one, and I would like to photograph them and I am asking you to refuse me.**"

The man looked at him for a second.

"No," he said.

"Thank you."

"You are the first person who has said *please refuse* instead of saying *no thank you* and I have been doing this since 1974 and I have never noticed that before." He put his hand flat on the box. "Also no, and I am going to give you a proper reason and not just no, because you have been straight with me. They are not yours and they are not hers," meaning the doctor, "and I am not having a doctor's number on a box in my shop. She can come and stand in my shop any day of the week and I will make her a tea, and I will not have a registration number and a date next to a box of paper that is not the shop's."

"That is a completely correct reason and I would have given it myself if I had thought of it."

"You would not have thought of it," said the man, "because you have not been in a shop."

---

He stood at the counter for about forty minutes and did not touch anything, and then he said:

"There is one thing and it is going to sound like nothing and it is the only thing I have come for, and I would like you to do it in my hearing and not in another room."

"Go on."

"Your book of jobs. The book you keep the tickets in."

The man got it out of a drawer. It was a hardback notebook with a rubber band round it and about six years of entries in three hands, one of them his wife's.

"I want you to find a job from about 2026 or 2027 that came in from Saint Orra Reach, and I want you to read me the number on the docket, and I want you to telephone the number you have written against it, on this telephone, in front of me, and ask them one question, and then I am going to go."

"Which question."

"Whether the number is right."

The man went through about forty pages with a finger, and stopped, and turned the book round so Elias Venn could see it, and it was a line in his own wife's hand that said a job number and a date and a street and nothing else, and no name at all, which was the way his wife had written about coats for thirty-one years.

He dialled it on the shop telephone and stood with the receiver held out a little way from his ear so that both of them could hear.

"Hello. Is that Saint Orra Reach. It is about a job of ours from a while back. We have a job number here and I want to know if it is right. I do not know the name of the person, I have got the number only."

There was a pause and a chair going back.

"That is right," said a woman's voice. "**That is a rehabilitation account and it is not yours, and I am not able to tell you whose it is, and I have not been able to tell anybody anything about it for about a year.**"

"That is all," said the man. "I have only got the number."

"That is all. I am sorry. We cannot go any further on a telephone and I am not going to pretend that we can."

The man put the receiver down and looked at Elias Venn, who had written eleven characters on the back of his own hand with a biro in about four seconds, without taking the pen out of his pocket, which is a thing he had never done before and did not mention afterwards.

"Right," said the man. "Is that you then. Sorted."

"That is me then," said Elias Venn.

He put the book back into the drawer himself, on top of the rubber band, square to the edge of the drawer, because the man had squared everything in the shop to the edge of the counter and he had decided that he was not going to be a man who left a drawer out of line in a shop in Wendle Street.

"That is the only door in this case that has ever been open," he said, "and I am not going to be the man who goes through it, and I want you to know that I have thought about it for about three minutes standing here and I have decided it and it is not going to change because somebody in a good coat asks me about it in a corridor one day."

The man had not moved, and the cigarette in the saucer had gone out, and he looked at the number Elias Venn had written on the back of his own hand for a second and then said, "That is not much of a thing, is it, for four minutes of my wife's book."

"It is the only number in this case that has ever come out of a document nobody in any of this has seen," said Elias Venn, "and I want to tell you what it is, because you are entitled to know what you have just read out over your own telephone, and I am going to tell you the whole of it and you are not going to like it and I am not going to be able to do anything about that."

"Go on, then."

"**That is a number of an account, and not a number of a person, and an account is a legal person, and it is a housing charity, and it is the charity that runs the building you can see from your back door.**" He had looked it up on the fourth of March in a hardback book on a table in a hall and had copied nothing and had remembered one line of it. He turned his hand over so that the man could read it, and read it out loud, which he had not intended to do and then decided to let stand: three figures, a stroke, a figure, a stroke, a figure. "And that charity settles one account. Not one account a week, and not one a resident, and not one a month. One. And every form that anybody who works for that charity has ever been handed has come off that one account, and the only part of any of those forms anybody is required to keep is a strip the size of a postage stamp with a person's name on it, and a strip has the account number on it, and a strip goes in a coat, and a coat comes to a laundry."

The man in the cardigan did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Eleven years," he said. "I have had that man's coat in about two hundred times and he has paid every single time and I have never once asked him what was in the pockets, and you have just told me that the number of the whole of his building is on a bit of paper I have had in a drawer since about 2027, and that I would have thrown it out with the dockets in about two years if you had not come in here."

"You would have thrown it out with the dockets in about two years and nobody would ever have known that it had been in your shop," said Elias Venn. "And that is the whole of what I have come to tell you, and I have told it to you, and I have not asked you for the box and I have asked you to refuse me once and you have refused me, and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to go and pay for the wash of a jacket I have not got."

"Go on."

"**It is one number for a whole building,**" said Elias Venn. "Forty-one beds, a hall, an office, a night porter, a fire door, and a man who works nights and has been in that building for twelve years, and in the paperwork of this city every bit of that is one line on one account, and the line has got your number on it, and if the line ever gets looked at then everything on it gets looked at at once, and nobody has to want to hurt anybody for that to happen. It just happens, on a Tuesday, in an office, in about four minutes."

The man looked at the box under the counter, which said UNCLAIMED, which was two feet from the telephone, which was on the wall, which had been on that wall in 1974 when he put it there himself.

"Keep it," he said.

"I am not going to keep it and I am not going to touch it."

"Then I'll tell you what I'll do," said the man, "and I have not decided to do it, so don't write it down. When that box goes, and it goes when this shop goes, because I am sixty and I have a bad knee, and I know the month, and I have known the month since January — I will ring a number and I will not say what it is about, and if somebody comes, they come. **I have been keeping other people's paper for four years because it was not mine to bin. I am not going to find out tonight that it was never anybody's.**" He picked the dead cigarette out of the saucer and put it in his pocket. "You can have the name of the charity off your own memory, mind. I'm not saying it out loud in my own shop."

Elias Venn went out into Wendle Street and turned round on the step, because he wanted to look at it once and he knew he was going to want to remember it. The counter, and the box under the counter with a word written on it in marker by a man who has been here since 2026, and about four feet away from that a telephone on the wall that he screwed up himself the year he took the shop. Neither of the two things was locked. Neither of the two things had ever been asked for. One of them had been in that shop for four years and the other of them had been on that wall since before the shop existed, and a claim number had gone through both of them on a Thursday afternoon in March in about ninety seconds, between a woman who would not say a name and a man who did not know a name was being listened to, and neither of the three of them had the least idea in the world what any of it was for.
