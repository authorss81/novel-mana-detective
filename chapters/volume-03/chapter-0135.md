# Chapter 0135 — A Woman With a Bed

He got to Calder Street at ten past nine on Friday the nineteenth of April and the porch light was off and the door was on a latch and the sign said *Enquiries 9 to 5* and the sign had meant it.

The day desk was a shuttered counter with a dead telephone on it and a cardboard stand of leaflets for a service that had been discontinued in February. The corridor was lit at half the strength it had been on Tuesday, because a building that runs on night hours does not have the same lamps on at night, and the two lamps nearest the desk were off, and the whole of the first part of that corridor was in what daylight did not reach at nine o'clock in April.

There was a care assistant of about twenty-four at the end of it with a trolley.

"Can I help you."

"I am not here about a bed. I am here about a form, and I was here on Tuesday and the woman on the day desk gave me a form, and I have come back at night because I want to say one more question and I am not going to ask it on a Wednesday when the desk is busy."

"She is not here. She finishes at four." The girl did not move the trolley. "**Are you police, and if you are not then I have to ask you to not walk up that corridor on your own, and it is not about you.**"

"I am not police and I will not walk up the corridor."

"Sit down then."

"There is a day room at the end of it."

"There is a day room at the end of it and there are nine people in it who are watching a television with the sound off because the sound off is what they like, and you cannot sit in there either." She turned the trolley about forty degrees, which did not release him and was not intended to. "I will get Denise."

"There is no Denise at ten o'clock at night."

"There is a Denise on until eleven and she is in the office with the door shut and she does not have visitors, and I have told her there is a man on a chair and she said *all right*, and that is all the message I got, and it is going to be about nine minutes."

So he sat on a chair in a corridor outside a day room with the sound off, and at about ten to ten the office door opened and a woman came out into the corridor with a cardigan over her arm and a mug in the other hand, and she was about forty-four and she had a face that had been doing this for a long time, and she did not stop at the chair.

"You are the man who came on Tuesday and did not ask me anything," she said.

"How do you know that."

"Because she told me at four o'clock and she told me in about eleven sentences and two of them were about the board, and she has never mentioned a man on a board in twenty-six years." She drank the tea. "**I am Gaynor Riddell. I am on nights. I have been in this building nine years and I have a flat above a chip shop in Calder Street and I do not have a family and there is nobody at home, so you may as well say it in this corridor where it is warm.**"

"I would like to ask you one question and it is about a form."

"Then ask it."

"It is about a removals firm off Calder Street. A job on Monday the twenty-eighth of January, two men, a small van, about three hours, paid in cash. The name on the booking is yours."

Gaynor Riddell put the mug down on the windowsill.

"I telephoned them on the Thursday," she said. "I telephoned them on the Thursday at about half seven in the evening from the office because the office telephone is the only one in this building that is in a room with a door, and the day desk was empty and the corridor was empty, and I did it in about four minutes and I did not write it down anywhere and there is no piece of paper with my name on it except in a garage off this street."

"Three hours and about nine Calder Street to here."

"About eleven Calder Street. Eleven Calder Street is us." She said it without any drama at all. "**He had nowhere. That is the whole of it and I am going to say it in one sentence and then you can do what you like with me. I had a bed that was not going to be used that week, and he had nowhere, and there was nobody to ask.**"

Elias Venn sat on a chair in a corridor outside a day room and found that a thing he had been carrying since the twenty-fifth of March had gone out of him without moving.

"Who was he to you."

"Nobody. **He was a name in a book on a Friday afternoon and a voice on a telephone on a Saturday and a man at a door at about four in the morning, and that is the whole of the nine years of it, and I am not going to say one word more about him and you are not going to ask me, and we are both going to pretend that is a rule and it is not, it is a mercy, and I have got about four more minutes of it and then I have got eleven people to do.**"

"I was not going to ask you."

"Then you are the first person in four months not to." She picked the mug up. "**And now I am going to tell you the half of it that nobody ever tells, because you have not asked me a single question, and because it is a Friday and because I have been the only one in this building who knows that since the twenty-eighth of January, and there is a limit to how long one person can carry a thing in a building full of people who do not know it is there.**

"Nobody arranged him. Nobody in any office in this city arranged that man. I did it, off my own book, with a telephone and a bed, and a woman in a garage who did not ask me anything, and two men with a small van, and about thirty pounds in cash out of my own money, and it took four minutes to arrange and it took three hours to do. **And there is no form anywhere in this city for what I did, and there is no form for it because what I did was not a service and not a placement and not an admission and not a transfer. I gave a man a bed for eleven nights and then I found him something, and both of those are things a person does.**"

"You have been told not to do it again."

"I have been told not to do it again on the sixth of April, by a woman from human resources in Calder Street, in a meeting with four people in it, of whom I was one, and the word she used was *unsanctioned*, and she was not being cruel and she was not even being firm. **She said the risk is that you take a person who has not been assessed and you have not got a plan, and if that person goes into the hospital system with no assessment then somebody in a different office finds a form with a box on it and the box is not you, and the box is empty, and then there is a hole in the care of a person who is a person.**" She drank the rest of the tea. "Which is true. It is completely true. I have thought about it every day since the sixth."

"Do you still have the bed."

"**It is empty every Friday and every Sunday and it has been empty every Friday and every Sunday since the sixth of April, and I have walked past it about forty times, and I have put a man in it in my head about eleven times, and I have not put anybody in it, and I am not going to.**" She looked down the corridor. "So that is what I have, and you may decide for yourself whether it is the kind of thing you came here for."

"I came here for a form."

"You came here for a man and you have got a woman and a Friday," said Gaynor Riddell, "and I want to say one more thing and then Denise is going to come out of that office and I have to go and do eleven people."

"Go on."

She looked at the floor for about three seconds, and then at the corridor, and there was something in her face that was not there before and that he did not have a name for.

"There's a thing I am not going to tell you tonight."

"All right," said Elias Venn.

"**That is the second time this week somebody has said *all right* to me in that voice, and the first one was me.**" She straightened her cardigan on her arm. "Whatever it is, you did not come here for it, and if you had come here for it I would have said *no*, and I would have said it in about nine seconds, and I would not have told you the telephone call either."

Then, because there were three minutes and because she had decided to spend them:

"You are looking at a corridor and you are not seeing anything, so let me tell you what a night is in here, because you have been in a good many corridors in this city since February and I do not think one of them was a night. There are eleven of us in this building. Between midnight and about four in the morning there are two of us awake on the floor and the other nine are asleep, except in February, when the heating goes and everybody comes out. **And that is the whole job. That is a night. I go round at midnight, and at two, and at four, and about four of them are awake, and what I do for those four is turn a light on.**"

"That is it?"

"That is it. **Nine years and I have never in this building been in a position to change anything anybody feels.** I can tell you the two names of the people who are awake at two in the morning on a Friday and I could not tell you what either of them had for dinner, and that is not because I do not care and it is because nobody in a building like this is allowed to know, and the whole of the arrangement is that we know the medical things and not the other things, and on a night that works beautifully." She turned the mug about a quarter turn. "So when you come in here at ten o'clock at night and say *a man had nowhere*, you are talking to a person whose entire profession is standing next to that and being given nothing to do about it. I am not being cold. I am being accurate."

"And the eleven nights."

"Eleven nights and then I found him a room in a hostel on a Friday in the second week of February, and I want to be exact about the word *found* because it is not mine, it is his: he found it, and I gave him the number of a desk in a parade, and he went and sat in a queue and got a bed on a Tuesday, and he telephoned me on the Wednesday to say he had got it, and he did not say thank you, and I would not have wanted him to." She looked at the floor again. "**I have never met him. That is the part I have not told anybody and I have told you four things in a corridor. I have given a man eleven nights and a telephone number and I have never in my life seen his face, and if he walked into this corridor now I would not know him, and he would not know me, and that is what it is supposed to be.**"

"And in January you had a bed and he had nowhere."

"And in January I had a bed because the bed at the end of the corridor was going to be empty for a week because the woman in it was going into a hospital in the city for something they had done twice before, and I knew she was going because I was the one who booked the ambulance, and I was not going to be there in January, and that is the only sentence in this conversation that is about anything other than a form." She put the mug down. "**So I did a job I was not authorised to do on a Thursday evening in January because of a woman going into hospital, and it is the reason a man is not on a street this week, and nobody in this building except me and a woman of sixty-two knows any of it, and the woman of sixty-two knows it because the day desk told her on Tuesday, and I did not tell her and she told me anyway, and she is a better person than the woman from human resources and I have told her so.**"

"There is nothing you are not authorised to do."

"There is nothing I am authorised to do," said Gaynor Riddell, "**and the reason I am the one standing in this corridor saying it is that I have been doing this building's nights for nine years and I have a course behind me and a card and a small working I can do on myself when somebody in this building will not settle, and every single one of those three things was given to me by somebody who paid for it.** And a bed on a Friday night is not in any of them, and I did it anyway, and a week later a woman in Calder Street used the word *unsanctioned*, and she was not wrong about the risk, and the risk has not happened, and I have done it once, and I am not going to do it again, and the reason I am not going to do it again is that I do not want to be a woman who did a thing once and got away with it."

The office door opened and Denise came out, who was about sixty-two and was in charge of the building at night, and who looked at Elias Venn on a chair and said, "She has told you she did it and she has told you it cost her and now you are going to want to write it down."

"I am not going to write it down."

"Then what are you going to do with it."

"Nothing," said Elias Venn. "**And I want to be careful here, because you are both in a corridor and I am a stranger, and if I had said the other thing you would have been right not to believe a word of it.**"

Denise looked at him for a moment and then at Gaynor Riddell and said, "Nine minutes," and went back in.

And then the girl with the trolley came back up the corridor at about twenty to ten with a woman's cardigan over her arm that was not Gaynor Riddell's cardigan, and they went past, and the corridor was quiet again, and Elias Venn stood up off the chair.

He had been in that building for fifty-five minutes and there was a board on the landing upstairs with a ruled line in pencil for every name that had gone into the building, and a fire door at the end of that landing with a bar across it and a wedge of wood under it, and a lift that was a chair on a cable with a sign over it, and a building full of people, and not one of them was a man of fifty-four who had been a night porter for twelve years and had signed two pieces of paper in one room on the ninth of December, because a woman in the corridor had told him ten minutes earlier that the man had been in a hostel in this city since the second week of February and that she had never in her life seen his face, **and that was the first time in four months that a person had told him where a man was not.**

And he had not gone up, and he had not asked, and he had been in the corridor for fifty-five minutes with a woman who had done a kind thing off her own book in four minutes and had been told in a meeting with four people in it that the kind thing was unsanctioned.

He had also learned, in about ten minutes, that a woman who does that is not a plot in a case. He had known it for about four minutes. He had known it properly for about a minute, and the minute was the one in which she had said *the second one was me*, and had looked at the floor, and had not said the thing.

That was the whole of what he knew, and it was more than anybody else in the city knew, and it was not enough to be a witness to, and there was no box for it.

He went down the corridor to the porch, and the door was on the latch, and the night office was the other end of the building, and the only way out of the front was past a counter that a person has to be wheeled past, in a chair, on a bad day.

He went out through the fire door at the end of the landing instead, and propped it with the wedge of wood on the way out because a fire door that has been propped will stay propped and a fire door that has been pulled shut behind a man at ten o'clock at night will be found by a night sister at about two, and she would have had to stop and open it and she would have had to think about who had been in her corridor.
