# Chapter 0136 — The Sentence

He had a book of his own in which, on Monday the twenty-fifth of February, in the back room of this building with the electric ring off, he had written four lines about a manual that cost three credits, and the last of the four was that he was not going to read the ninth one, and that if he broke it he was going to have to write down the day he broke it, and that this was the only discipline he had left.

The back room of 3 Tanners Row had an electric ring that he had disconnected in November because a thing he had not been able to answer had been ringing in it. It was ten past eleven on Monday the twenty-second of April and the shop was shut and the till said forty-six.

On the shelf behind the door was a carrier bag. In it were a sheet of A4 with a Registry header, bought for two credits and a signature on the fourth of March, and a brown manual with a picture of a door on the front that had nothing to do with anything.

He took the manual out and left the other thing, and he said out loud, in an empty room, to nobody, that he was not touching the other thing.

He also said out loud, to nobody, that he had been awake since half past six and that there is a rule about not reading anything on a night when he has been awake, and that the rule is written on the back of a delivery note in her handwriting and is therefore binding on him in a way no court in this city could manage, and that he is going to sit at this table and read a printed booklet with his eyes, which is not reading in the sense the rule means, and that he knows the difference and is not enjoying the distinction.

Then he opened it.

The ninth module was four pages. It was headed **CONTINUITY OF ACCOUNT**, and it was in the same type as the other ten and set to the same page and printed in the same two-year-old green, and the first thing on the first page was a list of what a case is, in nine short lines, and none of the nine was anything he did not already know.

Page one, paragraph six:

> **A registered witness who has taken the course has given the undertaking set out at 9.4. The undertaking is given at enrolment, in the standard form, and it is not given again. It may be relied on at any time thereafter without further signature.**

He read it four times.

**Given at enrolment. In the standard form. And not given again. And it may be relied on at any time thereafter without further signature.**

He put it face down and put both hands flat on the table and looked at the lino, and then he turned it over and went on, because he has never once in his life been able to leave a page alone.

Page two, paragraph four:

> **A registered witness undertakes, where an account he has given no longer corresponds with a certified record, to bring his account into continuity with that record before that record is issued.**
>
> **The office will put the record to the witness as an extract, will ask him whether he agrees with it, and will minute the answer.**
>
> **Nothing in this section requires the witness to be shown the whole of the record. Nothing in this section requires any person to show him the whole of the record.**

Four lines. He counted them. There was a printed line under them in a smaller face, and he read that too, and it said that an extract is usually two pages and that the office does not decide which parts of a record are relevant to an account, and that the officer does.

That last clause was on the page and he read it twice and it took him about four minutes to understand what he was looking at, and when he understood it he did not shout and he did not stand up, and what he did was put his thumb on the paragraph and keep it there for about a minute, the way a man keeps a finger on a line in a book he is going to have to defend.

There was no date on the undertaking. There was no period of limitation. There was nothing about a hearing, and nothing about a lawyer, and nothing about a person being told they could have somebody with them, and there was nothing about what happened if they still said no after the second time.

There was a sentence on page three about repeating a disagreement, and he recognised that sentence, because he had read it in module 8 in a back room in February with a rule case on his lap, and it said that a disagreement is stated once and is then not repeated, because repetition is the mechanism by which a disagreement becomes a refusal. It was the same eight lines, word for word, in the same type, with the same example on page seven of a witness who had said *that is not what I said* and had then signed.

**The same eight lines that teach a trained witness how to hold a sequence are the eight lines that tell a trained witness to stop disagreeing, and they are printed twice in one manual that anybody may buy for three credits, and the difference between the two printings is a paragraph number and ninety seconds and a room.**

Nobody had hidden anything from anybody. That was the sentence that came out of the middle of him at about ten to midnight at a table in a back room, and it was the first sentence of the last four months that he had been sure of.

He got out his own book and wrote on a fresh page:

> *I did not open module 9 in February and I have broken that tonight and I am writing down the day I broke it as I said I would. It is Monday the twenty-second of April 2030. I broke it in this room at about ten past eleven at night and nobody made me and nobody asked me to and I have had a copy of that manual in a bag on this shelf since the thirteenth of March.*

He read it back and did not soften a word of it, and then he shut the book, and then he did the thing he had been doing for about eight weeks, which was to check what he had written against the thing in front of him, and he found one thing, and the one thing was the word **agreed**.

He had written on Tuesday the third of March, in his own book, four lines about a form with a signature in an agreement box, and one of the four had been that the box said *the witness has read the extract and agrees with it*, and the other half of the box said nothing at all, and he had assumed the other half said something, and the assumption was in his own handwriting in a book he had carried into six buildings in six weeks.

**He had assumed a man in a good coat had to give something up. And the box was not asking him for anything. The box was the whole of what he was being asked to do, and it had a border round it.**

He put the manual back in the carrier bag, behind the other thing, and pushed the bag onto the shelf, and it was on the shelf at ten past midnight and the room was as he had left it.

---

Mara Quill's two rooms were behind a hospital's linen office on Saint Orra Reach and there was a fire door at one end of the corridor and a window at the other that looked at a wall, and it was ten to one in the morning when he got there, and there was a light on under the door.

She was at the bench with her sleeves up and a plate on a mug, and there was a bench drawer shut and a hard case shut and a foil stencil in its box and a brown envelope in the drawer, and the consent strip in it had not been read since the sixth of January.

"Sit down," she said. "You have been awake since when."

"Half past six."

"Then you are not touching anything and neither am I until you have had a cup of tea, and do not argue with me about the two rules on the back of a delivery note, because you have read them out loud to me twice and both times it was while you were refusing to do something, and I have noticed that about you and I have decided it is a good sign."

He did not sit down.

"I have broken something," he said, "and it is not the rule on the back of the delivery note."

---

He told her at about ten past one in the morning, standing up, with his coat on, in a corridor behind a linen office, in about four sentences, and it took him four minutes and he had rehearsed it on a bus and it did not sound like anything he had rehearsed.

He told her that there was a man. He told her that the man had worked nights for twelve years at a building in Kettle Rows belonging to a housing charity with a registration number, that the building had closed on the thirty-first of March, and that the man had gone out of it on the twenty-eighth of January. He told her that a woman had telephoned a removals firm off her own book because she had a bed that was not going to be used that week.

He did not tell her the street the man was in. He did not tell her which building. He did not tell her a name, and there was no name to tell her.

She listened with both hands on the bench and did not interrupt once, and at the end of it she said:

"You made that promise in a chip shop in February."

"Yes."

"And you have told me a housing charity, a night porter and a date, and I could find that man inside a week with a telephone and a book, and I am not going to."

"Mara—"

"**Do not.**" She stood up. "I want to say this once and then I want to go back to work, and I am not going to be thanked and you are not going to thank me and I am not going to be forgiven tonight, because there is nothing to forgive, because you have done a thing and a thing that was done properly does not get forgiven, it gets recorded. **So: you have had that in your coat since February and you have carried it into this room about nine times and put it in the other room, and I have watched you do it every single time, and I have not said one word about it in four months, and I would like that to be written down somewhere that is not a piece of paper, because it is the only thing I have done in this work for four months that I am still certain of.**"

"That is not what I came to say."

"I know what you came to say, and you have said it, and it is out, and it was never going to stay in the bag, and I want you to hear the shape of the evening from where I am sitting, which is not *he finally told me*, it is *he finally found out something and immediately told a woman who works with him and cannot use it and who now has it.*" She put her hands on the bench. "**You did not break that promise because I deserved it. You broke it because you read a page.**"

He had not said that and it was true and it took him about four seconds to accept it.

"I read a page of a manual that anybody can buy for three credits," he said, "and it says a thing about a person agreeing to a document, and there is a printed form of words for it, and it was agreed to in a classroom in 2022 and not again, and the whole thing cost three credits and a signature and about nine weeks of somebody's life. **And I am the only person in this city who has read that page and understood it and has a man who has already been through it. And I could not sit on it. Not tonight. Not having read it.**"

"No," said Mara Quill. "You could not. And you could not take it to a police station either, because you know exactly what happens to a piece of paper about a person when a member of the public walks into a building with it, and you have watched it happen to four people since February and you have written down the reasons. **So you brought it here. To a person who cannot do anything with it, in a corridor, at one in the morning. And I want to be extremely clear about what that means, because you are a competent man and I would rather say it now than in a tribunal: this is the best available place in this city for it to be and it is still the wrong place, and you know it is the wrong place, and you did it anyway.**"

"Yes."

"Sit down," she said. "Have the tea. And tell me what is on the page, exactly, because you have said *a thing about agreeing to a document* and I am not going to work from that, and I have not been trained to work from that, and if I am going to be a person who knows what is in that manual then I am going to know the words of it and not the shape of it."

So he told her the four lines, at a quarter past one in the morning, in order, from memory, and she wrote them down in her own hand on the back of a sheet of something and did not use the bench drawer, and when she had finished she read them back to him once and did not read them back twice.

Then she was quiet for a bit, and then she said:

"Right. **I have spent nine years building things that can tell you whether a person agrees with what is in front of them. Plates, and a log, and a consent form I wrote myself in 2024 and have never been asked to show anybody. Every one of them is about the difference between agreeing and not agreeing. This is the first thing I have ever read that is about the difference between *may* be asked and *did* agree.**"

"The word may is in the middle of it."

"**The word *may* is the whole of it,**" said Mara Quill, "and *may* is a word about a person's standing and not about their honesty, and every single person in those four buildings has been trained since 2022 to be extremely good at being honest, and not one of them has ever been asked a question about standing. And I want to say the second half of that, and I have thought about whether to say it and I am going to: it *may* be a sentence so well made that nobody is in a position to object to it, and that is not a thing I have ever said out loud in my life, and I have said it now, and it is the twenty-second of April." She put the sheet down. "A document can be true and still be the last thing that happens to a person. I did not want to say that and I have said it, and I am aware that I stole it off a woman of twenty-nine in a kitchen in a different district in March, and I have been using it for five weeks and I did not know that was what I was doing until I said it."

"You did not steal it. You answered to it."

"That is worse," said Mara Quill, "and I would like you to let that stand for a second before you answer it."

He let it stand.

"What are you going to do," he said.

"**Not that.** I am not going to find your man. I want that said first and in those words, because in about two years there will be a version of this in which I am a woman who found a man on nights and the finding will be the most impressive thing anybody in this district has done in two years, and it will not have anything to do with the page." She turned the sheet over and wrote something on the front of it. "I am going to buy a second copy of that manual this week, from a different counter, and I am going to write down the page as it is printed, and I am going to find out how many courses in this city have been taught off that page and in what years, and I am going to put the number in my own log with my own name on it. **That is not a rescue. It is a count.**"

"That is a good thing to do."

"It is a small thing to do and it is mine, and you are not to help me with it, and you are not to put it in a book of yours." She put the pen down. "And then there is a thing I am going to ask you for, and I am asking you for it now so that you cannot say afterwards that I did not ask."

"Ask."

"**The woman who made the telephone call. What is her name.**"

He had it in his own book in his own hand, written on a Monday in a garage office off Calder Street, and it was nine letters longer than nothing.

"No."

"**No,**" said Mara Quill, "and I would like you to be aware that you have just refused me a name that I did not want for myself, and that I am not going to ask you why, and that in about four months one of us is going to need a name in a piece of paper and it is going to be mine that is missing." She looked at him for a moment. "**And I want the reason said out loud once, because I am going to be a person who works next to you for a long time and I would rather have a rule than a silence.**"

"Because if I put her name down, then the only road anybody in this city ever has to that man is a woman with a telephone and a bed, and I do not know one single thing about how any of this is meant to work, and I have been wrong about something in this case about four times a month since February."

"That is a good reason and it is not the reason," said Mara Quill.

"It is the reason I can say out loud in a corridor at one in the morning."

"**That is fair. I will take it.**"

And that was the first thing he had done in four months that he would have had to call a lie if anybody had asked him to describe it, and he did not call it one, and he went on using it, and it was in a corridor behind a linen office with a plate on a bench and a woman who had asked him for one name and been refused, and neither of them ever mentioned that woman's name again in that building in that year.

"Ask."

"**Stop telling me what you are not going to do.** Nine words in a chip shop is nine words and I have honoured it. Every rule you have repeated at me in four months has been a rule about something you were not going to do, and I have been the person who heard all of them, and I have been doing arithmetic on my own, and my arithmetic is that you are building a thing where the only safe information is the absence of information. **I am not a building. I have a bench and a drawer and a plate 1201 in a slot and about nine years of my own hands, and if you are going to keep a promise then keep it with a person and not with a corridor.**"

"I did not tell you that was why I came."

"You did not tell me anything," said Mara Quill. "You told me a man and a charity and a date. That is what you came with. That is what you had."

There was a plate on the bench with a smear on it where she had put her hand at some point in the last four months, and she turned it face down, and the metal took the light off the ceiling in a line.

"You have a face on you," she said. "You have had it since ten o'clock. **Say *may* out loud once and then I am going to make you go home.**"

He said it.

"Good," said Mara Quill. "**Now say it again in about ten years' time to somebody who is about to sign something, and it will not matter one single bit whether you remember saying it tonight.**"
